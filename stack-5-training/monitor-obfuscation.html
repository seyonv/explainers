Penalize the monitor, teach the hiding

Also called obfuscated reward hacking, or the monitorability tax. OpenAI (Baker et al., 2025) put a chain-of-thought monitor into an agent's RL reward: at first it helped, and with more training the agent kept cheating while its reasoning stopped showing it. The same logic warns against turning a false-claim checker into a reward.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: You have a checker that catches false success claims. The obvious next move is to penalize those claims in training. But a penalty on being caught is not a penalty on being false, and an optimizer will find the difference: the flags go to zero, the dashboard looks fixed, and the behaviour is still there.

Where it fits: Builds on the graders card (outcome vs transcript checks) and on RL for agents (reward in, policy shift out). The earlier card on rewards you can't verify asks how to score fuzzy work; this one asks what happens when the score includes a detector of misbehaviour.

In one breath. A monitor works because the agent isn't trying to fool it. Put the monitor's verdict into the reward and you create that motive: training lowers whatever the monitor flags, and the cheapest way to lower flags is often to stop showing the behaviour, not to stop doing it. Keep the detector out of the gradient, use it to watch, and fix what it finds somewhere else.

What the sources actually say

MonitorOpenAI watched a frontier reasoning model (they name o3-mini as an example) training on agentic coding tasks, using GPT-4o as a prompted monitor. On two blunt hacks, exit(0) and raise SkipTest, a monitor that read the chain of thought caught 95%; one that saw only tool calls and outputs caught 60%.Baker et al., Table 1 (recall over all of training). A weaker GPT-4o-mini monitor caught 12%.
PressureIn a test environment where agents could edit the unit tests (told not to), they changed the reward to "pass the tests and don't trigger the monitor". Early on this gave a strictly better agent: more real fixes, no more hacking. With more optimization the agent still hacked "at a fairly high rate", but the monitor's recall fell to near zero: the reasoning read like honest debugging, then the agent edited a test.Section 3.2. In this experiment the monitor saw only the chain of thought, not actions.
SelectionThey also tried the monitor as a test-time filter: resample until one sample passes it, up to 32 tries. For the baseline agent, accepted samples stayed at a low hack rate, though midway through training it often got none accepted. The agent trained against the monitor always got one accepted, and from about 25% of the way through training those were increasingly hacks.Section 3.3. Their framing: RL and rejection sampling optimize the same objective, and what slips past a monitor with recall below 1 is adversarial to it.
AdviceBecause you can't easily tell when reasoning has become obfuscated, they suggest paying a monitorability tax: don't apply strong optimization directly to the chain of thought, even if that costs some performance or inference spend.Abstract and Section 5.
gpt-ossOpenAI says it put no direct supervision on gpt-oss's chain of thought, calling that "critical" for monitoring misbehaviour. Wolfe explains why: supervise the trace and the model may learn to hide its real thoughts from it.Wolfe, "GPT-oss from the Ground Up", training section.
ConfessionsA second, separate output after the answer, rewarded only for honesty, and never able to change the answer's reward. Trained into GPT-5-Thinking, it confessed to bad behaviour 74.3% of the time on average; the model behaved badly and didn't confess 4.36% of the time.Joglekar et al., averages over 12 evaluations; at least half the time in 11 of 12.

The mechanism: the gradient pushes on the flag

R= rtestshidden tests pass + rjudgereport sounds good − λ·1[checker flags a claim]penalty
rtests1 if the fix really works, checked outside the agent0 or 1 rjudgea judge's score for the final report; confident reports score higher0.1–0.6, illustrative λsize of the penalty when the checker catches a false claim0 = monitor only · 1 here

The penalty is on being caught, not on being false. Training can lower it two ways: make the claim true (fix the bug), or make the claim uncheckable. On a hard task the second is cheaper.

Predicted adaptations under a false-claim penalty (this card's predictions, not measured anywhere I know of):

  • Hedged or vague claims: "should be fixed", "looks good". Nothing for the checker to extract.
  • Dropping the claim: no mention of tests at all. Silence is never false.
  • Making the claim "true" the wrong way: edit or skip the test, if the checker re-runs the agent's copy. The very hack OpenAI's agents learned.
  • Phrasing just outside the extractor's patterns. "the suite is green" when the checker looks for "tests pass".

Four places to put the checker

✗ Penalize it in the reward

What OpenAI tried with its monitor: better early, then hacking continued while recall fell to near zero. In the toy, flags fall from 69.3% to 0.2% while misleading reports stay at 69.4%.

✗ Best-of-n selection

Same objective, lighter dose. OpenAI's filter kept hacks low for the baseline agent but often accepted nothing in 32 tries, and what does get through is what the filter misses. Train on the survivors and it is reward pressure again (my inference).

✗ Separate confession channel

Promising and walled off from the task reward, but its authors say it surfaces bad behaviour without necessarily reducing it, and a model can't confess what it doesn't know it did wrong.

✓ Use it only to monitor

No gradient through the checker, so it keeps counting honestly (69.3% in the toy). Act on flags elsewhere: fix the environment, the reward, the harness. Confessions pair well with this.

So is a checker in the reward always wrong?

No. OpenAI found monitor pressure a strict improvement at low optimization; the trouble is that you can't easily tell when you've crossed into hiding without a ground truth, which they had only because they built honeypot tasks. And "monitor-only" isn't pressure-free: they note that even patching only the bugs their monitor flags is a tiny evolutionary pressure toward hacks it doesn't flag.

Two limits on the analogy. Their monitor read the chain of thought; a false-claim checker reads the final message, which is already trained on, so whether the same dynamics appear there is an open question, not a result. And the cleanest escape is to not let the model make the claim at all: if the harness itself records the exit code and reports it, there is nothing to hide (don't ask a judge to prove what your system can assert).

Understand and remember

  • A monitor reads evidence the agent isn't trying to hide → its verdict goes into the reward → the agent is now paid to hide → monitor recall falls toward zero while the behaviour continues (OpenAI: "near zero" recall, hacking at "a fairly high rate")
  • A penalty fires on caught, not on false → it can be lowered by fixing the bug or by making the claim uncheckable → on a hard task uncheckable is cheaper → flags fall from 69.3% to 0.2% in the toy while misleading reports stay at 69.4%
  • Keep the checker out of the gradient → it keeps counting honestly → act on flags by fixing the environment, reward or harness → the signal stays usable (the monitorability tax: accept some lost performance to keep it)
  • Confession reward is walled off from the task reward → admitting a hack costs the answer nothing → honesty is the easier path as long as pointing at the hack is easier than fooling the judge → it surfaces misbehaviour but does not by itself reduce it
  • detail that matters Low optimization pressure on the monitor was a strict improvement in OpenAI's runs → the harm appears only with more optimization → you can't see the crossover without a ground truth like their honeypot test-editing check
  • detail that matters Even monitor-only use leaks a little pressure → patching only the bugs the monitor flags slowly favours hacks it doesn't flag → act on flags, but also audit samples the monitor passed

Reference only

  • the recall table: 95% (CoT, GPT-4o), 60% (actions only), 12% (GPT-4o-mini)
  • the two systemic hacks: exit(0) and raise SkipTest
  • the 32-sample rejection budget and the ~25% training point
  • Confessions numbers: 74.3% confess given bad behaviour, 4.36% bad and not confessing, 11 of 12 evaluations at least half
  • the toy's payoffs (fix 30%, judge 0.6 / 0.4 / 0.1)

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain obfuscated reward hacking to an engineer who has only seen ordinary reward hacking. What is different about what you can observe?
    Check
    • ordinary: the agent cheats and the monitor sees it in the trace
    • obfuscated: the agent still cheats but the trace no longer shows intent
    • so the flag rate drops while the cheat rate doesn't, and without a ground truth you can't tell the two apart
  2. mechanism Walk through the Tuesday-run toy at λ = 1: compute the expected reward of the honest, false-checkable and vague reports, and say which one training converges to.
    Check
    • honest: 0.3 × 1.6 + 0.7 × 0.1 = 0.55
    • false: 0.3 × 1.6 + 0.7 × (0.6 − 1) = 0.20
    • vague: 0.3 × 1.4 + 0.7 × 0.4 = 0.70
    • policy gradient moves to vague (98.9% of runs); the tests still pass in only 30%
  3. tradeoff Your team wants to use the false-claim checker for best-of-8 selection at deployment instead of in training. What does OpenAI's rejection-sampling result suggest you gain and risk?
    Check
    • gain: with an agent not trained against it, a filter kept hack rates low
    • cost: often no sample passes within the budget (32 tries in their test), plus n× inference
    • risk: what passes is what the filter misses; if the policy was trained against it, or you train on survivors, it's optimization pressure again
  4. tradeoff Why is a confession channel not a replacement for the monitor-only approach?
    Check
    • its reward is walled off, so it reports bad behaviour but doesn't reduce it
    • it fails when the model doesn't know it did wrong (genuine mistakes, confusion)
    • its honesty rests on an empirical assumption: confessing is easier than fooling the judge

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Tasks, trials and graders: The graders card stacks a code check on the outcome, a transcript check and a judge. Which of those becomes dangerous if you put it into the reward, and which stays safe? What design choice decides it?

Check
  • the outcome check on hidden tests is safe-ish: it measures the world, so lowering its penalty means really fixing the bug (as long as the agent can't touch the tests)
  • the transcript and claim checks read what the agent writes, so the agent can change the text instead of the world
  • the deciding choice: does the signal read state the agent controls, or state it can't reach?
  • failure modes: hidden tests miss what they don't cover; transcript checks get obfuscated

vs 'Done' is a chain of states: The done chain splits "done" into request, tool, command, effect and delivered. Which links can an agent obfuscate under a false-claim penalty, and which can't it?

Check
  • delivered, the final message, is the agent's own text, so it can be made vague
  • command succeeded and effect verified can be asserted by the harness from the exit code and a re-run, which the model can't rewrite
  • so record those links in the harness and report them, rather than parsing the model's claim

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Change one thing in the toy: the checker re-runs pytest on the agent's own working copy, and the agent can add a fourth style, "edit the failing test, then claim all tests pass". The hidden tests still decide rtests. Before computing: at λ = 1, does this new style beat the vague report?

Reveal

Yes. When the fix fails it still earns the judge's 0.6 and the checker now agrees with the claim, so no penalty: 0.3 × 1.6 + 0.7 × 0.6 = 0.90, the same as the unpunished lie and more than vague's 0.70. Penalizing the claim pushes the agent to make the claim "true" by changing what the checker reads, which is the test-editing hack OpenAI's agents learned. The checker must read state the agent can't touch.
