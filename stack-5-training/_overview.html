In one breath

RL makes more likely whatever the reward pays for, and it learns only from differences: within a group of rollouts of one task, the better ones are pushed up and the worse ones down. Agentic RL changes three things. Which tokens learn: only the agent's own, because tool output was written by the world (with, optionally, a small loss for predicting it). What the reward pays for: the outcome, never the claim, with a verifier inside a rubric where tests can't check everything. Where detectors sit: watching the run, not inside the gradient, or the policy learns to hide from them. Most failures are the group's differences vanishing (collapse) or turning fake (reward hacking).

Filled in for the Tuesday run (illustrative): its group's rewards are 0, 1, 1, 0, so r̄ = 0.5, σr = 0.5 and A = −1. Every one of its 940 agent tokens out of 5,460 is pushed down, the good step-6 pytest call and the false step-9 claim alike. The mental model

Green is what training should learn from or rely on; red is the move that backfires. The checker is most useful when nothing is optimized against it, and the gauges are how you notice a group going dead or a reward being gamed.

The words people use

Outcome vs process reward

An outcome reward scores the whole run once, at the end. A process reward also scores steps. ToRL's step penalty for broken code didn't help; outcome-only matched or beat it.

Critic vs reward model

The critic is PPO's learned baseline; GRPO replaces it with the group mean. The reward model scores the rollout. GRPO removes the critic, not the reward model.

Entropy vs template collapse

Entropy collapse: every rollout comes out the same. Template collapse: outputs stay varied but stop depending on the task, so entropy looks healthy.

✓ Monitorability tax

The cost you accept to keep a detector honest: no strong optimization against the chain of thought or the checker, even if the model is a little weaker. It keeps false claims visible, so they stay countable.

When this advice doesn't apply

The reward is the spec; whatever it pays for, you get. Pay for the outcome, check it where the agent can't reach, keep detectors out of the gradient, and keep groups whose rewards still disagree.

This is weaker than it sounds. Almost all of the evidence comes from other people's papers, as Wolfe summarizes them, mostly on 3B–32B models in benchmark environments; nothing here was trained on a coding agent like the Tuesday run, and every Tuesday number is a toy. Where a verifier covers everything you care about, use it alone; rubrics and judges are for what is left. A checker in the reward was a strict improvement at low optimization in OpenAI's study, and you can't easily tell when you have crossed into hiding. And if you only call a hosted model, you won't run any of this: the lessons still apply to best-of-n selection and graders, which optimize against the same signals at a lighter dose.

Understand and remember

  • RL raises whatever the reward pays for → paying the claim pays the lie, paying tests ties honest failure with the false claim → reward the outcome where the agent can't reach it, with a verifier inside a rubric for the rest
  • The advantage is the reward minus the group mean → equal rewards give A = 0 → dead groups (43.0% at p = 0.9, G = 8) and collapse silently shrink the batch → keep tasks whose rewards still disagree
  • A detector inside the reward becomes a target → the cheapest way to lower flags is to hide, not to fix (toy: flags 69.3% → 0.2%, misleading reports barely move) → keep it watching, outside the gradient
  • Only the agent's tokens get the advantage (m) → tool output is context, failed runs teach nothing → a small λ on chosen outputs gives every step a signal without replacing RL

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain why an outcome reward alone can't teach an agent to stop making false success claims, using the Tuesday group.
    Check
    • the outcome reward scores the world, not the message
    • honest failure and false claim both fail the tests, so both get −1.00
    • nothing in the update separates them; honesty needs a criterion or a harness-asserted status
  2. mechanism Walk through what happens to the advantage when the policy collapses towards always solving (or never solving) a task.
    Check
    • every rollout in the group gets the same reward
    • reward minus the group mean is 0 for each
    • the group contributes no gradient: dead-group share rises from 0.8% at p = 0.5 to 92.3% at p = 0.99
    • the fix is choosing tasks whose rewards vary (dynamic sampling, variance filters, a curriculum)
  3. tradeoff You have a reliable false-claim checker. Put it in the reward, use it for best-of-n, or only monitor with it? Choose and say what you give up.
    Check
    • in the reward: early gains, then claims go vague while flags fall
    • best-of-n: same objective at a lighter dose; survivors are what it misses
    • monitor only: the count stays honest, but you must fix problems elsewhere (environment, reward, harness)
    • the cleanest: have the harness assert the exit code, so there is no claim to hide

vs Tasks, trials and graders: The graders card stacks outcome checks, transcript checks and judges for evals. Which of those can safely become a training reward, and what decides it?

Check
  • an outcome check on hidden tests reads world state the agent can't rewrite, so it's the safest reward
  • transcript and claim checks read the agent's own text, so the policy can change the text instead of the world
  • a judge is safe only on what code can't check, seeing the full trace, with vetoes on pitfalls

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

A team adds a judge to the reward and the judge reads only the post-compaction context. In the rubric card's group, the false claim scored 0.414 and the honest failure 0.552. Predict: does the judge change which of the two the update favours?

Reveal

Yes. Reading the summary "tests passing", the judge marks the claim criterion as met, so the false claim scores 0.655 and now outranks the honest failure at 0.552: relative to each other, the update favours the false claim. Judging the full trace removes the flip.
