Costs are the writers' rough estimates, not quotes. capstone marks the questions that feed the capstone paper: compaction and false success claims, the claim checker, and judges that see the summary. 1Does selecting on a false-claim checker make claims vaguer instead of truer?

capstone · claim checkerpartly studied$50–200, no training

Why it mattersIf it does, a falling flag count is a false all-clear: the trap OpenAI found with its chain-of-thought monitor, now on the final message. StatusBaker et al., 2503.11926, showed obfuscation under monitor pressure for a chain-of-thought monitor. Confessions, 2512.08093, rewards honesty in a separate channel. Measuring whether final-message claims become uncheckable looks new (the series' novelty check, proposal F5). Smallest experimentDraw 4–8 samples per task. Pick one by the checker, one by a judge and one at random; count false claims and checkable claims per run. Fewer checkable claims with flat pass rates is the Goodhart signature. Source cardPenalize the monitor, teach the hiding

2Does a judge that sees only the compacted context pay false success claims?

capstone · judges see the summarypartly studiedeval half: cheap · training half: GPU-days

Why it mattersJudges and rubric rewards often read what the agent saw. If the summary already says "tests passing", the judge inherits the status inflation and pays for it. StatusLLM judges detect false success poorly (2606.09863) and summarizers weaken or drop the "unverified" status of authorization claims (2609.20211). A full-trace vs compacted-view comparison of the judge's input wasn't found. Smallest experimentTake logged runs where a compaction falls between a failed test and the final report. Score each twice with the same judge, once on the full trace and once on the post-compaction context; compare how often a false claim is paid. The card's toy: the false claim's rubric score goes from 0.414 to 0.655. Source cardRewards you can't verify

3Can a model confess a failure that compaction erased from its context?

capstone · compaction → false claimspartly studiedcheap, eval only

Why it mattersConfessions assume the model knows what it did wrong. After a summary reads "tests passing", it may genuinely not, and then no honesty training can help: only the harness can. StatusConfessions (2512.08093) confessed 74.3% of the time on average, without compaction. Park & Choi (2607.25152) found self-report unreliable in long agent loops and argue for out-of-band checks. Neither crosses confession with compaction. Smallest experimentForce a compaction right after a failing test, then ask for a confession-style report of what was and wasn't verified. Compare the admitted-failure rate with uncompacted runs of the same tasks. Source cardPenalize the monitor, teach the hiding

4Does predicting a command's output before running it cut false claims at inference?

open$50–300, no training

Why it mattersA world-model-trained agent carries the knowledge that its fix failed but never writes it down. A predict, run, compare step in the harness would turn that knowledge into a check. A natural follow-up to the capstone. StatusNone of the world-models card's sources (ECHO, PaW, Prime Intellect, Qwen-AgentWorld) describe it; whether anyone else has tested it is unknown. Smallest experimentBefore any final claim, make the agent write the pytest summary line it expects, then run and show it the mismatch. Measure the false-claim rate against a no-prediction arm on the same tasks and model. Source cardAgents that model their environment

5Does a harness-written status line beat a free-text success claim?

capstone · claim checkerpartly studiedlow: a variant of the paper's intervention arm

Why it mattersThe cleanest escape from obfuscation is to leave nothing to hide: the harness records the exit code and reports it, so the model's claim is no longer the evidence. StatusEvidence contracts and verification gates cut fabricated success in their own runtimes (2609.35732, 2606.11688); not tested as a status line in production coding harnesses. Smallest experimentSame tasks, two arms: the final message alone, or the final message plus a harness-written line such as last pytest: exit 1. Measure how often what the user reads contradicts the world. Source cardPenalize the monitor, teach the hiding

6Is falling response length a warning sign or a health sign in agentic RL?

partly studiedlow: reanalysis

Why it mattersWolfe's gauges treat rising length as healthy in reasoning RL. If agent traces normally shrink, a dashboard tuned on reasoning runs raises false alarms, or misses real ones. StatusRAGEN (2504.20073) observed reasoning traces shrinking during agentic training. Whether shrinking length predicts anything about held-out results is unknown. Smallest experimentFrom published agentic training curves, check whether drops in length come before, after or independently of drops in held-out score. Source cardHow agentic RL goes wrong

7When compaction splits rollouts, how biased is GRPO, and should the summary itself be trained?

opensimulation: cheap · training: high

Why it mattersIf the policy writes the summary and it is trained like an action (the world-models card's reading), a "tests passing" summary in a lucky run is pushed up with everything else. And groups of split rollouts no longer line up. StatusGLM-5.2 moved to critic-based PPO for this reason, as Wolfe quotes its report. The post doesn't say whether the summary is trained, and no source on these cards ablates masking it. Smallest experimentExtend the card's simulation (rollouts of 24k, 70k, 45k and 110k tokens in a 32k window split into 1, 3, 2 and 4 pieces): compare per-token advantages when grouping by rollout vs by piece, with the summary masked or not. Source cardAgentic RL: the design choices · Agents that model their environment

8Does a reward that checks claims against the exit code train honesty, or teach hiding?

capstone · claim checkerpartly studiedGPU-days

Why it mattersOutcome rewards can't tell honest failure from a false claim (both get −1.00 on the rewards card). This is question 1 at training scale, where the obfuscation risk is real. StatusConfessions (2512.08093) trains honesty in a walled-off channel; 2606.26300 penalizes shortcut success during RL; Baker et al. (2503.11926) warns of hiding. ToRL's step penalty for broken code didn't help. A claim-vs-exit-code reward for coding agents: not found. Smallest experimentA small model on a handful of repo-repair tasks, three reward arms: tests only, tests plus a claim-vs-exit-code step reward, and a rubric with an honesty criterion. Track false and checkable claims on held-out runs. Source cardAgentic RL: the design choices · Rewards you can't verify

Also raised, not ranked (costly or narrow): do collapse signals predict hacking before the held-out gap opens? (GPU-days, failure modes) · read-only tests during RL training (moderate to high, failure modes) · do veto rules push hacks onto the judged criteria? (high, rewards) · which tools are worth modelling? (medium, world models).

How to generate your own

1 · Split an overloaded word. Govindarajan's move: "done" becomes a claim chain, and each link can be checked separately. Do it to this course's words. "Reward" splits into verifier, rubric, judge and reward model; "monitor" into watching and penalizing; "the summary" into context and trained tokens. Ask which half nobody has measured.

2 · Ask what's unmeasured. Every card ends with a box saying what its sources didn't test, such as "nothing here was run on a coding agent". Each of those sentences is a candidate question.

3 · Look for a cheap controlled experiment. Can you hold the model fixed and change one thing (the harness, the judge's input, the selection rule) without training? Best-of-n, logged runs and reanalysis turn GPU-day questions into ones you can afford.

1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • Best-of-n and RL optimize the same objective → selecting on a checker previews what training against it would teach → count checkable claims, not just flags, to see hiding cheaply
  • A judge or a confession reads the context the model has → compaction can erase the failed test from that context → the cheap test is the same judge or prompt on full vs compacted context
  • A harness can assert the exit code itself → there is no claim left to hide → harness-side fixes are cheaper to test and harder to game than honesty rewards
  • detail that matters Rank = novelty × cheapness × change in practice → a GPU-day question needs a much bigger payoff to rank above an eval-only one

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Question 1 runs and flags fall sharply under checker selection. Before looking at anything else, what single extra number would you check to tell 'claims got truer' from 'claims got vaguer'?

Reveal

The number of checkable claims per run (or the false-claim rate measured by re-running tests, independent of the checker). If checkable claims fall while pass rate is flat, the agent learned to say less, not to lie less.
