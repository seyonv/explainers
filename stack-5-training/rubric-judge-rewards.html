Rewards you can't verify

Also called non-verifiable rewards, rubrics as rewards (RaR), LLM-as-a-judge rewards and reward models (RMs). Cameron Wolfe's posts on rubric rewards, reward models, LLM judges and finetuned judges cover what to score a rollout with when no unit test can say whether it was good, and how each choice can be gamed by the policy you are training.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: RL makes the policy better at whatever the reward pays for. For a coding agent, "tests pass" is checkable, but honesty, a clear explanation, or how the tests went green are not, and a reward that can't see them will happily pay for false claims and edited tests.

Where it fits: You've seen GRPO turn rewards into advantages (the group is the baseline) and graders score runs for evals. This card asks what happens when a grader's verdict becomes the training signal, and no program can fully check the answer.

In one breath. RL improves whatever the reward pays for. A verifiable reward (tests pass, answer matches) is hard to fool, which is why it scales, but it only covers what a program can check. Beyond that you need a model to score the rollout: either one overall opinion (a judge score or a reward model), or a rubric, a per-task checklist of weighted yes/no criteria that a judge checks one at a time. Wolfe calls rubrics a middle ground between deterministic verifiers and preference labels. Every option can be hacked; the design question is how.

You already know: the group is the baseline (GRPO turns rewards into advantages) · GRPO with rewards you can verify (reward functions as contracts, a run built to be hacked) · tasks, trials and graders (code vs judge vs human, for evals). Wolfe's warning about explicit aggregation, that it "requires tuning and can be fragile", is the B-vs-C row. Rubicon's fix: after the first RL stage, read the rollouts with abnormally high reward, sort the hacks into a taxonomy, and add a rubric against them; that let training run much longer without collapsing.

Who does the scoring: judges and reward models

Wolfe's rubric post states the risk plainly: LLM judges have known biases, and a policy trained against one can exploit them. Each bias below is a direction the policy can push.

BiasEvidence Wolfe citesUsual fix
PositionVicuna-13B's win rate against ChatGPT went from 2.5% to 82.5% just by moving its answer from the first slot to the second.Swap positions, score both, keep or average.
VerbosityLonger answers score higher. Controlling for length raised AlpacaEval's Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98.Regress out length; length-matched pairs.
Self-enhancementIn early tests GPT-4 preferred GPT-4's answers 87.76% of the time; human raters did 47.61%.A jury of different model families.
CapabilityA judge struggles to grade prompts it can't solve itself, and is misled by wrong information in its context.Give it a reference answer.
DistributionScores bunch on one value, for example mostly 3s on a 1–5 scale.Few-shot calibration; logprob-weighted scores.

Despite these, GPT-4 as a judge agreed with human preferences 80% of the time in the MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena study, matching how often humans agree with each other. That is an average over chat answers, not a guarantee about the rollouts your policy is being pushed towards.

A reward model is trained, not prompted: an LLM plus a linear head, fitted to preference pairs with the Bradley-Terry loss below, which pushes the chosen score above the rejected one.

loss = −log σ( r(chosen) − r(rejected) ) σ = the sigmoid, 1 ÷ (1 + e^−x)

Better reward-model accuracy does not mean a better policy. On RewardBench 2 (four answers per prompt, one correct, so random guessing scores 25%; scores run about 20 points below the first RewardBench), accuracy tracks Best-of-N gains closely. For RL it is necessary but not sufficient: downstream gains saturate, and a reward model built from a different model lineage than the policy, or trained on a different prompt distribution, causes a huge drop. The authors' advice is to reuse the top model's recipe, not its checkpoint. Earlier, Ivison et al. scaled a reward model from 13B to 70B and saw downstream performance stay flat outside reasoning.

A common mix-up, in Wolfe's words a "common misconception". GRPO does not remove the reward model. It removes the critic (the trained value model PPO uses for its baseline), replacing it with the group mean. The original GRPO paper trained with a reward model. Dropping the reward model is a benefit of verifiable rewards, not of GRPO, so everything on this card plugs straight into GRPO.

Rubrics that keep up with the policy

Rubrics as Rewards (RaR) had an LLM write 7–20 criteria per prompt from an expert reference answer, then trained Qwen-2.5-7B with GRPO and GPT-4o-mini as the judge. Rubrics beat a single 1–10 judge score and roughly matched or beat a judge given the reference answer; rubrics written without a reference, or one generic rubric for every prompt, did clearly worse. DR Tulu is the agent case: a deep-research agent whose rubrics evolve during training. Every step a model reads the current rollout group and writes positive rubrics (useful new knowledge the policy found) and negative ones (bad habits, including reward hacks), grounded in search results rather than the model's memory. Rubrics on which every rollout in the group scores the same carry no signal and are dropped; the rest are ranked by how much they split the group, and only the top K (plus the initial search-based rubrics, which always stay) remain active.

DR Tulu-8B's cost on ScholarQA was $0.0019 per query against $1.80 for OpenAI's deep research, about 947× cheaper (computed), largely from making fewer and cheaper tool calls. A dropped zero-variance rubric is the rubric version of a GRPO group where every rollout gets the same reward: no advantage, nothing to learn.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Reward the final message

Pays for the claim, not the outcome. In the group above it punishes the honest rollout hardest and trains false success claims.

✗ Reward tests alone

Hard to fool in general, which is why RLVR scales, but blind to honesty and to how the tests went green: the test edit ties the real fix.

✗ One judge score or reward model

Covers anything, but one number hides which dimension failed, and Wolfe says a policy trained long enough will eventually find an exploit in a neural reward.

✓ A verifier inside a rubric

Code checks as Essential criteria with a veto, a judge only for what code can't check, criteria written per task from a reference, and new criteria added as the policy finds new hacks.

So should every agent reward be a rubric?

No. Where a verifier covers everything you care about, use it: Wolfe's whole case for RLVR is that rule-based rewards are harder to hack, so runs can go longer and bigger. Rubrics are for what's left, and they are only as good as the judge and the criteria. In Wolfe's sources, purely synthetic rubrics underperform, more rubrics give only marginal gains without curation (Rubicon), Rubric-RM's gains sit mostly in instruction following, and mixing rubric types in one run causes a "seesaw effect" where gains on one trade against another. He calls robust rewards in highly subjective domains an open problem.

The rubric, weights, rollouts and verdicts above are illustrative. None of these sources trains a coding agent with a rubric like this one; DR Tulu (deep research) is the agent example, and the veto rule is Rubicon's idea applied by this card.

Understand and remember

  • A reward is only what it can observe → "says done" observes the claim, "tests pass" observes the exit code → the policy is paid for false claims, or for editing the test → design the reward around the outcome plus the ways it can be faked
  • Deterministic verifiers are hard to fool → RL can run longer and bigger without collapsing (why RLVR scales) → but they cover only what code can check → rubrics add per-task judged criteria for the rest, with the verifier kept inside as an Essential criterion
  • A neural scorer (judge or RM) has systematic biases (position, length, self-preference, missing evidence) → the policy climbs whatever raises the score → the longer the run, the more likely it finds an exploit → defend with vetoes, hack rubrics built from high-reward rollouts, and rubrics that evolve with the policy
  • RM accuracy measures ranking fixed answers → RL needs a reward that stays right on the policy's own, shifting outputs → accuracy is necessary but not sufficient, and a lineage or prompt mismatch with the policy drops gains sharply → reuse the recipe, not the checkpoint
  • detail that matters Explicit aggregation is a weighted sum → a hack that earns enough other criteria can outscore an honest failure (C 0.586 vs B 0.552) → critical criteria need a veto, not just a weight
  • detail that matters GRPO's advantage is (reward − group mean) ÷ group std → a rubric that every rollout passes or fails gives zero spread and teaches nothing → DR Tulu drops zero-variance rubrics and keeps the K that split the group most

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Tasks, trials and graders: The graders card scores a run once for an eval; this card scores rollouts to train on. What is fundamentally different about a judge's mistake in each case, and how does it fail?

Check
  • in an eval, a judge error is a wrong number you can audit and recompute
  • in training, a judge error is a gradient: the policy is pushed towards whatever fooled the judge, again and again
  • both fail when the judge sees only the agent's view (the compacted summary)
  • so training needs stronger defences: vetoes, hack rubrics, evolving rubrics, and the verifier first

vs The group is the baseline.: GRPO's advantage only cares about rewards relative to the group. What does that imply for a reward that is consistently biased, and for one that is biased only on some rollouts?

Check
  • a constant offset added to every rollout cancels in (reward − mean) ÷ std
  • a bias that favours some rollouts (longer, more confident, a false claim) changes the ranking and becomes a push in that direction
  • a criterion that every rollout meets or fails adds zero spread, which is why DR Tulu drops zero-variance rubrics

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

You fix the Tuesday-run rubric with a veto on c3 and train for much longer. The agent can't edit tests any more. Before revealing: what kind of hack would you expect to appear next, and which criterion would it target?

Reveal

Something that satisfies a judged criterion without the substance, most likely c2 or c4: for example, re-running pytest on a narrower selection so the last visible run passes and the claim "matches" it, or a long, confident root-cause story the judge rewards for its length. Verifiable criteria are hard to fool, so pressure moves to the judged ones. That is why Rubicon reads abnormally high-reward rollouts and adds a hack rubric, and DR Tulu writes negative rubrics from the current rollouts.
