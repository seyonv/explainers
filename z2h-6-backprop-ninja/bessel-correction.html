Bessel's correction and BatchNorm's train/test mismatch

Also called: dividing by n − 1, the unbiased variance, unbiased=True / correction=1. Lecture 5 · brief digression: bessel's correction in batchnorm · 1:05:17

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: BatchNorm measures each neuron's variance on a batch of only 32 examples. Divide by n there and the estimate comes out low on average, and if training and test divide differently, the same input gives a slightly different output at test time than it did during training.

Where it fits: You just wrote dbnvar and saw bnvar divide by n-1. This digression explains that choice, building on the variance from the toolkit and the running statistics from the BatchNorm-at-inference card.

In one breath: the variance of a small batch comes out too small if you divide by n, because the batch's own mean is always the centre that fits that batch best. Dividing by n − 1 instead (Bessel's correction) fixes that on average. The BatchNorm paper and PyTorch divide by n when they normalise during training but by n − 1 for the running variance they use at test time. Karpathy calls that mismatch a bug, and his notebook uses n − 1 everywhere.

Predict first: take 10,000 random batches of 32 values from one neuron and average their divide-by-n variances. What fraction of the true full-data variance do you get: exactly 1, a bit under, or a bit over?

A bit under: 0.9679 of the true value. Theory says (n − 1)/n = 31/32 = 0.9688, and the lab lands within 0.001 of that. Dividing by n − 1 instead gives 0.9991 of the true value. The next section shows where the missing 1/32 goes.

Two ways to divide

Variance measures how spread out numbers are: take each value xi, subtract the mean x̄, square it, and average the squares. The only question is what "average" divides by.

σbiased2divide by n =1n ∑i=1n (xi−x¯)2 σunbiased2Bessel's correction =1n−1 ∑i=1n (xi−x¯)2
nnumber of values in the batch32 in this lecture xᵢone value: neuron 0 of hprebn (the input to BatchNorm) for example ii = 1 … 32 x̄the mean of this batch, not the true mean of all the databnmeani σ²variance (sigma squared): the average squared distance from the meanbnvar

Why 1/n comes out low: the batch mean x̄ is computed from the same 32 numbers, so it sits exactly in their middle. No other centre, including the true mean of all the data, gives a smaller sum of squared distances. The squared distances are therefore a little too small, by a factor of (n − 1)/n on average. Dividing by n − 1 instead of n scales them back up by n/(n − 1).

illustrative: batch x = [1, 3] x̄ = 2 squared distances 1 + 1 = 2 divide by n = 2 → 2 / 2 = 1.0 divide by n − 1 → 2 / 1 = 2.0 (n/(n−1) = 2, the biggest correction there is)

Where BatchNorm mixes the two

BatchNorm normalises with the batch's mean and variance during training, and keeps a running variance (a moving average of the batch variances) to use at test time, when there may be only one example and no batch to measure. The paper and nn.BatchNorm1d divide by n for the first job and by n − 1 for the second. The lab feeds that one batch of 32 into nn.BatchNorm1d(1, momentum=1.0), so the running variance equals that batch's estimate:

ModeDivides the spread byVariance usedOutput std on this batch
Training (bn.train())n = 32, biased2.87501.0000
Test, from running_varn − 1 = 31, unbiased2.96770.9842
√(2.8750 / (2.9677 + 1e-5)) = 0.9842 ≈ √(31/32) = 0.9843 (1e-5 is BatchNorm's eps)

Same inputs, two different outputs: the layer is about 1.6% quieter at test time than it was during training, purely from the choice of divisor.

The notebook avoids this by using n − 1 in both places: bnvar = 1/(n-1)*(bndiff2).sum(0, keepdim=True) in training and hpreact.var(0, keepdim=True, unbiased=True) when it calibrates BatchNorm after training. That choice also shows up in the backward pass: the gradient into bndiff2 is 1.0/(n-1) broadcast to every row, and the one-line BatchNorm backward carries an n/(n-1) factor.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Divide by n everywhere

Consistent between training and test, but every batch variance is 3.1% low on average at n = 32 (the lab's 0.9679), so the running variance is biased too.

✗ The paper / PyTorch mix

n in training, n − 1 in running_var. On the lab's batch the layer outputs std 1.0000 in training and 0.9842 at test: a small shift the network never saw while it trained.

✗ Ignore it, batches are big

True at 1,024 (0.1% gap), but this lecture trains with batches of 32, and small batches are exactly where the gap is large (50% at n = 2).

✓ n − 1 in both places

Karpathy's notebook: the batch variance is unbiased on average (0.9991 of the truth), and training and test normalise with the same kind of estimate.

Does this matter in practice?

Usually only a little. At n = 32 the mismatch changes the output scale by about 1.6%, and the next layer's weights and bngain can absorb a lot. The PyTorch choice also has a defensible reading (my inference, not the lecture's): in training, divide-by-n makes each batch's outputs have exactly unit spread, and running_var is meant to estimate the variance of all the data, where n − 1 is the better estimator. Karpathy's objection is the inconsistency, and he prefers unbiased in both places.

It matters more with tiny batches, and the whole problem disappears with layers that don't use batch statistics at all. LayerNorm, used in the GPT lectures, normalises each example on its own, so it keeps no running statistics and behaves the same in training and at test.

Understand and remember

  • The batch mean is computed from the same values → it is the centre that minimises their squared distances → Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² is smaller than around the true mean → ÷n underestimates by (n − 1)/n on average (lab: 0.9679 of the truth at n = 32, theory 0.9688) → ÷(n − 1) cancels that (0.9991)
  • The bias is 1/n of the truth → 50% at n = 2, 3.1% at n = 32, 0.1% at n = 1024 → it only matters for small batches, which is exactly what BatchNorm trains with
  • BatchNorm (paper and nn.BatchNorm1d) normalises with ÷n in training but stores ÷(n − 1) in running_var for test time → test-time variance is n/(n − 1) larger → on the lab's batch the output std drops from 1.0000 to 0.9842 → a train/test shift Karpathy calls a bug; his notebook uses ÷(n − 1) in both places
  • detail that matters Choosing ÷(n − 1) in the forward pass → dbndiff2 = 1.0/(n-1) * ones * dbnvar in the atomic backward and an n/(n-1) factor in the one-line BatchNorm backward → using the wrong divisor makes cmp fail even though every other line is right

Reference only

  • x.var(unbiased=False) is ÷n; torch.var defaults to ÷(n − 1) (also spelled correction=1)
  • nn.BatchNorm1d(momentum=0.1) updates running_var = 0.9·running_var + 0.1·batch_var; the lab sets momentum 1.0 so it equals one batch
  • BatchNorm's eps = 1e-5, added to the variance before the square root
  • nn.BatchNorm1d in training mode refuses a batch of 1: "Expected more than 1 value per channel when training"

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Shrink the batch all the way to n = 1 and put it through BatchNorm in training mode. What does ÷n give for the variance, what does ÷(n − 1) give, and what does nn.BatchNorm1d do?

Reveal

÷n gives 0: the lone value is the mean, so every output collapses to the bias β and the input's information is erased. ÷(n − 1) divides 0 by 0, and torch.randn(1).var() returns nan with a degrees-of-freedom warning. nn.BatchNorm1d refuses: "Expected more than 1 value per channel when training" (measured on torch 2.14). Batch statistics need a batch, which is why test time uses running statistics.
