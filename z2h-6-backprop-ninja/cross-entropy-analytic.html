Cross-entropy backward in three lines: softmax − one-hot

Also called: the softmax cross-entropy gradient, "p minus y", dlogits in one go; Exercise 2. Lecture 5 · exercise 2: cross entropy loss backward pass · 1:26:31

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Backpropagating the loss atom by atom takes ten lines, two += branches and a division by probs that blows up when a probability is tiny. It also hides what the gradient actually says about the model's mistakes.

Where it fits: Exercise 2 of Lecture 5. You already walked the softmax and the loss one atom at a time; this card does the calculus once, on paper, for the whole softmax-plus-cross-entropy block, and the result opens every manual backward pass from here on (including Exercise 4's training loop).

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Which rewrite of −log py turns a messy quotient into two terms you can differentiate separately?
  2. Why is the target entry p − 1 but every other entry just p, and where does the ÷ n come from?
  3. Why must every row of dlogits add up to zero, and what does that say about shifting all logits in a row?
  4. If the model is confidently wrong (py near 0), how large does the target entry get, and why can it never exceed 1/n?
  5. Why does cmp say exact: False even though the formula is the exact derivative?

In one breath: the loss after the last layer (softmax, then minus the log of the correct character's probability, averaged over the batch) has a gradient with respect to the logits that is almost embarrassingly simple. Take the probabilities, subtract 1 at the correct character, divide by the batch size. Three lines of code replace the ten you wrote atom by atom, and they explain what training actually does: pull every wrong character down, push the right one up, in proportion to how wrong the model was.

Predict first: in row 0 of the batch, the model gives the correct next character h a probability of only 0.0178. What is dlogits[0] * n at h, and what do all 27 entries of that row add up to?

−0.9822, and the row sums to 0 (the lab prints exactly 0.0; across the whole batch the largest row sum is 6.05e-9, float rounding). The target entry is p − 1 = 0.0178 − 1. The other 26 entries are their probabilities, which add up to 1 − 0.0178 = 0.9822, exactly cancelling the target.

The derivation, in four lines

Symbols first. For one example, lj is the logit (raw score) for character j, one of 27. The softmax turns them into probabilities pj = elj / Σk elk, which are positive and sum to 1. y is the index of the correct next character (Yb[i]). 1[j = y] is 1 when j is the correct character and 0 otherwise: a one-hot vector. n = 32 is the batch size.

1Li=−logpy=−logely∑kelkThe loss for one example: minus the log-probability of the right answer.
2Li=−ly+log∑kelklog of a fraction = log top − log bottom, and log ex = x.
3∂Li∂lj=−1[j=y]+elj∑kelk−ly only depends on lj when j = y. The log-sum's derivative is 1/sum × elj (chain rule).
4∂L∂lij=pij−1[j=yi]nThe second term of line 3 is pj. The batch loss L is the mean of n per-example losses, so each gets a factor 1/n.
dlogits= softmax(logits)push every character down by its p − one_hot(Yb)push the right one up by 1 nbatch mean
softmax(logits)probabilities, shape (32, 27), rows sum to 1row 0: 0.0684, 0.0903, … one_hot(Yb)1 at each row's correct character, 0 elsewhererow 0: a 1 at index 8 (h) nexamples in the batch32

In code, the one-hot subtraction is just dlogits[range(n), Yb] -= 1: no one-hot tensor is ever built.

A pulley: the pulls and the push balance exactly

Gradient descent moves each logit by −lr × its gradient. So a positive entry pushes that character's logit down and the negative target entry pushes the correct character up. Row 0 × n, to scale:

26 wrong characters
each pushed down by pj
.aew
the target h
pushed up by 1 − py
1 − 0.0178 = 0.9822

Every segment of the top bar is one wrong character's probability (the widest are a 0.0903, e 0.0808, w 0.0761, . 0.0684). They add up to 0.9822, the length of the green bar. Karpathy describes it as a pulley system (in this chapter, after 1:26:31): the more confident the model is in a wrong character, the harder it is pulled down, and the total pull always equals the push on the right one. If the model put p = 1 on h, both bars would have length 0 and that row would stop training.

What imshow(dlogits) shows

All 32 × 27 = 864 entries of dlogits from the lab, one square each. Green = negative (the targets); grey = positive, stronger for larger. Row 0 is outlined in red.

Each row has exactly one green cell, at Yb[i]: the lab checks that argmin of every row equals Yb (True for all 32). The targets reach −0.0308, while the largest positive entry is only 0.0052. Every entry lies between −1/n and 1/n = ±0.03125, because p − 1[j=y] is always between −1 and 1.

In Karpathy's grey colormap the targets are the black squares.

The collapse: ten lines become three

loss_fast = F.cross_entropy(logits, Yb)
print(loss_fast.item(), 'diff:', (loss_fast - loss).item())
# backward: softmax, minus 1 at each target, divide by n
dlogits = F.softmax(logits, 1)
dlogits[range(n), Yb] -= 1
dlogits /= n
cmp('logits', dlogits, logits)
print((dlogits[0] * n).detach())
print(dlogits[0].sum().item(), dlogits.sum(1).abs().max().item())
plt.imshow(dlogits.detach(), cmap='gray')  # black = targets

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Ten atomic lines

Correct, but step 2 divides by probs, which blows up when the right character's probability is tiny, and two tensors need a second += branch you can forget.

✗ loss.backward() through the chunks

Also correct, but autograd has to keep the intermediate (32, 27) tensors of the chain until the backward pass, and it can't tell you why the gradient looks the way it does.

✗ Nudging each logit

A numerical derivative needs one extra forward pass per logit: 32 × 27 = 864 passes for one batch, each only approximate.

✓ softmax − one-hot, over n

One softmax, one subtraction, one division. Nothing divides by a probability, every entry stays within ±1/n, and the formula itself says what training does.

Why is it only "approximate"?

The three lines are the exact derivative, but they reach the numbers by a different floating-point route than autograd walking eight small ops (float32 keeps about 7 significant digits). Differences near 1e-9 are rounding, which is why cmp checks torch.allclose as well as bit-equality. A maxdiff near 1e-3 would be a real bug.

When this short form does not apply: it assumes a softmax followed by cross-entropy with one correct class per row. With soft targets (for example label smoothing), the same derivation gives p − target instead of p − one-hot. With a sum instead of a mean over the batch, the ÷ n disappears. And if you change only the softmax (say, a sigmoid per character), you have to derive it again. The next card does the same trick for BatchNorm, where the one-liner is much less obvious.

Understand and remember

  • −log py = −ly + log Σk elk → the first term only touches the target logit (−1 there) and the log-sum-exp term differentiates to elj/Σ = pj → dL/dlj = pj − 1[j=y] → the batch mean divides by n
  • probabilities in a row sum to 1 and the one-hot sums to 1 → each row of dlogits sums to 0 → the pull on the 26 wrong characters exactly equals the push on the right one (row 0: 0.9822 each way) → adding a constant to every logit in a row changes nothing, which is also why dlogit_maxes was ≈ 0
  • gradient descent moves each logit by −lr × dlogits → a wrong character is pushed down in proportion to its probability and the target up in proportion to 1 − py → the size of the correction tracks how wrong the model is → a perfect prediction (py = 1) gives an all-zero row and that example stops training
  • the fused formula takes a different floating-point route than autograd's eight small ops → results differ by rounding (maxdiff 9.78e-9 on torch 2.14) → cmp shows exact False, approximate True → judge fused gradients with allclose, and treat a maxdiff near 1e-3 as a real bug
  • detail that matters p − 1[j=y] always lies between −1 and 1 → every entry of dlogits is within ±1/n (±0.03125 here; measured min −0.0308) → no division by a small probability, unlike the atomic dprobs = 1/probs × … step

Reference only

  • dlogits = F.softmax(logits, 1); dlogits[range(n), Yb] -= 1; dlogits /= n
  • F.cross_entropy(logits, Yb) gives the same loss as the chunked forward pass (diff 0.0 on torch 2.14; 2.38e-7 in the notebook)
  • plt.imshow(dlogits.detach(), cmap='gray'): the targets are the dark cells
  • row 0 numbers: Yb[0] = 8 (h), p = 0.0178, target entry × n = −0.9822

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain, without the formula, why a model that gives the right character probability 0.9 gets a much smaller correction on that row than one that gives it 0.0178.
    Check
    • the target entry is (py − 1)/n, so its size is 1 − py: 0.1 vs 0.9822
    • the wrong characters share a total pull of the same size
    • the gradient is proportional to the error, so confident correct rows barely move
  2. mechanism Walk through the three lines on row 0: what does each line do to the entries for a (p = 0.0903) and for the target h (p = 0.0178)?
    Check
    • softmax: a → 0.0903, h → 0.0178
    • subtract 1 at Yb[0] = 8: a stays 0.0903, h becomes −0.9822
    • divide by 32: 0.00282 and −0.03069
    • the row now sums to 0
  3. tradeoff You could keep the ten atomic lines, call loss.backward(), or use the three-line formula. Which do you pick for a training loop without autograd, and what do you give up?
    Check
    • three lines: shortest, no division by probs, entries bounded by ±1/n
    • you give up bit-exactness against autograd (maxdiff ~1e-8) and must re-derive if the loss changes
    • atomic lines are for learning and bisecting bugs; loss.backward() is what you'd use when autograd is allowed
  4. mechanism cmp prints exact: False | approximate: True | maxdiff: 9.78e-09. Is the derivation wrong? What maxdiff would make you suspect a real bug?
    Check
    • no: the fused formula and autograd's eight ops round differently in float32
    • 1e-9 to 1e-8 is rounding
    • a missing ÷ n gives maxdiff 0.956; subtracting 1 in the wrong place gives about 0.03: anything that size is a bug

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Backprop through log, normalise and exp: The ten atomic lines and the three-line formula compute the same dlogits. What is fundamentally different about how they get there, and when does each one fail you?

Check
  • atomic: local derivative per op, needs sums for broadcasts and += for reused tensors; the algebra happens implicitly in the tensors
  • analytic: the algebra is done once on paper, so the terms cancel before any code runs
  • atomic fails numerically when p is tiny (1/probs) and structurally when you forget a branch
  • analytic fails only if you change the loss or the softmax without re-deriving

vs BatchNorm backward in one line, then train with no autograd: Both Exercises 2 and 3 replace a chain of atoms with one derived expression. Why does cross-entropy collapse to something as clean as p − y, while BatchNorm's one-liner still has three terms?

Check
  • in cross-entropy each row's logits only interact through one normalising sum, and the log cancels the exp
  • in BatchNorm every input in a column affects the mean and the variance, so each x has three paths (via x̂, μ, σ²)
  • both are checked against autograd with allclose, not bit-exactness

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Train with F.cross_entropy(logits, Yb, label_smoothing=0.1), which spreads 0.1 of the target's weight evenly over all 27 characters (the correct one gets 0.9 + 0.1/27). For row 0, predict the target entry of dlogits * n, the entry for a (p = 0.0903), and the row sum.

Reveal

The derivation now gives p − target instead of p − one-hot. Target entry: 0.0178 − (0.9 + 0.1/27) = 0.0178 − 0.9037 = −0.8859. For a: 0.0903 − 0.1/27 = 0.0903 − 0.0037 = 0.0866. The row still sums to 0 (autograd gives −1.86e-9), because the smoothed target also sums to 1. Autograd agrees with (softmax − target)/n to a maxdiff of 1.86e-9.
