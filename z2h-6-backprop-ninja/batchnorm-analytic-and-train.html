Three roads from x to the loss

Take one hidden neuron and a batch of n = 32 values x1 … x32 (one column of hprebn). BatchNorm computes a mean μ and a variance σ² over the batch, standardises each xi to x̂i (bnraw), then scales and shifts it to yi = γx̂i + β (hpreact). Changing one xi moves the loss by three roads, and its gradient is the sum of all three.

Predict first: σ² is computed from μ (it's the mean of (xi − μ)²). When you backprop, how much gradient flows along that edge from σ² into μ?

None. The slope is dσ²/dμ = −2/(n−1) · Σ(xj − μ), and the deviations from a batch's own mean always sum to zero. In the lab's batch the 32 values of bndiff in neuron 0 have absolute values adding up to 44.67, yet they sum to exactly 0.0 (at most 6.7e-6 in any of the 64 neurons, float32 rounding). Karpathy points out that this term cancels, which is what keeps the final line short.

The derivation, in the order the gradient flows

Write d<thing> for "the gradient of the loss with respect to that thing", as in the code, and let s = (σ² + ε)−½ (bnvar_inv; ε = 1e-5 keeps it finite). Go backwards: x̂, then σ², then μ, then x. Because μ and σ² feed all 32 x̂'s, their gradients sum over the batch index j.

dx̂ᵢ = γ · dyᵢ dσ² = Σⱼ dx̂ⱼ · (xⱼ − μ) · (−½)(σ² + ε)^(−3/2) dμ = Σⱼ dx̂ⱼ · (−s) + dσ² · dσ²/dμ ← second part is 0 dxᵢ = dx̂ᵢ · s + dσ² · 2(xᵢ − μ)/(n − 1) + dμ · 1/n road ① road ③ road ②

Substitute the first three into the last, use xj − μ = x̂j/s, and pull out the common factor. Watch the indices: the sums run over j, and only x̂i and dyi belong to the row you're computing (Karpathy stresses this renaming).

dxi= γsn [ n⁢dyiroad ① direct − ∑jdyjroad ② via μ − nn−1x^i∑jdyjx^jroad ③ via σ² ]
dydhpreact: the gradient arriving from tanh(32, 64) γ, sbngain, bnvar_inv: one per neuron(1, 64) x̂bnraw, the standardised input(32, 64) Σj.sum(0): over the 32 rows, separately for each of the 64 neurons(64,) n/(n−1)comes from the ÷(n−1) variance (Bessel's correction)32/31

Written in PyTorch, that bracket is one line, and broadcasting does all 64 neurons at once:

dhprebn = bngain*bnvar_inv/n * (n*dhpreact - dhpreact.sum(0)
          - n/(n-1)*bnraw*(dhpreact*bnraw).sum(0))

Exercise 4: the whole backward pass on one screen

Every other line comes from an earlier card: cross-entropy's softmax − one-hot, the matmul rule, tanh's 1 − h², the BatchNorm parameter sums, view, and the embedding scatter-add. Karpathy widens the net to n_hidden = 200 (12,297 parameters) and uses these lines in place of loss.backward().

dlogits = F.softmax(logits, 1)
dlogits[range(n), Yb] -= 1
dlogits /= n
dh = dlogits @ W2.T; dW2 = h.T @ dlogits; db2 = dlogits.sum(0)
dhpreact = (1.0 - h**2) * dh
dbngain = (bnraw * dhpreact).sum(0, keepdim=True)
dbnbias = dhpreact.sum(0, keepdim=True)
dhprebn = bngain*bnvar_inv/n * (n*dhpreact - dhpreact.sum(0)
          - n/(n-1)*bnraw*(dhpreact*bnraw).sum(0))
dembcat = dhprebn @ W1.T; dW1 = embcat.T @ dhprebn
db1 = dhprebn.sum(0); demb = dembcat.view(emb.shape)
dC = torch.zeros_like(C)
for k in range(Xb.shape[0]):
    for j in range(Xb.shape[1]): dC[Xb[k,j]] += demb[k,j]

No gradient is needed for hprebn's intermediate tensors any more, so none are computed. The only loop left is Python's scatter into dC.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Keep the twelve atomic lines

Correct, even bit-exact, but it materialises six intermediate gradient tensors per step. It's the version you check the one-liner against, not the one you train with.

✗ Nudge each input numerically

Nudging each of dhprebn's 32 × 64 = 2,048 entries costs 2,048 extra forward passes, and each result is only approximate. Fine for a spot check, useless inside a training loop.

✗ Differentiate on paper carelessly

Mix up i and j when substituting, or keep a road you meant to drop, and you get a formula that looks plausible and is wrong. Only cmp against autograd catches it.

✓ One derived, checked line

Three terms, one per road, built from two sums you already need for dbngain and dbnbias. Checked to 9.31e-10, it lets the whole net train with autograd switched off.

Should you train like this from now on?

No. From the next lecture on you use loss.backward() again. The point was to know exactly what it computes, so that a saturated tanh or a BatchNorm quirk isn't a mystery. And exact: False here is not a bug: the fused line adds the same numbers in a different order, so it differs from autograd in the last float32 bits.

The line is also tied to this forward pass. With a ÷n variance (what nn.BatchNorm1d uses in training) the n/(n−1) factor becomes 1. In a check on the lab's batch, keeping 32/31 then fails with maxdiff 3.9e-5, and 1 passes with 9.31e-10. At test time, when μ and σ² are fixed running statistics, roads ② and ③ disappear altogether.

Understand and remember

  • μ and σ² are computed from all 32 inputs → each xi reaches the loss directly, via μ and via σ² → its gradient is the sum of three terms → the one-liner's n·dyi, −Σdyj and −n/(n−1)·x̂i·Σdyjx̂j
  • μ is the mean of the same values → Σ(xj − μ) = 0 → dσ²/dμ = 0 → the edge σ² → μ carries no gradient → the derivation collapses to one short broadcasted line
  • BatchNorm's output doesn't change if you shift, or (up to ε) scale, a neuron's whole batch → the gradient can't point along those directions → the one-liner removes the mean of dy and its component along x̂ → each column of dhprebn sums to ≈ 0 (5.6e-9 in the lab)
  • Every layer's gradient now has a short analytic form → the full backward pass is 14 lines → loss.backward() can be deleted and the loop wrapped in torch.no_grad() → the same training (val 2.1086 in the lab) with no autograd graph recorded
  • detail that matters Roads ② and ③ reuse dhpreact.sum(0) and (dhpreact*bnraw).sum(0), the very sums that give dbnbias and dbngain → the short form costs little more than the parameter gradients you need anyway
  • detail that matters The n/(n−1) factor comes from differentiating the ÷(n−1) variance → with a ÷n forward it must be 1 (keeping 32/31 fails cmp with maxdiff 3.9e-5 on the lab's batch)

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain in plain words why the gradient of one BatchNorm input depends on the gradients of all 31 other rows in the batch.
    Check
    • μ and σ² are computed from every row
    • so changing xi moves every x̂j through μ and σ²
    • those effects are weighed by each row's dyj, hence the sums over j
  2. mechanism Walk through the derivation in order: dx̂, then dσ², then dμ, then dx. What does each step multiply by, and where does a sum over j appear?
    Check
    • dx̂i = γ·dyi
    • dσ² sums dx̂j·(xj − μ)·(−½)(σ² + ε)−3/2 over j
    • dμ sums −s·dx̂j over j; the dσ²·dσ²/dμ part is 0
    • dxi adds s·dx̂i, dσ²·2(xi − μ)/(n − 1) and dμ/n
  3. mechanism Walk row 0 of neuron 0 through the one-liner: which three numbers go in the bracket, and what prefactor multiplies them?
    Check
    • n·dy0 = −0.0307067
    • Σdyj = −0.00790285 (as dbnbias)
    • 32/31 · x̂0 · Σdyjx̂j = −0.00417372
    • bracket −0.0186301 × γs/n 0.0216354 = −0.000403071, matching autograd
  4. tradeoff You need BatchNorm's backward for a production model. Would you write the one-liner, keep the atomic version, or rely on autograd? What does each cost you?
    Check
    • autograd: no derivation, always consistent with the forward pass; you give up visibility, not correctness
    • one-liner: short and cheap, but tied to one exact forward (divisor, training vs inference) and only float-close to autograd
    • atomic: easy to check line by line, but many temporaries; best as the reference you test the one-liner against

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Switch the net to evaluation: BatchNorm now uses fixed running statistics (bnmean, bnvar) instead of the batch's own. Which of the one-liner's three terms survive in the gradient with respect to hprebn, and would its columns still sum to zero?

Reveal

Only road ① survives: μ and σ² are constants, so dxi = γ·s·dyi, one row at a time with no sums over the batch. The columns no longer sum to zero in general, because shifting the whole batch now does change every output: the fixed mean doesn't move with it.
