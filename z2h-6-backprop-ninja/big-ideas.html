The six big ideas of becoming a backprop ninja

The one-page version of the lecture: six claims, six sketches to redraw. Lecture 5 · Building makemore Part 4: Becoming a Backprop Ninja · 0:00:00, 1:55:24 long

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: This lecture is two hours of writing gradients line by line. Without a map of the few rules they come from, it is easy to finish with 26 lines that match autograd and still be unable to write the backward pass of a layer you haven't seen, or to say why training stalls.

Where it fits: This card spans the whole course. It compresses the ten backprop-ninja cards into six claims, and it leans on micrograd's chain rule and += for reused nodes, now applied to whole tensors, and on the MLP with BatchNorm from the last two lectures.

Big idea 1: Autograd is a leaky abstraction, so you have to know what each op sends back

loss.backward() differentiates exactly the function you wrote. If one op on the path has a slope of 0, everything behind it gets 0, and nothing crashes to tell you.

backprop is a leaky abstraction: if you don't understand it, a bug like clipping the loss instead of the gradients goes unnoticed · Karpathy, paraphrased, 0:02:18

Why it matters: Every later lecture calls loss.backward() again. Knowing each op's local gradient is what lets you explain saturated tanh or dead neurons when training quietly stalls.

Big idea 2: Chunk the forward pass, then check every gradient against autograd

Split the net into about twenty one-op tensors, keep autograd's gradient for each as an answer key, and compare every line you write with cmp. A mistake is pinned to one line.

“initializating with e.g. all zeros could mask an incorrect implementation of the backward pass” · Karpathy, 0:07:26

Why it matters: It is how you trust any backward pass you write yourself. The same idea, your gradient against autograd's, checked micrograd against PyTorch in Lecture 1; here it has to judge whole tensors, so it needs both exact and allclose.

Big idea 3: Sum and broadcast are each other's backward

A value copied into many places (a broadcast) collects the sum of their gradients. A sum hands its gradient back, unchanged, to every input. Either way each d<name> ends up with its tensor's shape.

a sum in the forward pass turns into a broadcast (replication) in the backward pass, and a broadcast in the forward pass turns into a sum · Karpathy, paraphrased, 1:09:45

Why it matters: It is micrograd's += rule at tensor scale. Any bias, and any (1, 64) BatchNorm parameter, is broadcast over the batch, so its gradient is always a sum over rows.

Big idea 4: Matmul backward is a matmul, and the shapes pick which one

Every gradient has the shape of its tensor. With 32, 64 and 27 all different, there is only one way to multiply the available tensors into that shape, so you never need to memorise the formulas.

you don't need to remember these formulas: the dimensions only work out one way · Karpathy, paraphrased, 0:49:49

Why it matters: Every linear layer in the series, up to GPT's, is a matmul, and its backward is two more matmuls of the same size: about twice the forward cost, with no giant Jacobian ever built.

Big idea 5: Cross-entropy's gradient is softmax minus one-hot: a pulley

Do the calculus once on paper and ten atomic lines collapse to three. Each wrong character is pulled down by its probability, and the right one is pushed up by the same total.

think of the gradients as forces in a pulley system: the wrong characters are pulled down, the correct one pulled up, and they balance · Karpathy, paraphrased, 1:26:31

Why it matters: The bigram, the MLP and GPT all end in softmax plus cross-entropy, so this one gradient trains every model in the series. It is also why F.cross_entropy beats the chunked version: nothing divides by a tiny probability.

Big idea 6: BatchNorm folds into one line, and then loss.backward() can go

Each BatchNorm input reaches the loss by three roads (directly, through the mean, through the variance), so its gradient is one three-term line. With every layer that short, the whole backward pass fits on a screen and training needs no autograd.

with a backward pass written by hand for every layer, you can comment out loss.backward() and the network trains just the same · Karpathy, paraphrased, 1:50:02

Why it matters: From the next lecture on, loss.backward() is back; the point was to know exactly what it computes. The line's n/(n−1) is Bessel's correction, and the three roads are why one example's gradient in BatchNorm depends on the whole batch.

1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • The backward pass multiplies local gradients along the path → one op with slope 0 (a clamp outside ±1, a saturated tanh, a dead ReLU) zeroes everything behind it → autograd is exact about the function you wrote but can't tell you it's the wrong function → you need to know each op's local gradient to debug training
  • Each d<name> has the shape of <name> → a forward broadcast (one value used many times) becomes a backward sum over the stretched axis, and a forward sum becomes a backward copy → a tensor used on two paths adds both with += → forget either and cmp prints False (e.g. dcounts off by 0.00613)
  • Matmul backward is two matmuls with a transpose (dh = dlogits @ W2.T, dW2 = h.T @ dlogits) and the bias gradient is a sum over the batch → when all dimensions differ only one arrangement fits the required shape → shapes choose the formula; with equal dimensions they can't, and cmp must
  • Deriving a whole block on paper lets terms cancel (the log cancels the exp; dσ²/dμ = 0 for the batch's own mean) → cross-entropy becomes (softmax − one-hot)/n and BatchNorm one three-term line → the whole backward pass fits in 14 lines → loss.backward() can be deleted and training still reaches val 2.1086
  • detail that matters Fused formulas add the same numbers in a different order from autograd's small ops → float32 results differ by about 1e-9 to 1e-8 → cmp shows exact: False, approximate: True for correct code → judge with allclose, and treat a maxdiff near 1e-3 as a real bug
  • detail that matters Every row of dlogits and every column of dhprebn sums to ≈ 0 → the loss can't change when you shift all logits in a row, or all 32 inputs of a BatchNorm neuron → the gradient has no component along directions the output ignores

Reference only

  • the Exercise 1 variable names (logprobs, counts_sum_inv, bnvar_inv, bndiff2, embcat, …) and their order
  • cmp's print format and torch.allclose's defaults (rtol 1e-5, atol 1e-8)
  • the exact one-liner: dhprebn = bngain*bnvar_inv/n * (n*dhpreact - dhpreact.sum(0) - n/(n-1)*bnraw*(dhpreact*bnraw).sum(0))
  • index_add_ as the vectorised dC scatter-add
  • the running numbers: 4,137 parameters (12,297 with n_hidden = 200), loss 3.3377 in the video vs 3.3444 in the lab

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain, without code, why Karpathy says backprop is a leaky abstraction, using the clip-the-loss bug as your example.
    Check
    • autograd returns the true derivative of whatever loss you wrote
    • clamping δ inside the loss makes the loss flat for |δ| > 1, so those examples get gradient exactly 0
    • nothing errors and the loss still goes down, so only knowing the local gradients reveals that the worst examples are ignored
    • the intended fix caps the gradient (Huber, or clip after backward), not the loss
  2. mechanism Walk the gradient from dlogits back to dhpreact: name each line, the shape it produces, and which of the six ideas it uses.
    Check
    • dh = dlogits @ W2.T → (32, 64), matmul by shapes
    • dW2 = h.T @ dlogits → (64, 27); db2 = dlogits.sum(0) → (27,), a sum because b2 was broadcast
    • dhpreact = (1 − h²) · dh → (32, 64), tanh's local gradient using the stored output
  3. tradeoff You add one new op to the forward pass. Compare writing its backward atom by atom, deriving a fused formula on paper, and relying on autograd. When would you pick each, and how does each one fail?
    Check
    • atomic: each line a known local rule, cmp pins a bug to one line, but many temporaries and slow
    • fused: short and fast, but tied to one exact forward pass, only float-close to autograd, and a derivation slip gives one opaque wrong tensor
    • autograd: always consistent, no derivation, but hides where a gradient dies
    • whatever you write, check it against autograd once with cmp
  4. mechanism Your hand-written dcounts prints exact: False, approximate: False, maxdiff 0.00613, while every earlier line was exact. What is the most likely cause, and how do you confirm it?
    Check
    • counts feeds two paths (into probs and into counts_sum), so its gradient needs both
    • a plain = keeps only the first path
    • the missing path adds 1/(n·counts_sum) to every entry of a row; the worst row (counts_sum 5.0985) gives 1/(32 × 5.0985) = 0.00613
    • add the second branch with += and cmp turns True

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Backprop by hand with the chain rule: In micrograd you backpropagated by hand through a handful of scalars. What is fundamentally new when every node is a tensor, which design choice causes it, and what new ways of failing appear?

Check
  • the chain rule is the same: gradient from above × local gradient, summed over uses
  • a tensor op hides many scalar uses at once (broadcasts, matmuls), so the sums become .sum over an axis or a transpose inside a matmul
  • new failures: wrong axis or missing keepdim, a gradient with the wrong shape that still broadcasts, a transpose on the wrong side
  • the fix is the shape rule plus cmp against autograd

vs Automatic backward: closures and topological order: micrograd's automatic backward and this lecture's hand-written backward both compute every gradient. What does each choose, and when would each break down?

Check
  • micrograd: one _backward closure per scalar op, run in reverse topological order; general and automatic, but one Python object per number
  • here: one hand-written tensor line per op, or one fused formula per block; fast and transparent, but tied to one architecture
  • micrograd breaks down on speed for real nets; the manual pass breaks as soon as the forward pass changes and nobody re-derives it

vs Logits to loss, and why F.cross_entropy: In the MLP lecture you switched to F.cross_entropy instead of the exp, normalise, log chain. What does this course show about why that is better, in terms of the backward pass?

Check
  • the chain's backward divides by probs, which blows up when a probability is tiny
  • the fused gradient is (softmax − one-hot)/n: bounded by ±1/n and computed in one step
  • the fused version keeps no intermediate (32, 27) tensors of the chain for the backward pass
  • the cost: you can no longer inspect the intermediate gradients, which is what the atomic version is for

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Swap the net's tanh for a ReLU and keep everything else, including BatchNorm. Before touching the code: how many of the 14 backward lines change, what does the new line look like, and which earlier idea tells you how some hidden units could stop learning?

Reveal

One line. Every backward line depends only on its own op's local gradient, so only the tanh line changes, to dhpreact = (hpreact > 0).float() * dh (the slope is 1 where the input was positive and 0 elsewhere). The first big idea tells you the risk: a unit whose preactivation is negative for the whole batch has slope 0 everywhere, so its column of dhpreact is all zeros, and nothing flows back into its BatchNorm parameters or its column of W1 on that step. That is the dead-neuron leak.
