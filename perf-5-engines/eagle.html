EAGLE, MTP and n-gram drafts

Also called feature-level drafting, EAGLE-2/3, multi-token prediction (MTP), or prompt lookup decoding (PLD). This card walks the EAGLE paper (Li et al., ICML 2024, arXiv 2401.15077) section by section, then gives one short section each to EAGLE-2, EAGLE-3, DeepSeek-V3's MTP modules and n-gram drafts. EAGLE's numbers are for Vicuna, LLaMA2-Chat and Mixtral at batch 1, mostly on MT-bench, and every speedup is against vanilla autoregressive decoding on the same GPU (a Hugging Face baseline where the paper names one). The paper names an RTX 3090 and 4×A100-40G for its batch and gpt-fast runs.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Separate drafts are too expensive and Medusa's heads guess blind about what was sampled. A draft needs to be nearly as cheap as a head, as accurate as a real model, and exact under sampling. EAGLE, MTP and n-gram lookup are three ways to get there.

Where it fits: Last speculative card. It builds on Medusa's tree verification and Part I's exact acceptance rule, and ends with why the gain fades at batch 64 in a real engine.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why is predicting the next feature easier for a small draft than predicting the next token?
  2. What ambiguity does the shifted token resolve, and why did it buy more (1.9 → 2.8×) than switching to features did?
  3. How does multi-round speculative sampling try several siblings and still return an exact sample from p?
  4. Why does the reused LM head dominate the draft's cost for Llama-3.1-8B but not for Vicuna-7B?
  5. Why did SGLang's batch-64 run use a 3-token chain and no tree?

In one breath

Don't train a separate small model to guess tokens. Reuse the big model's own work: take its hidden state just before the LM head (the "feature") and train one extra transformer layer to predict the next feature. Feed it the token that was actually sampled too, so it knows which branch the text took. Then turn each predicted feature into a token with the big model's own LM head. The big model checks a small tree of these guesses in one pass using the exact speculative-sampling rule, so the output distribution is unchanged. It accepts 3.2–4.5 tokens per pass, which gives about 3× at batch 1.

§1 Two observations Fig 3, Fig 4, Vicuna-7B, MT-bench, T = 0

The paper asks what the draft should autoregress on. Every draft has the same job: guess the next few tokens cheaply. Classic speculative decoding (Leviathan) predicts tokens from tokens. Medusa predicts tokens several steps ahead from one hidden state. EAGLE makes two observations.

1. Autoregression at the feature level is easier than at the token level. "Feature" means the second-to-top-layer output, the vector the LM head turns into a probability distribution. The paper argues feature sequences are more regular than token sequences. A draft that predicts the next feature and then applies the target's own LM head beat a same-size draft that predicts tokens: 1.5× → 1.9×.

2. Sampling makes the next feature uncertain, so give the draft the sampled token. After "I", the model might sample "always" or "am". Each choice leads to a different next feature. A draft that sees only fI can't know which one to predict. Medusa has the same problem, because its heads predict tokens further ahead from one hidden state. EAGLE's fix is to also input the token sequence shifted one step ahead, which already contains the sampling outcome. Then it predicts falways from (fI, talways) and fam from (fI, tam). That took the speedup from 1.9× to 2.8×.

§3.1 The draft model

EAGLE's draft has three parts, and only the middle one is trained. The embedding layer and LM head are the target model's own and stay frozen. The new part is the Autoregression Head: one fully connected layer that squeezes the concatenated [feature; token embedding] from 2h down to h, followed by one transformer decoder layer that outputs the next feature.

Redrawn from the paper's pipeline figure (Fig 6). Green is what EAGLE adds and trains. Grey is either borrowed from the target, frozen, or input.

Trainable parameters (paper, §4), by target size: 7B: 0.24B · 13B: 0.37B · 33B: 0.56B · 70B: 0.99B · Mixtral 8x7B: 0.28B Check, our computation (Vicuna-7B, assuming the standard LLaMA-7B shape: h = 4096, MLP 11,008): decoder layer = 4h² + 3·h·11008 = 67.1M + 135.3M = 202.4M FC 2h → h = 2h² = 33.6M total = 236M ≈ 0.24B ✓ Same for Llama-3.1-8B (GQA, MLP 14,336): 218.1M + 33.6M = 252M trainable

The draft's single decoder layer can cache its own keys and values like any decoder layer, so each draft step is a cheap one-layer decode (our note; the paper doesn't discuss it). The big cost that isn't trained is the reused LM head: it is h × V, and on the draft's path it runs once per draft pass (see the worked example).

  • Data: a fixed dataset, 68,000 ShareGPT dialogues, not text generated by the target. Regenerating answers with the target helped only a little: 2.78× → 2.88× (τ 3.62 → 3.75) on LLaMA2-Chat 7B. That's 1.04× for a costly extra step, so fixed data is the default.
  • Noise: the draft feeds on its own predicted features, so errors can build up. Training adds noise from U(−0.1, 0.1) to the target's features, which makes the draft robust to slightly-off inputs.
  • Optimizer: AdamW (β = 0.9, 0.95), learning rate 3e-5, gradient clipping 0.5. The target is frozen.
  • Cost: 1–2 days on 4×A100-40G for the 70B draft (the introduction's wording; §4 says "an A100 40G server"). The 7B–33B drafts train in 1–2 days on an RTX 3090 node. EAGLE trains on 2–4B tokens. By comparison, TinyLLaMA, a candidate standalone draft, was trained on 3,000B.

§3.1 and §3.3: drafting a tree, verifying exactly plus App A

Static tree. At each node, the draft keeps its top-k tokens (at T = 0) or samples k of them (at T > 0) as children. The same hand-picked shape is used for every model and experiment (App A.1, Fig 9: "not rigorously optimized but rather based on intuition", with k = 4 at the root). With tree attention the draft grows a whole tree layer in one pass, so a depth-m tree takes m passes. The pipeline figure drafts a 10-token tree in 3 passes, and experiments use 5 draft passes per cycle, tree or chain.

Verification. The target runs the whole tree in one forward pass with a tree attention mask, the same trick as Medusa. Then, at each node, EAGLE runs multi-round speculative sampling, which follows SpecInfer:

Algorithm 1 (App A.2): target distribution p, k sibling drafts t_i ~ p̂_i for i = 1 … k: r ~ U(0, 1) if r < p(t_i) / p̂_i(t_i): return t_i ← accept this child, go deeper p ← norm(max(0, p − p̂_i)) ← drop what t_i covered, next sibling return a sample from p ← all rejected: the bonus token

With one child (k = 1), this is exactly Leviathan's accept-with-min(1, p/q), resample-from-the-residual rule. Trying siblings in turn against the updated residual keeps the result an exact sample from p, so EAGLE is lossless at any temperature. That's why the paper doesn't evaluate output quality at all ("both unnecessary and meaningless"). The proof of the one-token case is on Speculative decoding I. The accepted tokens' features are kept for the next drafting round.

Why do standard drafts do so badly here? The paper's diagnosis is that distillation raises a draft's acceptance rate, "while the bottleneck for speculative sampling performance lies in the high overhead of the draft model". That's the same lesson as our Mac measurement on Speculative decoding II: a 1B draft for a 3B target costs c ≈ 0.66 and can't win.

Mixtral 8x7B: why MoE is harder

Mixtral reached only 1.50× (τ 3.25). The paper gives two reasons: a shorter accepted length, and MoE itself. Plain decoding reads the weights of only two experts per token. Verifying several draft tokens in one pass can route them to different experts, so the pass reads more expert weights than a normal step. A dense model reads all its weights on every step anyway, so extra tokens are nearly free there. The same issue applies to DeepSeek-V3's MTP (below). It routes to 8 of 256 experts, and whatever it gains still has to beat that effect (our note).

EAGLE-2: a dynamic draft tree EMNLP 2024, arXiv 2406.16858

EAGLE-2 keeps EAGLE's draft model and verification unchanged and changes only the tree's shape. Two observations drive it (Vicuna-7B, Alpaca). First, acceptance depends on context, not just on a token's position in the tree, so a fixed shape wastes slots. Second, the draft is well calibrated: tokens it gives confidence below 0.05 are accepted about 4% of the time, and those above 0.95 about 98%. So its confidence can stand in for the acceptance rate at no cost.

value of node i ≈ product of draft confidences along its path (a token survives only if all its ancestors do) Expand: feed the newest layer's top-k nodes by value back into the draft Rerank: send the top-m nodes by value, over the whole tree, to the target illustrative: a depth-2 node at 0.9 × 0.8 = 0.72 outranks a depth-1 node at 0.5

Result: 3.05–4.26×, 20–40% faster than EAGLE-1, still lossless, with τ ≈ 4–5.5. Vicuna-13B on MT-bench at T = 0 went from EAGLE's 3.07× (τ 3.98) to 4.26× (τ 4.83). The paper notes that PLD and Lookahead get speedups close to their τ, because their drafting is almost free.

EAGLE-3: direct token prediction, fused features arXiv 2503.01840

Scaling up the training data barely helped EAGLE. The authors blame the feature-prediction loss: it forces the draft's output to match the target's top-layer feature. EAGLE-3 makes three changes:

  • Direct token prediction. The feature regression loss is dropped. The draft's hidden output a is fed back in as-is and only has to yield good tokens.
  • Multi-layer feature fusion. Low, middle and high-level features from the target are concatenated (3k) and reduced by an FC layer to k, instead of using only the top layer.
  • Training-time test. During training, the draft's own outputs are fed back in for simulated extra steps, with an adjusted attention mask. The draft learns on the same kind of inputs it will see at inference.

With about 8× EAGLE's training data, the speedup now grows with data, a "scaling law" EAGLE didn't show. The paper reports up to 6.5× and about 1.4× over EAGLE-2 at batch 1, with τ up to 7.5 on HumanEval. For Llama-3.1-8B on MT-bench at T = 0 it gets 4.40× (τ 6.13), vs EAGLE-2's 3.16× (τ 4.05). The authors couldn't test 405B or 671B targets.

At large batch, in a real engine. The SGLang team ran Llama-3.1-8B on one H100 (SGLang v0.4.4, MT-bench, chain of 3, no tree). Batch-1 throughput went 158.34 → 244.10 (EAGLE-2) → 373.25 tok/s (EAGLE-3). The batch sweep:

DeepSeek-V3: multi-token prediction arXiv 2412.19437, §2.2 and §5.4.3

DeepSeek-V3 trains its own draft as part of pretraining. Next to the main model sit D sequential MTP modules. Module k takes the previous depth's representation of token i, combines it with the embedding of token i+k, and runs one transformer block. The output head then predicts token i+k+1. Earlier MTP work predicted the extra tokens in parallel with independent heads (like Medusa's, our comparison). DeepSeek instead predicts them one after another, so each depth keeps "the complete causal chain", which the report calls "similar to that of EAGLE".

The embedding and output head are shared with the main model, and are even the same physical parameters on the same pipeline stage. The report's main goal is better training: in its ablation, adding a 1-depth MTP module raised most benchmark scores at the same inference cost, for example HumanEval pass@1 from 44.5 to 53.7 for the large MoE baseline. At inference you can drop the modules, or reuse them as a speculative draft. On that the report says: "the acceptance rate of the second token prediction ranges between 85% and 90% across various generation topics, demonstrating consistent reliability. This high acceptance rate enables DeepSeek-V3 to achieve a significantly improved decoding speed, delivering 1.8 times TPS (Tokens Per Second)."

Our arithmetic (D = 1, so at most 2 tokens per step): expected tokens per step = 1 + acceptance = 1.85 … 1.90 reported speedup = 1.8× implied per-step overhead = 1.85 ÷ 1.8 … 1.90 ÷ 1.8 = 1.03 … 1.06 → the draft is nearly free

The report doesn't give the batch size, hardware or verification rule for the 1.8×. It says only "combined with the framework of speculative decoding". If verification uses the standard rule, the output is exact (our reading). MLA, the attention that makes DeepSeek-V3's KV cache small, is covered on MLA in course 4.

n-gram and prompt-lookup drafts no draft model at all

The cheapest draft is a table lookup. If the last few tokens appeared earlier in the prompt or output, guess that what followed them then will follow them now. Leviathan §3.6 already notes that such n-gram drafts have "negligible cost": even a trivial bigram model gave α ≈ 0.2 and 1.25× on English–German translation (γ = 3). Kiely's book describes how engines do it (§5.2.4). The engine builds an n-gram dictionary from the input during prefill. At each decode step it looks up the newest token and proposes the stored continuation as draft tokens, which the target verifies as usual. Kiely says these drafts can be longer than EAGLE's ("eight or so" vs "can exceed ten tokens") but only get accepted when the output resembles the input, so they are "mostly used for code completion and code revision", where "it easily outperforms EAGLE". His glossary calls n-gram speculation "Extremely effective for code completion" (p. 223). The EAGLE-2 paper measured prompt lookup decoding (PLD) at T = 0. It averaged 1.33–1.70× across Vicuna and LLaMA2-Chat 7B/13B, peaked at 2.53× on CNN/DM summarization (Vicuna-7B), where the answer copies the article, and got as little as 1.12–1.14× on open-domain questions (Natural Questions, 13B models). Verification is the standard rule, so it stays exact.

Worked example: EAGLE on our H100 our recomputation

Take Llama-3.1-8B in BF16 on one H100 SXM at batch 1. A plain step reads 16.06 GB of weights, so it takes at least 4.79 ms, a ceiling of about 209 tok/s. Suppose an EAGLE-style draft accepts τ ≈ 3.5 tokens per target pass, the middle of the paper's 3.2–4.5 (an assumption for this model). What does the draft cost?

Draft pass reads (our computation): decoder layer 218.1M + FC 33.6M + target LM head (128,256 × 4096) 525.3M = 777M params × 2 B = 1.55 GB → ÷ 3.35 TB/s = 0.46 ms (c ≈ 0.10 per pass) Cycle = 1 target pass + 5 draft passes = 4.79 + 5 × 0.46 = 7.11 ms (1.48×) (the tree is a few dozen tokens ≪ 295, our assumption, so verifying it hides under the 4.79 ms weight read) Rough ceiling = 3.5 tokens ÷ 7.11 ms ≈ 492 tok/s = 2.36 × 209 tok/s Sanity check on the paper's Vicuna-7B (V = 32,000, a 4× smaller LM head): our draft cost: 5 × 367M ÷ 6.74B = 0.27 extra per cycle paper's implied: 3.94 ÷ 2.90 = 1.36 → 0.36 extra per cycle

With a 128k vocabulary, the reused LM head is two-thirds of the draft's bytes. Our model ignores KV-cache reads, kernel launches and tree bookkeeping, so real engines land lower. SGLang's plain baseline reaches 158.34 ÷ 209 = 76% of the ceiling. Applying the same 76% to our 492 gives about 373 tok/s, which happens to match SGLang's measured EAGLE-3 (373.25). EAGLE-3 has a higher τ and a different draft, so read that as a coincidence, not a validation.

Why the gain shrinks at batch 64. Speculation turns spare compute into extra tokens. On an H100, a step stays memory-bound until it processes about Bcrit ≈ 295 tokens (course 2). Every sequence in the batch shares that allowance:

verified tokens per sequence before the step turns compute-bound ≈ 295 ÷ B B = 1: 295 B = 8: 37 B = 16: 18 B = 32: 9 B = 64: 4.6 B = 128: 2.3 At B = 64, a chain of 3 drafts (4 verified tokens) barely fits. A tree of dozens of tokens does not.

That's why the SGLang team's batch-64 test used a 3-token chain and no tree, and why the measured gain drops from 1.81× to 1.38× (EAGLE-3) or to about 1.0× (EAGLE-1). The drop starts well before this roofline says it should. Real kernels aren't at the roofline, attention reads the growing KV cache, and draft passes and bookkeeping scale with the batch (our reading; the paper says only that redundant compute "decreases"). The same fade appears in course 2's model on speculative sampling (2.24× at batch 1, 1.26× at batch 128) and is worked through on Speculative decoding II.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Separate small draft model

Exact, but you need a sibling model that agrees with the target and is cheap enough. For Vicuna/LLaMA2 there was none for 7B, and a 7B draft gave only 1.12× on 33B. Draft overhead, not acceptance, was the bottleneck.

✗ Medusa heads

Nearly free per step, but each head guesses k tokens ahead from one hidden state without knowing what was sampled. Accuracy is about 0.6 vs EAGLE's 0.8, and its fast sampling mode isn't exact. EAGLE was 1.5–1.6× faster on MT-bench.

✓ EAGLE feature draft

One trained layer that reuses the target's features, embedding and LM head, plus the sampled token to resolve uncertainty. Exact, 1–2 days to train, and 2.7–3.5× on a 70B. The best of the trained drafts in EAGLE's results. EAGLE-2/3 push it to 3–6.5×.

✗ MTP modules

Free if the model was pretrained with them (DeepSeek-V3: 85–90% acceptance, 1.8×). But you can't get them after the fact without training, and with D = 1 you get at most 2 tokens per step, while EAGLE's τ is 3.2–4.5.

Why predict features instead of tokens?

Because the feature carries more than the token. The token is one of 32k–128k symbols. The feature is the full state (4096 numbers for a 7B or 8B model) the target used to choose it, and the paper argues feature sequences are "more regular". Predicting the next feature and then reusing the target's LM head beat token-level drafting by 1.5× → 1.9× in the paper's ablation. The draft also starts from the target's real features for the prompt and the accepted tokens, so each round starts in sync with the target.

But features alone aren't enough. The bigger jump (1.9× → 2.8×) came from also feeding the sampled token, which resolves "always vs am". And EAGLE-3 later dropped feature prediction. Forcing the draft to reproduce the top-layer feature turned out to limit how much it gained from more data. The lasting idea is to draft from the target's internal states plus the sampled tokens. Matching the exact feature isn't needed.

When it's not the right choice: for code editing or summarization, where the output copies the input, a free n-gram draft can match or beat it (Kiely: "easily outperforms EAGLE" there). For a model pretrained with MTP, use its modules first. At large batch, the spare compute is gone, so use short chains or none. For MoE targets, gains are smaller (Mixtral 1.50×). And you need a draft trained for your exact target model, since a draft is tied to one target's features.

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

On the running example, EAGLE with 5 draft passes gives a rough ceiling of 492 tok/s at τ ≈ 3.5. If you cut to 3 draft passes and τ somehow stayed at 3.5, what ceiling would you get, and why won't τ actually stay at 3.5? Commit first.

Reveal

Cycle = 4.79 + 3 × 0.46 = 6.19 ms, so 3.5 ÷ 6.19 ms ≈ 566 tok/s (our arithmetic). But a depth-3 draft can propose at most 3 tokens along any path, so τ is capped at 4 and will be lower than with 5 passes, since the deeper positions that added accepted tokens are gone. Fewer passes trade τ for cycle time, which is exactly the knob engines turn down at large batch.
