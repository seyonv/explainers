Quantization for serving

Also called weight-only quantization (W4A16: GPTQ, AWQ), W8A8 (SmoothQuant), FP8 and NVFP4. This card asks one question: which format speeds up which phase of serving? It then walks the three papers' core sections (GPTQ §3–4, AWQ §3, SmoothQuant §3–4, as the papers ran them on OPT, Llama-2 and BLOOM on A100, A6000 and RTX 4090) and checks everything against Kiely's rule of thumb. The Llama-3.1-8B / H100 numbers are our recomputation.

In one breath

Serving quantization means choosing which tensors to store, and which to multiply, in fewer bits. Weight-only (W4A16) shrinks the bytes read each step, so it speeds up memory-bound decode at small batch. The math stays in BF16, because the kernel unpacks each weight to BF16 before the multiply, so prefill doesn't get faster. W8A8 / FP8 shrinks weights and activations, so the matmuls run on 2× faster tensor cores, which helps prefill and large-batch decode too. Neither shrinks the KV cache the attention reads each step. That is a separate choice (quantizing the KV cache), and it is the main reason real gains are 30–50%, not 2×.

2. The methods, one paper each

GPTQ · Frantar et al., ICLR 2023 · weight-only, 3–4 bit · §3–4

GPTQ quantizes one linear layer at a time, and each rounding error is paid back by nudging the weights that aren't quantized yet. The goal is to keep the layer's output WX, not W itself. That needs the Hessian of the layer loss, H = 2XXᵀ, built from calibration inputs X. It starts from OBQ, which quantizes one weight at a time in a greedy order (cubic cost per row), and makes three changes:

  • Arbitrary order. Greedy order barely beats a fixed order on big layers, so GPTQ quantizes columns in the same order for every row. H−1 is then updated once per column, not once per weight: O(drow·dcol3) becomes O(max{drow·dcol2, dcol3}).
  • Lazy batch updates. The per-column update is memory-bound, so GPTQ processes B = 128 columns at a time and updates the rest of the matrix once per block. The paper calls this "an order of magnitude speedup" for very large models.
  • Cholesky reformulation. Repeated inverse updates went numerically bad on models over a few billion parameters. GPTQ precomputes the rows it needs with a Cholesky decomposition and adds 1% of the mean diagonal to H ("dampening").

Speed, as the paper measured it: 3-bit OPT-175B at batch 1, 128 generated tokens. A100: 230 → 71 ms per token (3.24×, 5 GPUs → 1). A6000: 589 → 130 ms (4.53×, 8 GPUs → 2). The abstract rounds these to "around 3.25x" and "4.5x". The GPU count changed too, but the paper says communication costs are negligible in its setup, so almost all of the speedup comes from its kernels. The kernel is a "quantized-matrix full-precision-vector product": it dequantizes weights as it needs them, and the paper's limitations section says it "does not provide speedups for the actual multiplications, due to the lack of hardware support for mixed-precision operands (e.g. FP16 x INT4)".

AWQ · Lin et al., MLSys 2024 · weight-only INT4, group 128 · §3

§3.1: a few weights matter most. Keep 1% of weight channels in FP16 and 3-bit quantization almost stops hurting, but only if you pick the channels by activation magnitude. Channels that see large inputs process the important features:

AWQ Tables 1 and 3. On OPT-6.7B, AWQ matches keeping 1% in FP16 without a mixed-precision format.

§3.2: scale instead of keep. Mixed precision is hard to build a fast kernel for. So AWQ multiplies each salient weight channel by s > 1 and divides its input by s, which is exactly equivalent in full precision. The group's scale Δ barely moves, so the salient weights' relative rounding error shrinks by about 1/s. With s = 2 on the salient 1%, perplexity goes 23.54 → 11.92. Push s further and the other channels suffer. So AWQ searches s = sXα per input channel, where sX is the average activation magnitude, with a grid search over α ∈ [0, 1]. No backpropagation or reconstruction, so less overfitting to the calibration set than GPTQ, the paper argues.

Speed: its TinyChat engine gets "more than 3× speedup over the Huggingface FP16 implementation": 2.7–3.9× on an RTX 4090, batch 1, 4-token prompts, 200 generated tokens. Part of that is plain FP16 kernel fusion: 52 → 62 tok/s on Llama-2-7B before any quantization, then 3.1× more from the 4-bit kernels. The paper spells out the cost of dequantizing: one 4-bit weight needs "1 shift, 1 bitwise AND, and 1 FMA scaling" operation, and then gets just 1 useful FMA.

SmoothQuant · Xiao et al., ICML 2023 · W8A8 INT8 · §3–4

§3: why activations are the hard part. Weights are "flat and uniform" and easy to quantize. Activations have outliers "∼100× larger than most of the activation values", and they "persist in fixed channels". With one INT8 scale for the whole tensor, the outlier sets the step, and a normal channel with max mi gets only 28·mi/m levels: "2-3". Scaling each activation channel separately would fix it, but INT8 GEMM kernels can only apply scales along the outer dimensions (tokens T, output channels Co), after the multiply.

§4: move the difficulty into the weights. Divide input channel j by sj and multiply weight row j by sj. That is Y = (X diag(s)−1)·(diag(s) W), exactly the same product. The division folds into the previous layer offline, so it costs nothing at run time. The migration strength α splits the difficulty between the two:

Relative error = ‖q − x‖ / ‖x‖. The U-shape is the paper's point: α too low leaves the activations hard (the paper: below 0.4), α too high makes the weights hard (above 0.6). Our toy's best is also 0.5, but that is our data, not a claim about real models.

Results, as the paper gives them (A100 80GB): on OPT-175B, naive W8A8 collapses to 35.5% average zero-shot accuracy vs 66.9% for FP16, and SmoothQuant-O3 (static per-tensor) keeps 66.8%. Speed: "up to 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction" (FasterTransformer, OPT-13B/30B context stage, one GPU), decode up to 1.42×. MT-NLG 530B serves on one 8-GPU node instead of 16 GPUs.

FP8 and NVFP4 · formats, briefly

FP8 on an H100 is the same W8A8 row as INT8: 1 byte per weight, and 8-bit tensor cores at 1,979 TFLOPS. It keeps an exponent, so an outlier costs less precision than it does in INT8. Kiely: integer formats "are not suitable for quality-sensitive workloads due to their lack of dynamic range" (p.124), and FP8/MXFP8 "are generally the sweet spot for improving performance without sacrificing quality" (p.125). NVFP4 (4.5 bits with its block scales) has 4-bit tensor-core math only on Blackwell. On an H100 it could only be weight-only: the W4A16 row, with no faster prefill. Bit layouts, scales and error sims are on tensor cores and low precision, so this card doesn't repeat them.

3. Why you get 30–50%, not 2×

"In practice, quantization down a single level of precision generally offers 30 to 50 percent better performance for LLMs."Philip Kiely, Inference Engineering, §5.1, p.120. His stated reason: "Working with quantized data does introduce overhead, so it's not linearly twice as fast to go from 16 to 8 bits."

Our model says BF16 → FP8 is 1.97× at B = 1 and 1.11× at B = 238. Kiely's 30–50% sits between those. The reasons the ideal 2× leaks away, each marked with where it comes from:

  • KV reads aren't quantized (our framing, our numbers). At B = 238 the 19.07 ms of KV reads don't change, so FP8 weights and math only speed up 4.79 of 23.86 ms.
  • The batch regime (our framing). Weight-only gains fade after B = 76. Faster math only pays where the step is math-bound (prefill, big batches).
  • Dequantization and scale overhead (sourced). Kiely: storing small-block scales slightly reduces the gain, and applying scales costs compute (p.124). AWQ: 3 extra ops per 4-bit weight, and W4A16 must dequantize "into the primary computation loop", while W8A8 does it in the kernel's epilogue.
  • Parts that stay in high precision (sourced). Attention, softmax and non-GEMM ops (norms, rotary, sampling) run at the old rate. Kiely: "All but the most aggressive quantization schemes run functions like softmax in their original precision" (p.127).

Kiely's sensitivity ranking (p.126), least to most sensitive to quantization:

1 · Weightslinear layers: "least sensitive"
2 · Activations"only somewhat sensitive"
3 · KV cache"moderately sensitive"; errors compound token to token
4 · Attention"highly sensitive … especially equations like softmax"

His "moderate approach" (p.127): FP8, ideally MXFP8, on "select linear layers, activations, and often KV cache values", with attention "rarely quantized". And quantization "is a scale, not a binary decision": FP8 instead of FP4, or "weights-only quantization", lowers the quality risk (p.128).

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ BF16

No quality risk, but 16.06 GB read every step: 205 tok/s at B = 1, only 238 sequences of 2k fit, and prefill runs at the 989 TFLOPS rate.

✗ Weight-only INT4 (GPTQ, AWQ)

Best for batch-1 decode: 3.70× in our model, 3.24–4.53× in GPTQ's runs. But prefill doesn't move (1.00×), the gain fades after B = 76, and it's only 1.04× at B = 238.

✗ W8A8 INT8 (SmoothQuant)

Same bytes and math rate as FP8, so the same 2× prefill. But INT8 needs smoothing and calibration to survive ~100× outliers, and Kiely advises against integer formats for quality-sensitive work.

✓ FP8 W8A8

The best all-rounder on an H100, never slower than BF16 and never far behind: 1.97× at B = 1 (only 4-bit weights do better), 2.00× prefill, and the best at B = 238 (1.11×; 11,227 tok/s at its own full batch of 268). The exponent absorbs outliers: Kiely's "sweet spot".

Why does weight-only 4-bit speed up chat but not long-prompt prefill?

Because prefill is compute-bound. A 1,000-token prompt does 2 × 8.03e9 × 1,000 = 16.1 TFLOP of matmuls, which takes 16.24 ms at 989 TFLOPS. Reading the weights takes 4.79 ms in BF16 and 1.24 ms in 4-bit, and both are hidden under the math. W4A16 unpacks each weight back to BF16 before the tensor cores see it, so the math runs at the BF16 rate either way. Chat decode at batch 1 is the reverse: 0.02 ms of math and 4.79 ms of weight reads, so 4-bit bytes are nearly the whole win.

When weight-only is still the right choice: single-user or edge decode (AWQ's target: laptops and Jetson boards), GPUs without FP8 tensor cores (A100, T4), or a model that only fits in memory at 4 bits (GPTQ put OPT-175B on one A100).

When no weight quantization helps much: big batches of long contexts, where KV reads dominate. The lever there is KV quantization or fewer KV heads. Also quality-critical tasks: check perplexity, a benchmark and your own evals, as Kiely recommends (p.128).
