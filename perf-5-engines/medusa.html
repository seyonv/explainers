Before this: Speculative decoding I: exact sampling with a draft

Medusa: extra heads and tree attention

Also called Medusa heads, multiple decoding heads, tree attention, or typical acceptance. This card walks the Medusa paper (Cai et al., ICML 2024, arXiv 2401.10774 v3) section by section. Its numbers were measured on Vicuna and Zephyr models at batch size 1, mostly with a Hugging Face baseline. The abstract changed between versions: v1 said Medusa-2 gives "2.3–3.6×", v3 says "2.3–2.8×". This card cites v3.

In one breath

Instead of a separate small draft model, bolt a few extra output heads onto the big model's last layer. Head k guesses the token k+1 places ahead. Combine the heads' top guesses into a small tree of candidate continuations, check the whole tree in one forward pass using a special attention mask, and keep the longest prefix that passes. On Vicuna-7B that gives 3.47 tokens per step for a step that costs 1.22× a normal one, so about 2.8× faster.

Why not just use a draft model? §1, §2.1.1

Speculative decoding (Leviathan et al.) needs a second model that is small, fast and close enough to the big one that its guesses get accepted. The paper names three problems. You have to find or pre-train that model: the SpecInfer drafts it cites took 275 A100 GPU-hours. A separately trained draft can drift from the target's distribution. And a second model is awkward to serve, especially in a distributed system. Medusa's heads are one small layer each, sit on the same GPU as the model, and learn from the model's own hidden state, so there's nothing extra to serve or keep aligned.

§2.1.1 Medusa heads

One forward pass of the backbone yields h_t. The original head (grey) and K new heads (green) all read that same vector, so every guess comes from a single pass.

pt(k)= softmax( W2(k)d × V, starts as the LM head ·( SiLU(W1(k)·ht)d × d, starts at zero + htresidual ))

The initialisation is the neat part. With W₁ = 0, SiLU(0) = 0, so the head computes softmax(W₂h), and W₂ is a copy of the LM head. Before any training, every head predicts exactly what the original model predicts for the next token. Training then moves each head from "next token" towards "k+1 tokens ahead". The paper found that five heads are enough at most, and with an optimised tree three or four are sometimes enough. Unused heads can be dropped at no cost.

Head size, our computation: d·d + d·V parameters per head Vicuna-7B, V = 32,000: 16.8M + 131.1M = 148M/head; 5 heads = 0.74B = 11% of 6.74B Llama-3.1-8B, V = 128,256: 16.8M + 525.3M = 542M/head; 5 heads = 2.71B = 34% of 8.03B

With a big vocabulary, W₂ dominates. That matters for the running example below, because at batch 1 every extra byte of weights is read on every step.

§2.1.2 Tree attention

Each head proposes its top-sk tokens, not just one. The candidates are the Cartesian product: with s₁ = 2 and s₂ = 3 there are 2 × 3 = 6 candidate continuations. They share prefixes, so instead of 6 separate sequences they form a tree. The whole tree goes through the model as one short sequence. An attention mask lets each token see only the cached prompt and its own ancestors, and the position indices are set by depth, so siblings get the same position. The batch size doesn't change.

The paper's Figure 2 idea, redrawn with illustrative tokens. The root comes from the original LM head, and the tree adds 2 + 2 × 3 = 8 new tokens. Row 3 ("the" under "is") can see 0, 1 and 3, but not its sibling "a" or anything under "'". The Cartesian product also builds nonsense branches like "' the". Those just get rejected.

new tokens per step= ∑k=1K ∏i=1k si e.g. 2+2·3=8

This grows fast. Five heads each keeping their top 3 would be 3 + 9 + 27 + 81 + 243 = 363 tokens per step, which is why §2.3.3 prunes the tree.

§2.3 Extensions

§2.3.1 Typical acceptance, instead of rejection sampling

Speculative decoding's rejection sampling gives exactly the target's distribution, but the paper notes it gets less efficient as temperature rises, and says reusing it "cannot further enhance the acceleration rate". Medusa's alternative accepts a candidate token if the original model finds it plausible enough:

poriginal(xn+k∣x≤n+k−1) > min( εhard floor, δ·exp(−H(poriginal))lower when the model is unsure )

The first token of every step is chosen greedily and always accepted, so each step makes progress. The rest are checked with the rule, and the step keeps the longest accepted prefix across all candidates. At temperature 0 this is plain greedy decoding. When the distribution is flat (high entropy H), more continuations count as reasonable, so more get accepted.

Illustrative: ε = 0.09, δ = √ε = 0.3 (the paper's ablation ties δ to √ε) confident, H = 0.5 nats: min(0.09, 0.3·e^−0.5 = 0.182) = 0.090 → p = 0.05 rejected unsure, H = 3.0 nats: min(0.09, 0.3·e^−3.0 = 0.015) = 0.015 → p = 0.05 accepted

The catch is that this is not distribution-exact. The output is no longer a sample from the model's distribution, unlike Leviathan's min(1, p/q) rule. The paper argues this is fine because temperature is mostly a "creativity" knob. Its Figure 5 (writing and roleplay, T = 0.7) shows a trade-off: raising ε from 0.01 to 0.25 raises quality and lowers the acceleration rate.

§2.3.2 Self-distillation

This is for when the training data doesn't match what the model actually says, for example after RLHF or when the data is private. Feed seed prompts (ShareGPT, UltraChat) to the model and train on its own replies. The paper collected about 100k samples each for Zephyr-7B and Vicuna-33B. For Medusa-2, training the backbone on these hard labels hurt quality, so the backbone loss becomes KL(poriginal ‖ p), distilling from the original model. To avoid holding two copies in memory, the backbone is fine-tuned with LoRA, and the teacher is simply the same model with the adapter switched off. The paper recommends not quantizing it, or the teacher becomes the quantized model.

§2.3.3 Optimised tree construction

Not every branch is equally likely: candidates built from different heads' top guesses have different accuracies (intuitively, a near head's top-1 guess is right far more often than a far head's third guess). Using a calibration set, measure each head's accuracy ak(i) for its i-th guess. Estimate a node's chance of being accepted as the product along its path, then grow the tree greedily by adding the most likely connected node until you hit a node budget. For Medusa-2 Vicuna-7B, a 64-node sparse tree of depth 4 (so four heads) beat dense Cartesian trees with 256 nodes on acceleration rate. More nodes also cost speed: bigger trees push the step towards being compute-bound, and if the extra accepted tokens don't cover the extra step time, it's a net slowdown.

Worked example: Medusa on our H100 our recomputation

Llama-3.1-8B in BF16 on one H100 SXM decodes at most about 209 tok/s at batch 1, since each step reads 16.06 GB of weights. Suppose Medusa-2 heads matched Vicuna-7B's Table 1 numbers. They were measured on a different model and GPU, so that is an assumption.

Vicuna's 32k vocabulary makes each head about 3.7× smaller than Llama-3's 128k one, so 1.22 may be optimistic here. Implementations can shrink W₂ (for example by sharing the LM head), but the paper's head doesn't.

A serving engine would shrink the tree as the batch grows (our inference). This is the same "speculation fades at large batch" story as on critical batch size and the practice card.

How it compares, and what came next

✗ Separate draft model (Leviathan)

Distribution-exact, but you must find or train a small model that agrees with the big one, then serve and align two models. In the paper's own baseline, off-the-shelf drafts gave only 1.47–1.60×.

✗ Medusa-1 (frozen backbone)

Cheap (5 A100-hours for 7B) and leaves the model's outputs untouched, but it leaves speed on the table: 2.18× vs 2.83× on Vicuna-7B.

✓ Medusa-2 (joint training)

Heads and backbone learn together with a combined loss, 4× head learning rate and warm-up: 2.83× on Vicuna-7B/13B with MT-Bench within ±0.14. No second model to serve.

→ EAGLE (feature-level draft)

A small draft layer autoregresses on the second-to-top features and gets fed the sampled token. It stays exact, and EAGLE's own paper reports 1.5–1.6× over Medusa on MT-Bench. See EAGLE, MTP and n-gram drafts.

Does Medusa change the model's outputs?

With greedy decoding and Medusa-1, no. The backbone is frozen and the first token of every step is its own greedy choice. Each later token is accepted only if it matches what the backbone would have picked, so you get the backbone's greedy output (up to floating-point differences from computing in a tree, our note).

With sampling and typical acceptance, yes, a little. Typical acceptance is not distribution-exact: it accepts "plausible" tokens rather than sampling exactly from p. If you need exactness, the paper says you can use speculative decoding's rejection sampling instead, but you lose the extra speed. Compare with the exact rule on Speculative decoding I.

Medusa-2 changes the model itself. It fine-tunes the backbone, so even greedy output can differ from the original checkpoint. Table 1 shows MT-Bench changes of −0.14 to +0.05 on a 10-point GPT-4-judged scale, which the paper calls comparable quality. It isn't bit-identical.

When not to use it: at large batch sizes, where the tree's extra math is no longer free (above); when you can't fine-tune or ship custom heads; and for a new deployment where EAGLE-style drafts, which are exact and faster in their authors' benchmarks, are available in your engine.
