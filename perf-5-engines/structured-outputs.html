Before this: Anatomy of vLLM and SGLang

Structured outputs inside the engine

Also called grammar-constrained decoding, token masks, or XGrammar (the grammar backend this card follows). This card covers only the engine-level question: where the mask sits in the decode loop and what it costs per step, using the XGrammar paper (Dong et al., MLSys 2025, §3–4). For how masking, grammars and schemas work, and what they do to quality, see the hub's structured-outputs course.

In one breath

To force valid JSON, the engine gives the sampler a yes/no mask over the whole vocabulary at every step, and the sampler sets the "no" tokens' scores to −∞. The mask is built on the CPU from the grammar's current state, and it has to be ready before sampling. That puts it on the critical path of a loop whose GPU step is only about 5 ms. XGrammar makes the mask cheap by working out almost every token's answer in advance, and makes the rest disappear by building the mask while the GPU runs the forward pass.

Where the mask sits in the loop

CPUGPUdashed green: the loop runs once per output token

The mask is applied after the forward pass and before sampling, per request, per step. In vLLM it is applied in the sampling step of the model runner (see Anatomy of vLLM and SGLang, step 6). A bitmask over Llama-3.1's 128,256 tokens is 16 KB (our arithmetic). Hosted APIs run this loop on their side; the where-it-runs card maps backends to engines.

Why it's a performance problem

The naive way to build the mask is to take each of the 128,256 tokens and walk its characters through the grammar's pushdown automaton, starting from the current state (stack included). That is a CPU job of 128k small parser runs, every step, for every request in the batch, and the sampler can't start until it's finished. XGrammar's ablation (Table 3) measured exactly that baseline, "checking the entire vocabulary", on unconstrained JSON with Llama-3.1-8B: 65.776 ms per token. The running example's whole GPU step is 4.8 ms.

The paper saw the same thing: vLLM with Outlines slowed down most at larger batch sizes, because "a larger batch size leads to higher throughput, putting greater pressure on grammar processing on the CPU side".

Run it: labs/token-mask.py

A toy grammar engine, stdlib only, under a second. It builds a byte-level pushdown automaton for {"a": (int | obj)}, a seeded 5,000-token vocabulary and the per-node cache, then generates {"a": {"a": {"a": -42}}} and builds every mask three ways (naive, cache, cache + a one-character context expansion), asserting that they agree. It prints the per-step checks (5,000 vs 0–248 vs 0–9), the dependent share (1.44%), the 791 → 29 expansion, and the running-example overhead table above. Try this: V = 50000 (naive checks grow 10×, the cache's grow only with the dependent tokens) or a vocabulary full of }.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Prompt only: "please output JSON"

Costs nothing per step, but nothing stops a wrong token. In the paper's function-calling test, Llama-3.1 8B's output was syntactically correct 62% of the time without XGrammar, and 100% with it (Table 4).

✗ Naive per-step mask

Correct, but it walks the grammar for every one of 128k tokens every step: 65.776 ms per token in the paper's baseline, 13.7× the running example's whole GPU step, and multiplied by the batch.

✗ Precompiled FSM mask (Outlines-style)

Turns a regex into a finite-state machine with a token table per state, so each step is a lookup (regex and FSM card). But it can't express nesting, since unconstrained JSON "cannot be handled by regex-based methods", and big schemas take seconds to compile.

✓ XGrammar

Full context-free grammars (nesting, recursion) with a per-node cache that leaves about 0.1% of tokens (120 of 128k) to check at runtime, and a mask built while the GPU runs. Result: under 40 µs per token and 6.2 → 6.3 ms TPOT, which is close to zero overhead.

Does constraining the output hurt quality?

It can shift what the model says as well as how it's formatted, and when and why is the subject of the quality-shift card. On speed: XGrammar isn't always the right backend. If every request brings a new schema, compile time dominates rather than per-token time (see grammar compilation time and compile vs per-token), and a backend with no precompute, such as llguidance, may be the better fit.
