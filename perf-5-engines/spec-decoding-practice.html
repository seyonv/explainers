Before this: Speculative decoding I: exact sampling with a draft

Speculative decoding II: drafts, batch size and our Mac

Also called choosing the draft model, the cost ratio c, and speculation at batch 1 vs batch 64. Card 2 of 2 on Leviathan et al. It covers §4 (T5-XXL 11B on one TPU-v4 at batch 1, Tables 2 and 3) and Appendix A.3 (the c values), then Chen et al.'s Chinchilla 70B run, then our own measurement on the reader's M3. It ends with why speculation fades at large batch.

In one breath

Theorem 3.8 has two inputs you control: how often the target accepts the draft's guesses (α) and how much one draft step costs compared with one target step (c). The papers got 2–3.4× because their drafts cost 2–13% of a target step. On a Mac, a 1B draft for a 3B target costs 66.5% of a step, so the same formula predicts about 1× or worse, and that's what we measured. Speculation also needs idle compute, and a large batch doesn't leave any.

Chen et al.: Chinchilla 70B with a 4B draft

DeepMind derived the same algorithm at the same time. Instead of taking an off-the-shelf small model, they trained a draft for speed: 4B parameters, dmodel 6144, and only 8 layers (Chinchilla has 80). Both models run on the same 16 TPU v4s. Few layers means little cross-chip communication per step, so the draft samples at 1.8 ms/token against Chinchilla's 14.1 ms/token. They also note that a Chinchilla-optimal 7B would be a poor draft: it's fastest on 4 TPUs (5 ms/token), and on 16 TPUs it gets slower.

For XSum nucleus, 14.1 / 7.52 = 1.88, not the paper's 1.92, and the paper doesn't say how it averaged. The implied α assumes Thm 3.8 exactly, with no loop overhead. Chen don't report α directly. The benchmark scores match within noise, which is the empirical check that the draft doesn't bias the output. Code again gets the biggest speedup. The authors' explanation is common sub-sequences such as for i in range(len(arr)):. On XSum nucleus, K = 3 was best, and larger K "plateaus or even regresses".

Our measurement: Llama-3.2 3B with a 1B draft on the reader's M3

Setup: an Apple M3 with 24 GB, llama.cpp 0.4.1 on Metal, and the GGUF files Ollama had downloaded. The target is Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct Q4_K_M (1.87 GiB, 3.21B params) and the draft is Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct Q8_0 (1.22 GiB, 1.24B). Three prompts ran at temp 0 for 200 tokens: code (a Fibonacci function with doctests), chat (backup tips for a new employee) and repeat (repeat an 18-fruit list). Measured 2026-09-22/23.

(a) c from llama-bench -p 512 -n 128 -r 3 (token generation): target 3B 28.8 tok/s → 34.7 ms/step draft 1B 43.3 tok/s → 23.1 ms/step c = 28.8 / 43.3 = 0.665 both are memory-bound, so c ≈ bytes read: 1.22 GiB / 1.87 GiB = 0.652 (draft: 39% of the params, but ≈ 8.5 bits/param vs the target's ≈ 5.0; file ÷ params)

(b) Acceptance = accepted ÷ drafted, from llama-speculative, temp 0, 200 tokens. Code: 130/142, 156/180, 172/232. Chat: 118/168, 136/268, 152/400. Repeat: 115/174, 136/260, 146/448. These count tokens, not seconds, so the busy machine doesn't affect them. Acceptance falls as n grows because every extra draft token has to survive one more comparison. The "repeat" prompt scores surprisingly low. Our unverified guess is that the two instruct models frame the list differently.

Bars: predicted speedup from Thm 3.8, with each acceptance rate as α and c = 0.665 (our calculation, labs/spec-mac.py). Green is above break-even and grey is below. Dots: measured with llama-server at n = 4. Only one bar out of nine clears 1.1×.

Caveat: the Mac was busy. Load average was ≈ 33 on 8 cores from other processes while these timings ran. Target-only speed fell from 28.8 tok/s (llama-bench, quieter) to about 16. Look at the raw runs: each run landed either "slow" (≈ 15–16) or "fast" (≈ 36–38), apparently at random. For chat, 3 of the 5 speculative runs happened to land fast and only 1 of the 5 target-only runs did, so its 2.2× median is noise. Treat every timing here as rough and re-run the lab on an idle machine. The acceptance rates and the predicted ≈ 0.9–1.2× are the reliable results. Why did n = 8 and repeat measure closer to 1× than predicted? llama.cpp's accepted ÷ drafted isn't exactly Leviathan's α: llama.cpp can stop drafting early when the draft is unsure, and acceptances aren't i.i.d. So use the formula as a rough model.

Paper rows as the papers give them. The Chen α is our back-calculation. The H100 row is course 2's roofline model, not a measurement. Our α is the highest in the table and our speedup is still the lowest. Hypothetically, with our code α = 0.867 at n = 4 and Leviathan's c = 0.02, Thm 3.8 gives 3.83 / 1.08 = 3.55× (our calculation; a draft that cheap would also have a lower α).

At batch 1, both models are memory-bound. Each step's time is roughly its weight bytes divided by bandwidth, so c ≈ the draft's bytes ÷ the target's bytes. A 1B draft next to a 3B target reads 65% as many bytes, because the draft is stored at 8 bits. No acceptance rate can rescue that: even α = 1 at n = 2 gives only 3 / 2.33 = 1.29×. For speculation to pay, the draft must be one to two orders of magnitude cheaper, the way Leviathan's T5-small (143× smaller) and Chen's shallow 4B draft are. Alternatively, the draft can cost almost nothing, like an n-gram lookup or a head on the target itself (Medusa, EAGLE). To measure how memory-bound the Mac's decode step is, see prefill vs decode on the Mac.

Why speculation fades at large batch

Leviathan §6 names the limit themselves: latency is bought "at the cost of an increased number of arithmetic operations", so the method "is not helpful for configurations where additional computation resources are not available". A decode step at batch 1 leaves the GPU's math units almost idle, and verifying γ + 1 tokens fills them for free. At batch B, the verify pass carries B(γ + 1) tokens. Once that passes the critical batch (≈ 295 tokens on an H100 in BF16), every drafted token costs real FLOPs.

It's reprinted by labs/spec-mac.py. For a published point, EAGLE-3 reports 1.38× throughput at batch 64 in SGLang, against up to 6.5× at batch 1 (EAGLE card). Our inference: in an engine, speculation is a setting for latency-bound, low-concurrency traffic, and you have to re-benchmark it at your real concurrency. The course's H100 lab (benchmarking card) runs it at concurrency 1 and 64 for exactly this reason.

Why it beats the alternatives: choosing a draft

✗ Tiny separate draft

Leviathan's winner: T5-small at c ≈ 0.02 gives 3.4×. But it needs a small model with the same tokenizer, which often doesn't exist, and it costs extra weights and KV memory. Its α is typically 0.5–0.9.

✗ Similar-size draft

Our Mac: 1B for 3B, c = 0.665. The high α (up to 91.5%) can't pay for a draft that costs two-thirds of a step. Predicted best 1.18×, measured about 1×.

✗ N-gram / prompt-lookup draft

c ≈ 0, with no model at all. It's great when the output copies the input (edits, repeated code, RAG quotes), but on free text its α is low: a bigram gets α 0.20, which is 1.25× (EAGLE card).

✓ Trained heads / feature drafts

Medusa heads and EAGLE's one-layer draft reuse the target's own features, so c is small and α is high (EAGLE about 0.8). They're the default in practice (Medusa, EAGLE).

"Speculation didn't help on my laptop. Is the theory wrong?"

No. The formula predicted it. Plug in our c = 0.665 and our measured acceptance and Theorem 3.8 says 0.23–1.18×. We measured about 1× (1.08× code, 0.95× repeat). The theory's promise of 2–3× assumes c < 0.05, and we were 13× above that. Acceptance was fine. The draft was just too expensive.

When speculation isn't the right choice: the only draft you have is close in size to the target. The server is already running near the critical batch, so there's no idle compute to spend. Your traffic is prefill-heavy: speculation doesn't touch TTFT. Or memory is tight, since the draft's weights and KV cache take space that could hold more users. Throughput-first serving usually fills idle compute with more requests (critical batch size) instead. On a Mac, a cheaper draft (a smaller or lower-bit model) or prompt lookup is the thing to try. A second 1B model isn't. We haven't tested those on this Mac.
