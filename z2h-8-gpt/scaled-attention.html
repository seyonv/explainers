Why divide by √head_size

Also called: scaled dot-product attention, the 1/√dk factor, "scaled" self-attention. Lecture 7 · note 6: "scaled" self-attention. why divide by sqrt(head_size) · 1:16:56

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: An attention score is a dot product, so it adds up head_size products. Make the head wider and the scores spread wider, and softmax snaps each row to almost one-hot before training has even started.

Where it fits: The self-attention-head card built softmax(q @ kᵀ) and showed that bigger scores give peakier weights. This card adds the one factor the paper's formula has and ours didn't: dividing the scores by √head_size.

In one breath: a score q·k adds up head_size products, so its spread grows with head_size. Feed wide scores into softmax and each row snaps to almost one-hot. Multiplying the scores by head_size**-0.5 (that is, dividing by √head_size) brings their variance back to about 1 whatever the head size, so attention starts out spread over many tokens.

Predict first: every entry of q and k is drawn with mean 0 and variance 1, and head_size is 16. What is the variance of one score q·k: about 1, about 4, or about 16?

About 16. Replaying Karpathy's cell gives 17.47; with 1,000 samples the lab gets 16.00. The dot product sums 16 independent products, each with variance 1, and variances of independent terms add. Its standard deviation is √16 = 4, so dividing by 4 brings it back to 1 (measured: 1.0918 in the cell, 1.000 with 1,000 samples).

Where the 16 comes from

Variance is the average squared distance from the mean; its square root, the standard deviation, is the typical size of a value. Three facts do all the work: a product of two independent mean-0, variance-1 numbers has variance 1; variances of independent terms add; and multiplying a number by c multiplies its variance by c².

Why a wide spread hurts: softmax turns peaky

Softmax exponentiates, so what matters is the gaps between scores. Multiply every score by 8 and a gap of 0.2 becomes 1.6, which is a factor of e1.6 ≈ 5 in weight. Karpathy's five illustrative scores, then the same scores ×8 and ×50 (green = the largest weight):

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ No scaling

Score variance equals head_size: 16.00 at d = 16, 63.10 at d = 64. Rows start near one-hot (0.80 at ×8) and softmax's gradient shrinks (1.57 → 0.67 → 0.00018).

✗ Divide by head_size

Overshoots: the variance becomes 1/d (0.0625 at d = 16, 0.0154 at d = 64). Scores collapse toward 0 and every row starts as a near-plain average, flatter the bigger the head.

✗ Divide by √C (the Colab's bug)

The final Colab cell scales by C**-0.5, the embedding width, not the head's. At gpt.py sizes that gives variance 64 / 384 = 0.17, not 1. Karpathy flags it at 1:20:05; gpt.py uses k.shape[-1]**-0.5.

✓ Divide by √head_size

One multiply per score, no parameters. Variance stays near 1 at any head size (1.000 at d = 16, 0.986 at d = 64), so softmax starts diffuse and gradients can flow.

Does this make every score unit variance?

No. The rule assumes q and k entries have variance 1, like the torch.randn in Karpathy's cell. It removes the dependence on head_size, not every scale problem. In the crux cell, q and k come from untrained nn.Linear layers and have variance about 0.34 and 0.32 (computed with the same seed), so the raw scores have variance 1.92, and scaling takes that to 1.92 / 16 = 0.12. That's why row 7 of wei[0] flattens from a 0.2423 peak to 0.1558 with the scaling on.

It's also only about the start. Training can grow the query and key weights and make attention sharp on purpose; the scaling just stops a big head from being born saturated. The paper gives the same variance argument (Vaswani et al. 2017, section 3.2.1). The fused F.scaled_dot_product_attention that nanoGPT uses applies this 1/√d scale by default.

Understand and remember

  • q·k sums head_size products → each product has variance 1 when q and k entries are independent with variance 1 → variances of independent terms add → Var(q·k) ≈ head_size (16.00 at 16, 63.10 at 64)

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

The next card uses 4 heads of 8 dims each, with C = 32. If you kept the Colab's bug and scaled by C**-0.5 instead of head_size**-0.5, what would the variance of unit-variance scores be, and would the heads start sharper or flatter than intended?

Reveal

Flatter. Unscaled variance is head_size = 8; dividing by C = 32 gives 8 / 32 = 0.25 instead of 1, so the typical score is half the intended size (√0.25 = 0.5). The fix, k.shape[-1]**-0.5, divides by 8 and gives 1. With one 32-wide head the bug is invisible, because then C = head_size.
