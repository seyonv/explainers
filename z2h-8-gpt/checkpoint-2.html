Checkpoint 2: from one head to a scaled-up Transformer

Ties together one head, then many heads, feed-forward and residual connections, LayerNorm, dropout and scaling up, from our decoder to nanoGPT to ChatGPT, Adam and AdamW and the training script, with the step-0 loss, BatchNorm, Bessel's correction and "plus copies the gradient" from earlier courses. Lecture 7, 1:19:11 to 1:56:20.

What this ties together, and why now. The second half of Lecture 7 turns one attention head into gpt.py, and the val loss improves with each piece: 2.41 with one head, 2.29 with four heads, 2.27 with a feed-forward layer, 2.08 once the blocks are on a residual stream, 2.06 with LayerNorm, and 1.48 after scaling up 255× with dropout. Each piece fixes a different problem. Multi-head gives a token several things to look for at once. The feed-forward layer gives each token some computation of its own. The residual path lets gradients reach deep layers. LayerNorm keeps the inputs to every sublayer a steady size. Dropout slows memorisation. AdamW with a smaller lr keeps the steps safe. The next lectures (the tokenizer and GPT-2) take this block as given, so check now that you can say which piece fixes which problem.

Comes back from earlier courses: the residual highway works because plus copies the gradient (micrograd). LayerNorm is BatchNorm turned sideways, and so it needs none of BatchNorm's running statistics. Karpathy's hand-written LayerNorm divides by n − 1, which is Bessel's correction. A step-0 loss above ln 65 is the step-0 loss check. The train/val gap is the under- vs overfitting reading from the MLP course.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. You stack three Blocks (n_embd 32, 4 heads) with plain x = f(x). They reach val 2.3318, worse than one attention + feed-forward layer at 2.2748, even though they have 16,865 parameters against 8,609. Why? And what does wrapping each sublayer in x = x + f(x) do to the gradient, with the lab's toy f(x) = 0.01·tanh(x) at x = 2?
    Check
    • backprop multiplies one local derivative per layer. Three small factors give almost nothing: 0.0007065 × 0.0099991 × 0.0100000 = 7.064e-08
    • with a residual, each factor is d/dx (x + f(x)) = 1 + f′(x), so the product stays near 1: 1.0007065 × 1.0006935 × 1.0006807 = 1.0021
    • the 1 comes from the +, because addition hands the gradient to both inputs unchanged. That's the micrograd rule, applied to a whole block
    • measured: the same 16,865 parameters with only x + added reach 2.1560, and with projections and a 4× feed-forward they reach 2.0801

    If you missed it, reopen feed-forward and residual connections, "Why adding helps: the gradient superhighway" and "What each change bought, measured", and backprop by hand, "Does plus always copy and times always swap?".

  2. debugYour gpt.py-shaped model (n_embd 32, 4 heads, 3 residual Blocks, 41,921 parameters, PyTorch's default init) prints step 0: train loss 4.6328. The feed-forward lab's version of the same model printed 4.7957. What should step 0 print, what's wrong, and what's the one-function fix gpt.py ships with?
    Check
    • a model that knows nothing gives each of the 65 characters 1/65: the loss is −ln(1/65) = ln 65 = 4.1744
    • 4.63 means the model starts out confidently wrong. With the default init, the logits have std 0.998 on a 2,048-prediction batch, so softmax prefers characters for no reason (computed; that batch scores 4.6103). It's the bigram's 4.88-vs-4.17 story again
    • nothing crashes, but the first steps go to undoing that confidence instead of learning
    • fix: gpt.py's _init_weights, which draws every Linear and Embedding weight with std 0.02 and zeroes the biases. The logit std drops to 0.003 and the batch scores 4.1743. With LayerNorm too, step 0 prints 4.1700 and val ends at 2.0411, against 2.0629 without the init

    If you missed it, reopen the loss you should see at step 0, "What "knowing nothing" should cost", the bigram baseline, "Where 4.88 sits", and fix 1: the output layer, "Where the 27 comes from".

  3. Model A has one attention head of size 32, and model B has four heads of size 8, concatenated. Count the q, k and v weights in each, and say which one reaches the lower val loss and why. Then do the same for nanoGPT's single c_attn = Linear(384, 1152) against six Heads of 64 (bias=False).
    Check
    • A: 3 × 32 × 32 = 3,072. B: 4 × 3 × 32 × 8 = 3,072. Both models have 7,553 parameters in total
    • B wins, val 2.2884 against 2.4103. It has no extra capacity, but it gives each token four separate softmax rows, so it can look for four different things at once
    • six heads have 18 matrices of 384 × 64, which is 18 × 24,576 = 442,368 weights. The single 384 × 1152 matrix holds the same 442,368. With the shared projection (147,840) both total 590,208
    • once the weights are copied across, the outputs match exactly (max diff 0.00e+00). Batching changes how the work is scheduled, not the maths

    If you missed it, reopen one head, then many heads, "Where the 7,553 parameters are", from our decoder to nanoGPT, "nanoGPT: every head at once", and the toolkit's matmul by shapes, "More than two dimensions, and counting parameters".

  4. which idea?A teammate wants to swap LayerNorm back for the makemore BatchNorm1d, since "it's the same normalisation". Which idea says no? Name the axis each one normalises, and say what happens at generation time with a batch of 1. Also, their hand-written LayerNorm1d differs from nn.LayerNorm by up to 0.0161. Why?
    Check
    • BatchNorm normalises each feature (column) across the batch. LayerNorm normalises each example's features (row). On the lab's (32, 100) x, the row x[0,:] comes out with mean 2.4e-9 and std 1.0000, while the column x[:,0] is left at mean 0.1469, std 0.8803
    • BatchNorm makes each example's output depend on its batchmates, so it needs running averages for test time. Generation runs a batch of 1, where the batch statistics don't exist
    • LayerNorm uses only the token's own 384 numbers, so it has no running buffers and behaves the same in training and at test time
    • the 0.0161 comes from x.var, which divides by n − 1 (Bessel's correction), while nn.LayerNorm divides by n. On [1, 2, 3, 6] that gives −0.9258 against −1.0690 for the first entry

    If you missed it, reopen LayerNorm, dropout, and scaling up, "Rows, not columns: BatchNorm turned sideways", BatchNorm at test time, "The problem: an example's output depends on its batchmates", and Bessel's correction, "Two ways to divide".

  5. which idea?A teammate trains gpt.py at lr 1e-2 "because bigram.py did", reasoning that Adam will sort out the step size. Which idea says that's too big? Use Adam's first-step behaviour and the size of gpt.py's weights to put a number on it.
    Check
    • Adam divides by √v̂, so each weight moves about lr per step whatever its gradient: for g = 0.001, 1 and 1000 the first step is 0.099999, 0.100000 and 0.100000 at lr 0.1. Adam normalises the gradient, so it can't shrink a step that lr sets too big
    • so compare lr with the weights. gpt.py initialises them with std 0.02: lr 3e-4 is 3e-4 / 0.02 = 1.5% per step, and 1e-2 would be 0.01 / 0.02 = 50% per step (computed)
    • bigram.py's 1e-2 is fine on its std-1 table (1% per step). Karpathy already dropped to 1e-3 when the first head went in, because self-attention can't take very high learning rates
    • it's the update:data ratio from the BatchNorm course: aim near 1e-3, not 0.5

    If you missed it, reopen Adam and AdamW, "Why dividing by √v̂ matters", one head, then many heads, "Three bits of plumbing", and the update:data ratio, "The number you track".

  6. nn.Dropout(0.2) on ten ones prints [1.25, 0, 1.25, 1.25, 1.25, 1.25, 1.25, 1.25, 0, 1.25]. Why 1.25, and why doesn't anything need rescaling at test time? What goes wrong if your estimate_loss and sampling code forget model.eval()?
    Check
    • the survivors are scaled by 1/(1 − 0.2) = 1.25, so the expected value is 0.8 × 1.25 + 0.2 × 0 = 1.0, the same as with dropout off. Over 1M ones, 0.200148 were zeroed
    • so at test time dropout just switches off: after model.eval() the ten ones stay 1.0
    • without eval(), 20% of activations are still dropped while you measure and sample. The val loss comes out worse than the model really is, and each sample comes from a random thinned network
    • estimate_loss wraps model.eval() / model.train() around the evaluation for exactly this reason. It was a no-op for the bigram, and it starts to matter at dropout 0.2

    If you missed it, reopen LayerNorm, dropout, and scaling up, "Dropout: zero 20%, scale the survivors by 1.25", and training it, and moving to a script, "What the script adds to the notebook code".

  7. which idea?After the residual stage your run ends at train 1.9845, val 2.0801. You now want to scale to 10,788,929 parameters on the same 1,003,854 training characters. Which idea tells you what to watch, and which two changes in gpt.py are there for it?
    Check
    • overfitting: train running ahead of val is the first sign of memorisation, and only a loss on held-out text shows it. That's why estimate_loss reports both splits
    • scaling up gives 10,788,929 / 1,003,854 ≈ 10.7 parameters per training character, and 10,788,929 / 42,369 ≈ 255× the 2.06 model, so it would memorise faster
    • gpt.py adds dropout 0.2 in three places (after the attention softmax, after the projection, at the end of the feed-forward layer) and lowers lr to 3e-4. Val reaches 1.48 (the video's A100 run)
    • dropout is a small-data tool: nanoGPT's train.py defaults to dropout 0.0 for pretraining and uses 0.2 only for small sets like this one

    If you missed it, reopen feed-forward and residual connections, "Does depth now come for free?", LayerNorm, dropout, and scaling up, "Should you run gpt.py on your M3, and is dropout always on?", and train/dev/test, "Reading the two losses".

  8. Match each job to a Transformer shape: (a) continue Shakespeare, (b) translate French into English, (c) classify a review's sentiment. Then say which of ChatGPT's three fine-tuning stages change the network's architecture, and how far GPT-3 sits from our model in parameters and tokens.
    Check
    • (a) decoder-only: every block's attention is masked, and nothing else feeds in. That's what we built, and it's GPT's setup
    • (b) encoder-decoder: the encoder reads the whole French sentence with no mask, and each decoder block cross-attends to it (queries from the English, keys and values from the encoder)
    • (c) encoder-only: the whole input is known up front, so every token may see every other
    • none of the stages changes the architecture. SFT, the reward model and PPO are all more training, on different data and with a different objective. nanoGPT covers pretraining only
    • GPT-3 175B / 10,788,929 = 16,220× the parameters; 300B / 338,025 GPT-2 tokens = 887,508× the data

    If you missed it, reopen from our decoder to nanoGPT to ChatGPT, "Three kinds of Transformer, and which one we built", "From a document completer to an assistant" and "Ours vs. GPT-3: the same design, scaled".

Start from your gpt.py with its Head, MultiHeadAttention, FeedFoward and Block:

  1. Three switches. GPTLanguageModel(..., residual, layernorm, init_weights). With residual=False, a Block is x = sa(x); x = ffwd(x), with no projections and a Linear(32, 32) + ReLU feed-forward. With residual=True, it's x = x + sa(ln1(x)) and x = x + ffwd(ln2(x)), with projections and the 4× feed-forward. layernorm=False swaps ln1, ln2 and ln_f for nn.Identity(). init_weights applies gpt.py's std-0.02 _init_weights.
