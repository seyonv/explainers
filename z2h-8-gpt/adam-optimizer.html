Adam and AdamW, explained

Also called: adaptive moment estimation, torch.optim.AdamW. Lecture 7 · training the bigram model · 0:34:53

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Plain gradient descent moves each weight by lr × gradient. When some parameters get gradients of 1000 and others 0.001, no single learning rate suits them all, and one noisy batch can shove every weight the wrong way.

Where it fits: In makemore you updated weights by hand with p.data += -lr * p.grad. At this point in the GPT lecture Karpathy replaces that line with torch.optim.AdamW without opening it up. This card opens it before the Transformer arrives.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Which of Adam's two running averages decides the direction of a step, and which decides its size?
  2. On step 3 the gradient turns negative. Which stored number keeps p moving down anyway?
  3. Why is the very first Adam step exactly lr, and what would it be without bias correction?
  4. If you multiply every gradient by 1000, what happens to SGD's step and to Adam's step?
  5. Why does adding λ·p to the gradient not act like real weight decay once Adam divides by √v̂?

In one breath: an optimizer is the rule that turns each parameter's gradient into an update. Plain gradient descent (SGD) steps by lr × gradient. Adam keeps two running averages per parameter: one of the gradients, which says which way to go, and one of the squared gradients, which says how big that parameter's gradients usually are. It divides the first by the square root of the second, so every parameter moves about lr per step whatever its gradient scale. AdamW is Adam plus a separate, gentle pull of every weight toward zero.

Karpathy swaps makemore's hand-written update (p.data += -lr * p.grad) for torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3) here and moves on. This card opens it up.

Predict first: one parameter starts at p = 1.0 and sees gradients 1.0, 0.5, then −0.2 (lr 0.1). A negative gradient means "increase p". On step 3, does Adam move p up or down? What does plain SGD do?

Adam still moves p down, by 0.0597 (0.806782 → 0.747111). SGD moves it up, by 0.02 (0.85 → 0.87). Adam's direction comes from the running average of gradients, m, which is still +0.106 after two positive steps. One small negative gradient slows it down but doesn't turn it around. That's momentum.

Why dividing by √v̂ matters

Real networks mix parameters whose gradients differ by orders of magnitude. With SGD, one lr is too big for some and too small for others. Adam's first step is about lr no matter how big the gradient is (lr 0.1, measured):

gradient gSGD step = lr × gAdam step
0.0010.00010.099999
10.10.100000
10001000.100000

So in Adam, lr is roughly how far each weight moves per step, in the weight's own units. That is a rule of thumb for picking it: compare lr with the size of the weights. bigram.py uses lr 1e-2 on an embedding table that PyTorch initializes with standard deviation 1 (1% per step). gpt.py uses 3e-4 on weights initialized with std 0.02 (1.5% per step, computed). A deep stack also compounds small changes through many layers, which is why Karpathy calls roughly 3e-4 a typical good setting while tiny nets tolerate 1e-3 or more (chapter 6).

▶ Build itruns on your M3 · about 5 s

When: pause the video at 0:34:53, right where torch.optim.AdamW appears, and write Adam yourself before trusting the library.

import math
grads = [1.0, 0.5, -0.2]
lr, beta1, beta2, eps = 0.1, 0.9, 0.999, 1e-8
p, m, v = 1.0, 0.0, 0.0
for t, g in enumerate(grads, start=1):
    m = beta1 * m + (1 - beta1) * g      # momentum
    v = beta2 * v + (1 - beta2) * g * g  # running mean of g squared
    m_hat = m / (1 - beta1**t)           # bias correction
    v_hat = v / (1 - beta2**t)
    step = lr * m_hat / (math.sqrt(v_hat) + eps)
    p = p - step
    print(f"t={t}  m_hat={m_hat:.6f}  v_hat={v_hat:.6f}  p={p:.6f}")
import torch
w = torch.tensor([1.0], dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([w], lr=0.1)
for g in grads:
    opt.zero_grad(); (w * g).sum().backward(); opt.step()
    print(f"{w.item():.6f}", end="  ")  # w.grad == g here

What the W adds: decoupled weight decay

Weight decay shrinks every weight a little each step, so weights that the loss doesn't need drift back toward 0. The old way adds λ·p to the gradient (called L2 regularization). Inside Adam that backfires: the extra term is then divided by √v̂ like everything else, so its strength depends on each parameter's gradient history. Loshchilov and Hutter's fix, AdamW, applies the shrink separately, outside the division. Computed with PyTorch, p = 1.0, a true gradient of 0, lr 0.1, λ = 0.01:

after step123
Adam(weight_decay=0.01): decay inside the gradient0.9000000.8004120.701587
AdamW(weight_decay=0.01): decay applied separately0.9990000.9980010.997003

With nothing else in the gradient, Adam normalizes the tiny λ·p = 0.01 up to a full lr-sized step, 100× the intended 0.001 shrink. AdamW shrinks by exactly lr·λ = 0.1% per step.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Plain SGD

p -= lr * g. The step scales with the gradient: g = 1000 gives a step of 100, g = 0.001 gives 0.0001. One lr can't suit every parameter, and one noisy batch sets the direction.

✗ SGD with momentum

Steps along a running average of gradients, so noise cancels and consistent directions build speed. The step size still scales with the gradient, so the one-lr problem remains.

✗ RMSprop

Divides each gradient by the root of its running squared average, so step sizes even out across parameters. It has no momentum, so each step follows the latest noisy gradient.

✓ AdamW

Momentum for direction, RMS scaling for size, bias correction so the first steps aren't wrong, and weight decay that does what its λ says. It works with little tuning, which is why the lecture, nanoGPT and the GPT-2 reproduction all use it.

Is AdamW always the right choice?

Not always. It costs memory: m and v are two extra numbers per parameter. For gpt.py that's 2 × 10,788,929 = 21,577,858 floats, 86 MB at 4 bytes each (computed), on top of the weights and gradients. SGD with momentum stores one extra number and is still used where it generalizes well, and newer optimizers exist (Karpathy's nanochat pairs AdamW with Muon).

The defaults aren't sacred either. The lecture uses PyTorch's betas (0.9, 0.999) and weight decay 0.01 on every parameter. The GPT-2 reproduction switches to betas (0.9, 0.95) and decay 0.1 on the 2-D weight matrices only, leaving biases and LayerNorm alone (optimizer recipe). And no optimizer fixes a bad lr: Adam makes lr easier to pick, not unnecessary.

Understand and remember

  • m is a running mean of gradients → noise from single batches averages out and consistent directions accumulate → Adam can keep going the same way through one contrary gradient (step 3: g = −0.2, p still drops 0.0597)
  • the step is m̂ / √v̂ → a gradient's scale appears on top and bottom and cancels → each parameter moves about lr per step whatever its gradient size → lr becomes "how far a weight moves", so pick it against the weights' size (1e-2 for a std-1 bigram table, 3e-4 for gpt.py's std-0.02 weights)
  • m and v start at 0 → early averages are biased toward 0, v much more than m (β2 = 0.999) → dividing by 1 − βᵗ undoes it → without it step 1 would be 0.316 instead of 0.1
  • L2 decay adds λ·p to the gradient → Adam then divides it by √v̂ → the decay's strength depends on each parameter's gradient history (0.9 per step instead of 0.999 when g = 0) → AdamW applies p ← p − lr·λ·p outside the division, so λ means what it says
  • detail that matters Adam stores m and v for every parameter → optimizer memory is 2× the weights (gpt.py: 21,577,858 extra floats, 86 MB in fp32) → the optimizer state takes twice the memory of the weights themselves

Reference only

  • torch.optim.AdamW(params, lr=1e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8, weight_decay=0.01) are PyTorch's defaults
  • the lecture's learning rates: bigram.py 1e-2, the notebook 1e-3, gpt.py 3e-4
  • GPT-3 recipe: betas (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.1 on 2-D weights only
  • zero_grad(set_to_none=True) and the opt.step() call order
  • Kingma & Ba 2014 (Adam), Loshchilov & Hutter 2017 (AdamW)

Check
  • BatchNorm: batch means and stds of activations, used at inference
  • Adam: gradients and squared gradients, used on every training step
  • Adam uses its averages from step 1, while they still carry the start-at-zero bias

vs The GPT-3 recipe: AdamW, clipping, warmup and cosine decay: This lecture uses AdamW's PyTorch defaults; the GPT-2 reproduction changes β2 to 0.95 and applies decay 0.1 to 2-D weights only. What does a smaller β2 change, and why exempt biases and LayerNorm?

Check
  • β2 0.95 averages over roughly the last 20 squared gradients instead of 1000, so v reacts faster when gradient sizes change
  • biases and LayerNorm gains are few and set offsets and scales; pulling them to 0 hurts more than it regularizes
  • weight matrices are where the capacity is, so that's where the decay helps

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Remove the bias correction (use m and v directly). On the worked example, what is step 1, and after many steps does the missing correction still matter?

Reveal

Step 1 becomes 0.1 × 0.1 / √0.001 = 0.316, about 3× too big, because v starts much further below its true size than m does (1 − 0.999 vs 1 − 0.9). The corrections 1 − βᵗ approach 1 as t grows (β2ᵗ is still 0.37 at t = 1000), so the error fades over the first few thousand steps. It matters most at the start, which is exactly when the weights are fragile. That's one reason the GPT-2 recipe also warms up the learning rate.
