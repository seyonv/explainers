Every way to raise throughput, ranked

Also called inference optimisation, tokens/s/GPU, or cost per million tokens. This card answers the course's third big question, "what are the ways to increase inference throughput?", with one table: every lever, grouped by which term of the step-time formula it attacks, with a measured or cited effect, its cost, and who controls it. Our own numbers are for Llama-3.1-8B in BF16 on one H100, from our simulator's 100%-of-peak cost model.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: There are dozens of ways to raise throughput, and a list of all of them doesn't tell you which one to try first. Some are worth 49×, some +4%, some help only at a small batch and some only at a large one, and some cancel each other. Without tying each to a term of the step formula you can't predict which.

Where it fits: First card of group 4, answering the course's third big question. It regroups every lever you have met (batching, paging, chunking, prefix caching, overlap) plus the ones outside the scheduler (quantization, speculation, parallelism) under the term of the step formula each attacks.

In one breath

Throughput is the number of useful tokens a step produces divided by how long the step takes. So there are only five things you can do: put more tokens in each step, read fewer bytes per step, do less work per useful token, cut the time the GPU sits waiting on the CPU, or buy more GPUs. The big levers are the first two. Continuous batching and a full batch are worth 10–49×, while most of the others are worth 1–2× each, and some of those only work at a small batch or only at a large one.

Everyday version: a delivery van's parcels per hour. You can load more parcels per trip (batching), use a lighter van (quantization), skip parcels someone already delivered (prefix caching), cut the time spent at the depot between trips (overhead), or buy more vans (hardware). Each fix only helps if it attacks what's actually slowing the van down.

Which levers stack, and which don't

Gains multiply only when two levers attack different terms. When they attack the same term, the second one mostly finds nothing left to cut.

✓ Batch + FP8 KV: stack

The batch raises T_out and FP8 halves the KV read, which is 80% of a full step. At B = 238, FP8 KV takes 9,973 to 16,609 tok/s on top of the 49× from batching; with its doubled capacity (476 seqs) it reaches 17,761.

✗ Batch + 8-bit weights: overlap

Both shrink each token's share of the weight read. 8-bit weights give 2.0× at B = 1 but +4% at B = 238, and with them batching is worth 404 → 10,377 = 25.7×, not 49×. Quantize weights when you can't batch.

✗ Batch + speculative decoding: fight

Speculation spends idle math to get more tokens out of each weight read. A big batch has already spent that math, which is why the cited gain falls from 1.96× toward 1.21× as the batch grows (the source gives the range by batch size).

✗ Continuous batching × bigger batch: don't multiply

They're the same gain counted twice. Continuous batching is how the batch stays full, so the 10× and the 49× aren't independent. Likewise, prefix caching only pays across replicas when routing sends requests to the replica that holds the prefix.

Throughput or latency: which lever when?

Every lever in the top two groups makes each user wait longer, cuts their latency, or leaves it alone, so the right set depends on where your SLO and your load sit. Our SLO is TTFT ≤ 1 s and TPOT ≤ 50 ms.

Low load (can't fill a batch)High load (queue is full) Loose SLO
Cut the bill, not the latencyFewer or cheaper GPUs, 8-bit weights (2.0× at B = 1). Batching buys nothing: at 2 req/s every strategy gives ≈ 520 tok/s (E2).
Max throughput: offline and batch jobsRun at the memory cap, FP8 KV, max_num_seqs up (512 seqs: 8,980 tok/s at 37.2 ms TPOT, E4), big token budget, prefix caching.
Tight SLO
Latency levers8-bit weights, speculative decoding (best at a small batch), tensor parallelism, a small token budget so long prompts don't stall decodes.
Goodput, not raw tok/sCap the batch at the TPOT SLO (16 seqs: 5.8 ms but 2,694 tok/s), chunked prefill, priority, disaggregation, and more replicas when capacity (40 req/s here) runs out.

When not to chase throughput: a single interactive user (a laptop running one stream) gets nothing from batching. Past the SLO knee, more tok/s can mean less goodput: at 60 req/s continuous batching reaches 9,102 tok/s but only 41% of requests meet the SLO (E2). Quantizing also has an accuracy cost that no throughput number shows, so check quality before shipping it.

Understand and remember

  • Throughput = T_out ÷ (max(math, W/BW) + KV/BW + t_cpu) → every lever raises the numerator or shrinks one denominator term → it helps only if that term is large right now
  • At B = 1 the weight read is 98% of the step, at B = 238 only 20% (KV read 80%) → weight quantization is a small-batch lever, KV quantization a large-batch lever → FP8 KV stacks with batching (9,973 → 16,609), 8-bit weights barely do (+4%)
  • Speculative decoding spends idle math for extra tokens per weight read → a big batch has already spent that math → its gain falls (1.96× → 1.21×) and it fights batching
  • Past capacity, more tok/s can mean less goodput → pick levers by where you sit: loose vs tight SLO, low vs high load
  • detail that matters Cost per million output tokens = GPU price per second ÷ tok/s × 10⁶ → the 49× batching gain is a 49× cost cut ($3.39 → $0.070 at an illustrative $2.50/h)

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

You apply both 8-bit weights and FP8 KV at B = 64, 2k context. Before computing: is the combined gain roughly the product of the two separate gains, or less?

Reveal

Close to additive in time, not multiplicative in gain. Alone, 8-bit weights take the step from 9.92 to 7.53 ms (1.32×) and FP8 KV to 7.36 ms (1.35×). Together they remove both savings from one 9.92 ms step, about 4.96 ms, giving about 12,900 tok/s (2.0×) versus 1.78× if you multiplied. At B = 64 the two reads are similar in size, so both levers pay; the real product-vs-overlap rule is about which terms each one cuts.
