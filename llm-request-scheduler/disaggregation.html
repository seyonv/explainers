Splitting prefill and decode

Also called disaggregated serving, P/D disaggregation or prefill/decode disaggregation (DistServe, Splitwise, Mooncake, NVIDIA Dynamo, llm-d). A variant that sends some requests straight to decode is called conditional disaggregation. It's the step past chunked prefill: instead of sharing a GPU more politely, give each phase its own GPUs.

In one breath

Prefill and decode want different things from a GPU. Prefill wants math and decode wants memory bandwidth. On one GPU they get in each other's way, and both have to use the same parallelism setup. Disaggregation runs prompts on a prefill pool and ships each request's KV cache over a fast link to a decode pool that does nothing but generate tokens. Chunking softens the interference, and splitting removes it. The price is a KV transfer per request and a second pool to size and run.

The new request pays a few ms of TTFT, and the 64 running streams stop paying 63 ms. Mooncake doesn't wait for the full prefill: it streams the KV to the decode node layer by layer as prefill runs, which hides much of the transfer (see Course 4's reading of the paper).

Sizing the pools: a ratio, not a count

Each pool has to keep up with its own token demand. At an arrival rate λ, the prefill pool must process λ·p̄ prompt tokens per second and the decode pool must produce λ·ō output tokens per second. λ cancels, so the split depends only on the workload shape and each pool's per-GPU rate:

# our estimate: CostModel + poisson_trace (20,000 samples), 100% of peak course workload: prompt median 512 → mean 700; output median 200 → mean 268 R_P: one 2,048-token prefill step = 33.3 ms → 61,434 tok/s R_D: mean decode context 933 tokens; 487,504 / 933 → 522 seqs fit step = 27.5 ms (under the 50 ms TPOT) → 18,964 tok/s N_P / N_D = (700 / 268) x (18,964 / 61,434) = 2.62 x 0.309 = 0.81 ≈ 4 : 5 long prompts (median 2,048 → mean 2,283; 193 seqs fit) = 1.12 : 1

Our estimate, not a measurement: a ceiling where each pool runs at 100% busy, with no queueing and no transfer overhead. Even though prompts are 2.6× longer than outputs, the decode pool needs more GPUs, because one decode GPU produces 3.2× fewer tokens per second than a prefill GPU processes. Longer prompts tip the ratio toward prefill. Traffic mix drifts during the day, which is why NVIDIA Dynamo's Planner moves GPUs between the two pools based on TTFT and ITL targets instead of fixing the ratio.

What the split buys here, in the same model: 8 GPUs as 4 P + 4 D top out at ≈ 283 req/s (decode-limited). The same 8 GPUs colocated manage ≈ 8 × 33.5 = 268 req/s (E4: 8,980 tok/s at overload with 512 seats, ÷ 268 mean output). That's only about +6% throughput for an 8B model with short prompts. The larger gain is that decode steps never carry a prefill, so TPOT stays at the decode-only step time. The published wins below come from bigger models, longer prompts and SLO-bound goodput.

When not to disaggregate

  • Short prompts. A 512-token prompt adds only 4.6 ms to a shared step, and over a 100 Gbps link its handoff costs 65% of the prefill it saves. Chunked prefill or conditional disaggregation handles these better.
  • Small models. An 8B model fits on one GPU with room for 522 streams. In our estimate the split buys about +6% throughput. The large published gains come from 70B–671B models whose phases want different parallelism.
  • A single GPU. There's nothing to split. Course 2's 1 prefill + 1 decode example removes the stalls but halves per-GPU throughput at that scale.
  • Low traffic. At 2 req/s the course workload needs 2 × 700 = 1,400 prefill tokens/s, which is 2.3% of one prefill GPU. That GPU would sit idle, and the colocated server already gets a TTFT p50 of 11.1 ms (E2).
  • No fast link. Below ≈ 8.1 GB/s it's cheaper to prefill again than to ship the KV. Near that speed the transfer eats most of the TTFT benefit.

Why it beats the alternatives at fleet scale, with long prompts

✗ One pool, whole prompts

Good TTFT, but a 4,096-token prompt turns a 9.9 ms decode step into 72.8 ms for all 64 streams.

✗ One pool, prefill-first batches

Prompts never share a step with decodes, but streams just pause while the prefill runs: 66.7 ms for the same prompt.

✗ One pool, chunked prefill

The stall is capped, but not gone: ITL p99 is 36.2 ms at budget 2,048 vs a 9.9 ms decode-only step. Both phases still share one parallelism layout and one GPU count.

✓ Separate pools, KV over a fast link

Decode steps carry only decodes, each pool gets its own parallelism, hardware and size, and a 4,096-token handoff costs 1.2–10.7 ms on NVLink or a 50 GB/s link.

Is chunked prefill or disaggregation better?

Neither, in general. They fix the same interference at different scales, and large systems use both: Mooncake still chunks long prefills inside its prefill pool. A rough guide:

If your situation is…Chunked prefill, one poolDisaggregation
GPUs per model1 to a fewmany; the pools need enough GPUs to size separately
Prompt lengthshort to medium (≈ 512)long (2k and up); the handoff is cheap relative to prefill
Interconnectanything; no KV movesNVLink or RDMA well above ≈ 8 GB/s
SLOsTPOT target loose enough for a budget-sized stepstrict TTFT and strict TPOT at the same time
Modelfits one GPU or one nodebig MoE or dense models wanting different parallelism per phase
Prefix-cache hitshigh hits make prefill cheap anywayuse conditional disaggregation: cached requests skip the handoff
Ops costone pool, one configtwo pools, a ratio to keep right, a KV transfer layer (NIXL, connectors)

Start with chunked prefill. That's the default in vLLM V1 and our Scheduler. Split when you run many GPUs, long prompts and tight SLOs on both TTFT and TPOT. Even then, let short or cached requests skip the handoff.
