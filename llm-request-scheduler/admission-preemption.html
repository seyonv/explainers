Admission, preemption and thrashing

Also called KV cache OOM handling, eviction, retraction (SGLang's word), pausing (TensorRT-LLM's), and the watermark (the blocks kept back at admission). It shows up in every continuous-batching engine as the if not can_allocate(...) branch of step(). This card uses the course's running example (Llama-3.1-8B BF16 on one H100, 487,504 tokens of KV), our simulator labs/scheduler.py at 100% of peak (a floor, not a benchmark), and the 8-request toy trace.

In one breath

When a request is admitted nobody knows how long its answer will be, and its KV cache grows by one token (128 KiB here) every step. The scheduler can reserve for the worst case and never evict anyone, reserve only for the next step and evict someone when memory runs out, or guess in between. Eviction (preemption) frees a victim's KV and rebuilds it later, either by running its prefill again or by copying it back from CPU memory. When admission is too eager, the same request gets admitted and evicted over and over: that's thrashing.

How SGLang's estimate moves

From the source (new_token_ratio_tracker.py): the ratio starts at 0.7 (× schedule_conservativeness, default 1.0), falls by (0.7 − 0.098) ÷ 600 ≈ 0.001 on each decode step with no retraction, and bottoms out at 0.7 × 0.14 = 0.098. After a retraction it's reset upward from what was actually observed: (tokens decoded + 20 × requests) ÷ Σ max_new_tokens. Admit optimistically, back off after running out: an additive-decrease, jump-up controller, much like TCP's congestion window.

Preemption, part 1: who is the victim?

A running request needs one more block and the free list is empty. Somebody's KV has to go. Waiting requests never trigger this; only running ones do (vLLM, and our step()):

while not self.bm.can_allocate(req, n):
    victim = self.running.pop()   # newest admitted
    self._preempt(victim)
    preempted.append(victim)
    if victim is req:
        break

Preemption, part 2: how do you bring it back?

Recompute frees the victim's blocks, sets num_computed = 0 and puts it at the front of the waiting queue; later it's prefilled again, prompt plus every token it had generated. Swap copies its KV to CPU memory over PCIe and copies it back later. Which one is cheaper for n tokens?

Thrashing, in the toy trace

Toy config: 12 KV blocks of 4 tokens, a 16-token budget, at most 4 sequences, watermark 0. Request 4 has a 30-token prompt, so its prompt alone takes 8 of the 12 blocks. Each row is one step's pool after allocation.

Our simulation, E8 (labs/results.md). Request 4 is admitted at step 4 because its first 8-token chunk fits; the rest of its prompt doesn't. Twice it grabs blocks, runs out while 1 and 3 keep growing, and is evicted as the newest. It computes 22 + 14 = 36 prefill tokens that are thrown away, more than its whole 30-token prompt, and gets its first token at 50.7 ms after arriving. Request 6 is evicted once more at step 12: 3 preemptions in 21 steps.

Four fixes, and what they cost

  1. Watermark reserve. Admit a new request only if, after its allocation, some fraction of blocks is still free, so running requests have room to grow. vLLM: watermark, default 0.0 (off), applied only to waiting or preempted admissions. Our scheduler.py: 0.01.
  2. Admit only if the whole prompt fits. Chunked prefill lets a request in on its first chunk; check the blocks for the whole remaining prompt instead, even if you compute only a chunk this step.
  3. Don't admit in a step that preempted. vLLM and our step() already do this (the not preempted test); E8's thrashing happens anyway, because the readmission comes one step later.
  4. Back off. After a preemption, pause admission for a few steps (a cooldown), or, like SGLang, jump the reservation estimate back up and let it decay again.

On the realistic pool, whole-prompt admission with a 5% reserve removes 98–100% of preemptions and is slightly faster, and reservation to completion is the slowest in both.

When admission keeps failing: backpressure

Every fix above just moves the wait into the queue. At 2.1 GB the p99 request waits 61–67 s for its first token, which is a failure by the course SLO (TTFT ≤ 1 s) even though it's eventually served. Past that point, a server should say no quickly instead of queueing forever:

  • Bound the queue, return HTTP 429. Hugging Face TGI's max_concurrent_requests (default 128) refuses excess requests "to handle backpressure" with an overloaded error; Triton's queue policy has max_queue_size, a timeout, and timeout_action REJECT or DELAY.
  • Reject early, by prediction. Mooncake (Moonshot AI's Kimi serving system) uses "prediction-based early rejection" when overloaded. As we read it, the point is to refuse at arrival, using predicted load, before the request has spent any prefill compute, rather than evicting or timing it out later.
  • Let the layer above react. A fast 429 lets the client back off and the router send the next request to another replica, or the autoscaler add one. See Handling concurrent requests for the queue in the server and Many replicas: routing, admission and autoscaling for the fleet view.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Reserve to completion

Never preempts, but holds 1,536 tokens for a request that uses 733 on average: 317 fit instead of ≈ 665. In our sim at 8.4 GB: 4,663 vs 6,108 tok/s, TTFT p99 17.9 s vs 4.1 s.

✗ Optimistic with no guard

Fills memory, but admits on a first chunk and then evicts: request 4 re-prefills 36 thrown-away tokens for a 30-token prompt; at 2.1 GB, 995,150 tokens are recomputed (about 16% of GPU time in our model).

✗ A low max_num_seqs

Caps the batch so memory never runs out, but the cap also caps throughput: 2,694 tok/s at 16 seats vs 8,980 at 512 (E4, 60 req/s). See the box below.

✓ Optimistic, guarded, with backpressure

Allocate per step, admit only if the whole prompt fits plus a small reserve, evict the newest and recompute, and return 429s when the queue is too long. Preemptions 469 → 11 at 2.1 GB, full packing everywhere else.

Why not just cap max_num_seqs low enough to never preempt?

Because you'd be sizing the batch for the worst case all the time, which is (a) with extra steps. E4's sweep at full memory and 60 req/s (overload):

A cap of 16 leaves 70% of the throughput on the table (2,694 vs 8,980 tok/s), and since the queue grows faster, the median wait goes from 0.52 s to 38.9 s. Memory is only one of the limits; the cap throttles compute too.

When a cap is the right tool. max_num_seqs is a good latency knob: TPOT rises from 5.8 to 37.2 ms across the sweep, so a strict TPOT SLO (say 10 ms) justifies a cap near 64. And when latency must be predictable per request (a paid tier, a long batch job that must not restart), reserve-to-completion (TensorRT-LLM's default) is a fair choice, especially with a tight max_new_tokens. On a laptop serving one user, none of this matters: one sequence never competes for memory.
