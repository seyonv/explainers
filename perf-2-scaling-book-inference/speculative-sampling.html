Speculative sampling, and is batch > 240 real?

Also called speculative decoding, draft-and-verify, or lookahead decoding; plus the book's empirical batch-scaling check. This is Scaling Book Part 7, Appendix D ("Speculative Sampling") and Appendix A ("How real is the batch size > 240 rule?"). The book's numbers are TPU v5e. The Llama-3.1-8B / H100 figures are our recomputation, not in the book.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: At batch 1 a decode step spends almost all its time reading the weights and does a sliver of math. Batching fills that idle math with other users' tokens, which helps throughput but not the single user waiting. Speculative sampling fills it with guesses for the same user, and you need to know when that is exact, when it pays and when it backfires.

Where it fits: The last card: Appendices D and A. It uses the flat-then-linear step time from the critical-batch card and the step-time formula, and Appendix A checks that shape against a real measurement.

In one breath

A decode step at small batch is memory-bound: its time is spent reading the weights, not doing math. So checking 4 guessed tokens costs almost the same as generating 1. A cheap draft model's guesses become free tokens whenever they're right, and the output distribution doesn't change. Appendix A's measurement confirms the flat-then-linear step time that makes this work, and batching too.

The book's recipe (greedy decoding first)

  1. Draft. "Sample greedily from some smaller, cheaper model." Ideally that's one trained to match the big one, for example by distillation, "but it could be as simple as simply using n-grams or token matching a small corpus of text." Generate K tokens.
  2. Verify in one pass. Run the big model once over all K drafted tokens and get the next-token logits at every position.
  3. Keep the longest correct prefix. Where the big model's top token disagrees, keep what came before, replace the first wrong token with the big model's token, and discard the rest. If all K are correct, the last logit gives one bonus token. Go back to 1.

Why it's a latency win

"This scheme still requires us to do the FLOPs-equivalent of one forward pass through the big model for every token, but because we can batch a bunch of tokens together, we can do all these FLOPs in one forward pass and take advantage of the fact that we're not compute-bound to score more tokens for free."

On the running example (our recomputation): Llama-3.1-8B at batch 1 on an H100 reads 16.06 GB of weights per step, which takes 4.79 ms. Verifying K + 1 = 5 tokens adds 2 · 8.03e9 · 5 / 9.89e14 = 81 µs of math. That's far below the weight read, so the verify step still costs about one step. The book adds two more points:

  • Each accepted token costs more FLOPs (rejected drafts, plus the draft model), "but we wring more FLOPs out of the hardware, and the small model is cheap, so we win overall." The book also notes that speculative step times "are usually quite a bit slower than normal autoregressive sampling", but you get more than one token per step.
  • KV loads are shared across the verified tokens, "so speculative decoding can also be a throughput win for long context."

Non-greedy decoding: the output distribution is unchanged

The book describes it as "a Metropolis-Hastings inspired algorithm": compare Pdraft(chosen token) with Ptarget(chosen token) and reject probabilistically when the ratio is small. It points to two papers that derived it at the same time (Leviathan et al.; Chen et al.). Their rule (Leviathan et al. §2.3), with p the target and q the draft:

Appendix A: is the batch > 240 rule real?

The book measured one layer with dmodel = 8192, dff = 32768 and "only 2 matmuls per layer" (from its Colab). Step time "increases very slowly up until around batch 240, and then increases linearly", and throughput rises "until about BS240 per data parallel shard". The rule is "roughly true but ignores some ability of the TPU to prefetch the weights" while other operations (like inter-device communication) leave HBM idle.

book: "layer is about 600M parameters sharded 4 ways … roughly 365us at minimum" 2 bytes × 600M / 4 chips = 300 MB per chip / 8.2e11 B/s = 365.9 µs ✓ reproduces 365 exact params: 2 × 8192 × 32768 = 537M → 268 MB per chip = 327.4 µs knee: T_math = 2·B·600M/4 / 1.97e14 equals 365.9 µs at B = 1.97e14 / 8.2e11 = 240

Step = max(2 bytes × params / 3.35e12, 2 × params × tokens / 9.89e14), ignoring KV reads, which would favour speculation at long context. Once B(K+1) passes Bcrit ≈ 295, the verify step is compute-bound and the gain fades. By B = 256 it's a slowdown. This fits the book's takeaway: speculation trades throughput for per-token latency, and it's "a win-win" only "where batch size is limited (e.g. small hardware footprint or large KV caches)".

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Bigger batch

Fills the same flat region with other users' tokens. Total throughput goes up, but each user still gets one token per 4.79 ms step, so single-user latency doesn't improve.

✗ Smaller model

A 1B model steps in 0.6 ms instead of 4.79 ms, but you get the 1B model's answers. Quality changes.

✗ Lookahead without verification

Emit the draft's tokens directly. It's fast, but the output follows q, not p: TV = 0.152 in the toy lab versus 0.002 with verification.

✓ Speculative sampling

Spends the idle FLOPs of a memory-bound step on checking guesses: 2.24× at α = 0.8, K = 4 on our 8B example, with the target's exact output distribution.

Is it always a win?

No. It costs extra compute (rejected drafts, the draft model) and extra memory for the draft's weights and KV cache. At high batch, where the step is already compute-bound, it can slow things down (0.70× at B = 256 above). Acceptance also drops with temperature and with any mismatch between draft and target. When α is low, even a cheap draft "can still be too expensive" (book). That's why drafter heads inside the model (Medusa, EAGLE) are popular: they share most parameters, run faster and match the target more closely.

Throughput-first serving usually fills the flat region with more users (critical batch size) rather than with guesses. Speculation is the latency lever. It doesn't speed up prefill, so TTFT is unchanged. For the consumer-side view, see speculative decoding (llm-latency) and speculative decoding (standalone card).

On Appendix A: 240 is a roofline number. The measured knee is soft because the TPU can prefetch weights while HBM is otherwise idle, so treat "B > 240" (≈ 295 on H100) as roughly true, not exact.

Understand and remember

  • At small batch a step is bound by reading the weights (4.79 ms for Llama-3.1-8B), and verifying 5 tokens adds only 81 µs of math → one big-model pass scores K + 1 positions for about the price of one → accepted drafts are free tokens
  • Accept each draft x with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)); on rejection resample from max(0, p − q) → every emitted token is distributed exactly as p → quality is unchanged, only the trajectory differs
  • Expected tokens per verify = (1 − αK+1)/(1 − α) and each round costs K draft steps plus one verify → speedup = that ÷ (K·c + 1) → cheap, well-matched drafts (small c, high α) win; the best K rises with α
  • Verification multiplies tokens per step by K + 1 → once B·(K + 1) passes Bcrit the verify step turns compute-bound → gains fade (2.12× at B = 64) and invert (0.70× at B = 256) → speculation is the latency lever where batch is limited, not a throughput lever
  • detail that matters Appendix A's measured step time is flat, then linear, with the knee near batch 240 per data-parallel shard → the rule is roughly true but the knee is soft because the TPU prefetches weights while HBM is otherwise idle
  • detail that matters KV loads are shared across the verified tokens of a sequence → speculation can also help throughput at long context, where KV reads dominate
