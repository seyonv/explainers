The step-time formula

Also called the decode latency/throughput model, "the one equation of LLM serving", or the latency–throughput Pareto frontier. This is Scaling Book Part 7, "Theoretical estimates for LLM latency and throughput", which the book introduces with: "if there is one thing we want the reader to take away from this entire chapter, it's the following." The book's numbers are TPU v5e. H100 and Llama-3.1-8B figures are our recomputation.

In one breath

Every decode step has to read all the weights once, read every sequence's KV cache, and do 2 FLOPs per parameter per token. So step time ≈ KV reads + max(weight reads, matmul math). Batching spreads the one weight read over more tokens, until either the math or the KV cache's memory takes over.

The book's Pop Quiz, worked

"Assume we want to take a generate step with a batch size of 4 tokens from a 30B parameter dense model on TPU v5e 4x4 slice in int8 with bf16 FLOPs, 8192 context and 100 kB / token KV caches. What is a reasonable lower bound on the latency of this operation? What if we wanted to sample a batch of 256 tokens?" Try it before reading on.

Parameters, int8: 30e9 params × 1 byte = 30 GB KV per sequence: 100e3 B/token × 8192 tokens = 819 MB 16 chips: W = 16 × 8.2e11 = 1.312e13 B/s C = 16 × 1.97e14 = 3.152e15 FLOP/s B = 4 (small, so use formula 1) (4 × 819e6 + 30e9) / 1.312e13 = 33.3e9 / 1.312e13 = 2.54 ms (book: 2.5 ms) check with formula 3: 0.25 KV + max(0.08 math, 2.29 weights) = 2.54 ms B = 256 (past B_crit = 120 for int8 weights, so the MLP is compute-bound) KV: 256 × 819e6 / 1.312e13 = 15.98 ms MLP: 2 × 256 × 30e9 / 3.152e15 = 4.87 ms (> 2.29 ms weight read) total = 20.86 ms (book: "roughly ... 21ms")

Formula 1 alone would give 18.27 ms at B = 256, too low, because it ignores the math. Our check, not in the book: at B = 256 the slice holds 209.7 GB of KV + 30 GB of weights = 239.7 GB of its 256 GB, so the batch just fits. The trade: 64× the batch costs 8.2× the latency and buys 7.8× the throughput (1,577 → 12,274 tok/s, or 99 → 767 per chip).

Latency against throughput: the Pareto curve

Each point is one batch size. Moving right trades per-user latency for total throughput. Throughput rises steeply while the fixed weight read is being shared, then flattens toward BW ÷ SKV: past that point every extra sequence adds its own KV read, and attention is bandwidth-bound at any B. The book's figure: PaLM's latency–cost frontier

The book follows the quiz with: "there's a clear tradeoff between throughput and latency here. Small batches are fast but don't utilize the hardware well. Big batches are slow but efficient." It then shows a Pareto frontier of cost (read: throughput) against latency for several PaLM models, from the Efficiently Scaling Transformer Inference (ESTI) paper. The figure isn't reproduced here. Its caption says:

  • Chip count C and batch size B move you along the frontier. Each point is a (C, B) setup.
  • The green dot is off the frontier (PaLM 540B, C:32, B:16): "the available memory prevented the setup from supporting a good batch size and caused throughput to suffer."
  • "Throughput generally tends to flatten around after the batch size 240." That is Bcrit.
  • "int8 weights offers a better latency-throughput pareto optimal, but not a better max throughput."

The same flattening appears in the Pop Quiz setup (our recomputation). Per-chip throughput is 27, 99, 304, 467, 637 tok/s at B = 1, 4, 16, 32, 64, then 767 at B = 128, 240 and 256. Past Bcrit (120 here) both the KV term and the math term grow linearly in B, so B ÷ step is constant: 1 ÷ (SKV/W + 2N/C). Our reading of the int8 bullet: int8 halves the weight read, which speeds up small batches, but it doesn't change the bf16 math term, which sets the plateau.

"Not only do we trade off latency and throughput with batch size as knob, we may also prefer a larger topology to a smaller one so we can fit larger batches if we find ourselves limited by HBM."

Takeaway (book): "if you care about generation throughput, use the largest per-chip batch size possible. Any per-chip batch size above the TPU arithmetic intensity (Bcrit, usually 120 or 240) will maximize throughput. You may need to increase your topology to achieve this. Smaller batch sizes will allow you to improve latency at the cost of throughput."

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ tok/s = BW ÷ params

3.35e12 ÷ 16.06e9 = 209 tok/s. Right at B = 1 (the formula gives 205), but it ignores KV and batching. At B = 128 and 2k the H100 does 8,505 tok/s total, 41× this rule, and each user gets 66, not 209.

✗ FLOPs ÷ peak

2 × 8.03e9 ÷ 9.89e14 = 16.2 µs per token. At small batch that's 295× too optimistic, because the 4.79 ms weight read is the real cost. It only becomes a fair estimate past Bcrit, and even then it misses attention.

✗ "Just benchmark it"

You get one number for one setup, and no model of what to change. It can't tell you that at 8k context and full batch the KV read is 80% of the step (18.91 of 23.70 ms), so FP8 weights would help far less than a smaller KV cache.

✓ KV + max(math, weights)

Three terms, each tied to a knob: batch, context and KV size, weight precision, chip count. It reproduces the quiz's 2.5 and 21 ms, predicts where throughput flattens, and names which term to attack.

Is this what a real server will do?

No. It's a lower bound on step time, and so an upper bound on throughput. It assumes peak bandwidth, perfect overlap of HBM reads with FLOPs, and nothing else running. The book's own "nits": "In practice we often don't quite see a sharp roofline" because:

  • "Our assumption that HBM reads will be perfectly overlapped with FLOPs is not realistic, since our compiler (XLA) is fallible."
  • "For sharded models, XLA also often fails to efficiently overlap the ICI communication of our model-sharded matrix multiples with the FLOPs themselves, so we often start taking a latency hit on linears over BS=32."
  • "Batch sizes larger than the theoretical roofline will still see some improvement in throughput because of imperfect overlapping, but this is a good heuristic."

Beyond the book (our notes): real GPU engines also lose time to kernel-launch overheads and to prefill work mixed into decode steps, so measured decode typically lands below this ceiling. The formula also leaves out attention FLOPs, the sampling step and communication, and it counts the embedding table as matmul FLOPs.

When it's the wrong model: MoE (only the active experts' weights are read at small B, but most experts are touched at large B), speculative decoding (several tokens per weight read), and prefill (compute-bound; use 2·N·T ÷ C instead). Consistency note: perf-1 serving-metrics uses formula 1 at ~1,100 context: B = 1 → 207 tok/s, B = 443 → 18,567 tok/s total, 23.86 ms. Up to Bcrit the general formula gives the same numbers (we get 4.84 ms at B = 1 and 9.96 ms at B = 120). At B = 443 its MLP turns math-bound (7.19 ms > 4.79 ms), and the book's additive formula gives 26.26 ms and 16,870 tok/s. The gap is the overlap assumption.
