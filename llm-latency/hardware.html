Inference hardware

Also called "where the model runs" or "the host's silicon". It's the biggest reason the same model is 36× faster on one provider than another, and 110× faster than on your laptop.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The same open model runs at 48 tokens/s on one host and 1,745 on another, and at 15 on your laptop. Without a picture of where bandwidth comes from you can't tell a real speed advantage from a marketing number, or decide whether buying hardware is worth it.

Where it fits: Second mechanism card: the bandwidth side of the memory-bandwidth division. Quantization and MoE, next, work on the bytes side.

In one breath

Hardware sets the memory bandwidth, and bandwidth sets the ceiling on tokens per second. Datacenter GPUs have about 30–80× your M3's bandwidth. Specialty chips (Groq, Cerebras) keep the weights in on-chip SRAM, which is faster again.

1,745 ÷ 48 ≈ 36× between hosts serving the same weights. Hardware isn't the only difference: providers also differ in quantization, batch size (how many users share a chip) and speculative decoding.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Your laptop (M3)

Private, free and offline, but 100 GB/s caps a 4B model at about 35 t/s, and larger models slow down in proportion.

✗ Consumer GPU (RTX 4090)

10× the bandwidth, but only 24 GB of memory. Big models don't fit, or have to be heavily quantized.

✗ Cheap, heavily shared GPU host

H100-class chips, but large batches share each GPU across many users, so you may see only 48–90 t/s.

✓ Latency-tuned host (SRAM or speed tier)

Weights in SRAM or across many GPUs, small batches, speculative decoding. That gets 477–1,745 t/s on the same model.

Should I always pick the fastest host?

No. Specialty hosts only serve a short list of open models, may charge more per token, and are sometimes quantized, so check the quantization column on OpenRouter. They can't run Claude or GPT at all.

For short answers the difference disappears: at 80 tokens, even 48 t/s is 1.7 s, and network plus TTFT dominate. If your answers are long, cut output length first. It's free.

Understand and remember

  • Hardware sets memory bandwidth → bandwidth sets the tokens/s ceiling → laptop 100 GB/s, H100 3.35 TB/s, SRAM chips 80+ TB/s to 21 PB/s → the ceiling spans orders of magnitude
  • SRAM sits on the chip next to the compute → far more bandwidth but little capacity (44 GB per Cerebras wafer) → big models must be split across many chips → only a short list of models is served
  • A host shares each chip across many users to cut cost → your slice of the bandwidth shrinks → cheap H100 hosts deliver 48–90 t/s despite a ~1,240 t/s single-user ceiling
  • detail that matters Capacity decides what fits, bandwidth decides how fast → a 24 GB RTX 4090 has 10× the M3's bandwidth but can't hold big models without heavy quantization

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

A new laptop chip doubles the M3's bandwidth to 200 GB/s. Predict qwen3.5:4b's speed on it from the M3's measured 15.4 t/s, and say whether you'd expect exactly 2×.

Reveal

About 30 t/s if the same 44% of the ceiling is reached (200 ÷ 2.85 ≈ 70 t/s ceiling × 0.44). Probably a bit less than 2×: the Apple chip table showed the share of ceiling falling as bandwidth rose (75% → 58%), since fixed software overheads take a bigger slice as each token gets faster.

Your qwen3.5:4b is Q4_K_M, and OpenRouter lists each provider's quantization (bf16, fp8, fp4).In one breathStore each weight in fewer bits, like 4 instead of 16. The model gets smaller, and since generating each token means reading every weight, fewer bytes means faster tokens. The cost is a small loss of accuracy that grows as you drop bits.
