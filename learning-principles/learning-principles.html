Learning principles: study to use it, not to store it

Also called: the Study Mode playbook. It's a synthesis of Justin Sung's approach to learning (iCanStudy), adapted for deep technical topics. Skim it before a course, and come back when a study step feels pointless. The ideas are credited to him where they're his; the wording and technical examples are mine.

For every detail, ask: "Does knowing this change my ability to explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug?" If yes, learn it. If no, reference it.

Remember the mechanisms that explain behaviour. Keep as reference the details that merely implement them.

Notes are not the goal. Don't optimise for capturing information. Optimise for being able to explain, reason about, compare and use it later. Copy-paste is fine for reference material, but copied text isn't learning. When a copied detail matters, add one line of your own: Why this matters: "the backend uses an FSM, which makes transitions cheap but limits which grammars it can represent."

The loop every card walks you through

Prime→Learn→Encode→Compress→Retrieve→Compare→Predict→Apply→Reflect

Each card opens with a Prime block (the problem, where it fits, what you'll meet, and questions to read with). It ends with a Study this card stepper, where each step appears only once you've opened the one before. For a tutor that grades your answers, run /study <course>/<card> in Claude Code.

Eleven principles

1Prime before you study

Do

Spend 5–10 minutes per hour of study getting the gist: what problem this solves, the few big ideas, and roughly how they relate. Guess how they connect before you read.

Don't

Pre-read the details ("pre-study"). If priming runs past ~10 minutes, you've slid into studying. Don't try to understand everything yet.

Technical example

Before FlashAttention: "attention is memory-bound → the win must come from moving less data → expect tiling and recomputation." Now every detail has a hook to hang on.

On the cards: Prime block · questions to read with

2Encode relationships, not transcripts

Do

Read a coherent claim, pause, decide what matters and why, and then write the smallest note that captures the relationship. Group ideas, draw arrows for cause → effect, and make the backbone visually heavier.

Don't

Copy sentence by sentence. Long notes feel productive (the "illusion of learning") and delay the actual thinking. Don't let a tool draw your links for you; deciding each link is where the learning happens.

Technical example

Not "KV cache stores K and V". Instead: "decode re-reads every past K,V each step → cache them → memory grows with context × batch → that memory, not FLOPs, caps batch size."

On the cards: while you read

3Hunt for mechanisms, causes and tradeoffs

Do

For each idea, ask why it exists, what would break without it, how the mechanism produces the behaviour, and which design choice causes its strengths and its limits. This is higher-order learning: analyse and evaluate, not just understand.

Don't

Stop at "understand". Understanding a definition is still lower-order, because the fact stays isolated. A name and a description are not a mental model.

Technical example

BatchNorm: "normalise with batch statistics → activations stay in tanh's live range → deeper nets train → but each example now depends on its batch → inference needs running stats, and tiny batches break it."

On the cards: Core model

4Compress into your own model

Do

After reading, rebuild the topic in 3–5 sentences, or as a small causal diagram, from memory. Chunk: fold five cases into the one pattern that generates them. Deliberately cut words.

Don't

Build a mini-textbook. A long note is a sign you haven't found the pattern yet.

Technical example

Five decoding tricks (greedy, temperature, top-k, top-p, min-p) → "all reshape one distribution before sampling: flatten or sharpen it (temperature), or cut its tail (the rest)."

On the cards: Compress

5Retrieve without the source

Do

Close the card and answer structured questions: explain it, walk through the mechanism, choose between options. Match the format of the real task: for interviews, speak or write whole explanations. Check only after a full attempt.

Don't

Re-read and call it review; recognising text is not recalling it. Don't lean on trivia flashcards ("what does FSM stand for"). Flashcards are a last resort, for arbitrary details only.

Technical example

Bad: "What is a PDA?" Better: "Why can a grammar engine accept nested JSON that a regex engine can't, and what does it pay per token for that?"

On the cards: Retrieve

6Find the gaps, then repair just those

Do

Treat confusion as a signal. Turn "this feels fuzzy" into a specific question (the "confusion compass"). Reopen only the section that answers it, then retry the question you missed.

Don't

Re-read the whole source because one thing was shaky. Don't skip answers you got right by luck; low confidence is a gap too.

Technical example

You can say that attention is scaled by √d but not why. Repair question: "what happens to the softmax if logits have variance d?" Reread one paragraph, retry.

On the cards: Retrieve checks · Reflect

7Interleave and compare close neighbours

Do

Once you understand each idea on its own, study close variations side by side: what's fundamentally different, which design choice causes the difference, when you'd pick each, and how each fails. Pick neighbours that are close, not trivially different.

Don't

Block-study one topic to "mastery" and never meet it again. Don't compare things that are too far apart to be confusable.

Technical example

FSM vs PDA · regex vs CFG · speculative vs plain autoregressive decoding · PPO vs GRPO (the group mean replaces a learned critic as the baseline).

On the cards: Compare · checkpoint cards

8Predict before you see the answer

Do

Reason generatively: "if my model is right, then X must happen." Commit to a prediction, then check it. A wrong prediction marks exactly where your model is broken.

Don't

Peek, then tell yourself you would have guessed it.

Technical example

"If a constrained-decoding backend swaps its FSM for a stack-based PDA, which new class of structures becomes representable, and what gets slower?" Predict, then read.

On the cards: Predict first · Predict

9Apply it: build, trace, break, debug

Do

Use the model on something concrete soon after learning it: implement a tiny version, trace an algorithm by hand, modify working code, debug a failure, benchmark two variants, or answer a design scenario.

Don't

Watch tutorials without doing anything, which gives knowledge you can't use. Don't save all the practice for the end of the course.

Technical example

Hand-trace a 3-state FSM over four generated tokens and write the logits mask at each step. Then break it with nested brackets and explain why it breaks.

On the cards: Build it · Apply

10Reflect on where your model failed

Do

At the end of a topic, ask what surprised you, what you couldn't explain cleanly, which prediction was wrong and which relationship is still fuzzy. Also ask what to change about how you studied.

Don't

Finish a card and move on without deciding what to revisit.

Technical example

"I could state the FSM limit but not why it's architectural: finite states can't count unbounded depth. Revisit cfg-pda before the grammar-engine comparison."

On the cards: Reflect

11Separate the core model from reference

Do

Ask of every detail: does knowing this change my ability to explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug? If yes, learn it. If no, keep it as reference. Promote an implementation detail when it explains behaviour, performance or a limit.

Don't

Memorise APIs, constants, class names or memory layouts "just in case". Don't drop an implementation detail when it is the reason for a capability.

Technical example

Learn deeply: FSM vs PDA (it explains capability). Understand moderately: "token prefixes live in a trie" (it explains speed). Reference only: FooTokenCache._transition_lookup.

On the cards: Core model: must vs reference

Four layers: what to learn, and what to look up

Sung sorts knowledge into four layers. Build them top-down: the logic first, the arbitrary details last, if ever.

LayerWhat it isOn the cardsConstrained-decoding example
1 · LogicThe big picture: why it exists and how it flowsPrime · CompressEvery step, only some tokens are legal; mask the rest before sampling
2 · ConceptsThe mechanisms and their causal linksCore model: understand and rememberConstraint → state → allowed tokens → logits mask → sample
3 · Important detailsDetails that explain behaviour, speed or limits"Detail that matters"Precomputed state→token index → masking is a lookup, not a vocabulary scan
4 · Arbitrary detailsNames, flags, layouts: look them upReference onlyThe index's internal format, cache-key layout, library function names

Reading it all in order just moves information around.

After: a study path

Prime: constrained decoding must decide which tokens are legal at every step; an FSM is one way to hold that state. Watch grammar expressiveness → state representation → token masking → speed.

Core: constraint → FSM state → allowed transitions → allowed tokens → logits mask → sampling. Finite state → no unbounded stack → no arbitrary nesting → CFG/PDA engines express what FSMs can't.

Retrieve: "Walk through what happens between the model producing logits and sampling the next token." Compare: FSM vs PDA. Predict: "Which JSON shape first exposes a pure FSM's limit?"

Apply: build a tiny grammar, track the state over several tokens, and list the masked tokens at each step. Reflect: "Can I say why the limit is architectural, not just that FSMs are less expressive?"

See it on the cards: Regex and finite state machines · Grammars and pushdown automata

What these principles are not. They aren't anti-flashcard dogma: spaced retrieval still matters, and Sung keeps flashcards for layer-4 details that connect to nothing. They also aren't "never take notes": notes should be small, relational, in your own words, and written after you've decided what matters. And slower-feeling study is expected. Interleaving and generating answers feel harder than re-reading, and that difficulty is the point.
