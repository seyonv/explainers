FlashAttention-3, -4 and FlashInfer

Also called FA3 (the Hopper/H100 version), FA4 (the Blackwell/B200 version), warp specialization, pingpong scheduling, and the FlashInfer kernel library. A skim card: one section per source (FA3, arXiv 2407.08608; FA4, MLSys; FlashInfer, README and arXiv 2501.01005), each asking what changed and why the hardware forced it. No lab: there is nothing here a CPU can measure.

In one breath

The FlashAttention algorithm (tiles, online softmax, never writing the N×N matrix to HBM) hasn't changed since FA2. What changes each GPU generation is the kernel. Each new chip makes matmul much faster but leaves the exponential unit and shared memory almost where they were. So FA3 and FA4 are mostly about keeping the tensor cores busy while the slow units catch up. FlashInfer is the library serving engines use to get these kernels, plus the ones serving needs (paged KV, decode, shared prefixes), behind one API.

1. FlashAttention-3: Hopper made data movement and matmul asynchronous

What the H100 forced: FA2 reaches only 35% of the H100 (vs 80–90% for GEMMs) because it uses Ampere-style synchronous instructions. Hopper's speed lives in two asynchronous units, TMA (tile copies HBM → SMEM) and WGMMA (a warpgroup-wide tensor-core matmul), and in a 256× gap between matmul and exp.

Producer–consumer warp specialization. One "producer" warpgroup only issues TMA loads of Kj, Vj into an s-stage circular SMEM buffer and gives most of its registers away (setmaxnreg). The "consumer" warpgroups only do WGMMA and softmax, releasing each buffer stage when done. Loads never wait on math.

Pingpong scheduling. With two consumer warpgroups, barriers force warpgroup 1's two GEMMs to run before warpgroup 2's, so each one's softmax (on the exp unit) runs while the other's GEMMs hold the tensor cores. The paper: "from 570 TFLOPS to 620-640 TFLOPS" (FP16 forward, d = 128, N = 8,192), and "not as clean as depicted".

Intra-warpgroup 2-stage pipeline. Inside one warpgroup, issue the next block's Snext = QiKjT (without waiting) before the softmax of the current block, so GEMM and softmax overlap across loop iterations, at the cost of extra register buffers.

FP8. Block quantization (one scale per Br×d or Bc×d block, not per tensor) and incoherent processing: multiply Q and K by M = random ±1 diagonal × Hadamard, so (QM)(KM)T = QKT but outliers get spread out. Cost O(d log d), fused with rotary.

2. FlashAttention-4: Blackwell doubled matmul and nothing else

What the B200 forced: "asymmetric scaling". Tensor cores go from 4,096 to 8,192 ops/clk/SM, but the exp unit (16 ops/clk/SM) and SMEM reads (128 B/clk/SM) stay the same, so "shared memory traffic and exponential operations now dominate execution time, exceeding MMA compute by 25-60%" on typical workloads. Hopper's MMA instructions also aren't forward compatible, which is why FA3 "does not run on B200".

From FA4 Table 1 (we checked the three formulas give these values). At M = 256 the numbers are 2,048 / 2,048 / 1,536. The exp unit is as slow as the whole matmul, so the kernel only reaches matmul speed if the two overlap almost perfectly.

Fully asynchronous MMA pipeline. Blackwell MMAs write their output straight to TMEM (256 KB per SM of tensor memory) on 128×128 tiles, not to registers. FA4 ping-pongs two 128-row Q tiles through two softmax warpgroups, like FA3, and because P goes through TMEM it can move output rescaling into a separate correction warpgroup, off the critical path.

Software exp on the FMA units. Compute 2x as 2⌊x⌋ × a degree-3 polynomial for the fraction (Cody–Waite range reduction, Horner's method on FMAs), for 10–25% of each softmax row; the rest still goes to hardware MUFU.EX2. After rounding to BF16 it matches the hardware to within 1 ULP on 99% of inputs.

Conditional rescaling. Online softmax multiplies O by emj−1−mj whenever the running max rises. FA4 only rescales when the max jumps by more than τ = log2(256) = 8, and tracks the skipped scaling so the final division is still exact (see online softmax).

Backward: 2-CTA MMA. Two CTAs share one MMA, each staging half of operand B, which cuts SMEM traffic and halves the dQ atomic adds. There's also a deterministic mode.

Results on B200, BF16: up to 1.3× faster than cuDNN 9.13 and 2.7× faster than Triton, up to 1,613 TF (71%). Written in CuTe-DSL (Python): one forward kernel compiles in 2.5 s instead of FA3's 55 s (22×), backward 1.4 s vs 45 s. Venue: MLSys. The PDF footer says 2025 ("8th MLSys Conference"), the proceedings URL says 2026.

3. FlashInfer: the serving-side packaging

What serving forced: an engine doesn't run one long prefill. It runs a batch that mixes prefills and decodes of different lengths, with KV cache scattered across pages, some of it shared, and it wants the whole step captured in a CUDA graph. FA2/3/4 alone don't handle all of that.

What it is: a "library and kernel generator for inference" with one API for attention, GEMM and MoE, and several backends underneath (FlashAttention-2/3, cuDNN, CUTLASS, TensorRT-LLM). The paper uses the FA2 algorithm up to Ada (sm89) and FA3 on Hopper. The README lists SM75 (Turing, the T4) through Blackwell, FP8 and FP4, and CUDA graph and torch.compile support. Used by SGLang, vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, TGI and MLC-LLM.

Attention kernels: paged and ragged KV cache; decode, prefill and append kernels; MLA kernels; cascade attention (a shared prefix's KV stored once and attended to with bigger blocks; the paper calls this "composable formats", the same idea as RadixAttention's shared prefixes); block-sparse; POD-attention (prefill and decode fused into one kernel).

Paper ideas (arXiv 2501.01005): one block-sparse format for every KV layout (a page table is a block-sparse matrix); JIT-compiled attention templates so a new variant is a few lines of CUDA; a load-balanced scheduler inspired by Stream-K that splits long KVs into chunks across CTAs and merges the partials in a fixed order (no atomics, so it's deterministic), planned on the CPU each step so the CUDA graph stays the same.

Paper results (FlashInfer 0.2 inside SGLang v0.3.4 vs SGLang's Triton backend; one setup is Llama 3.1 8B Instruct on 1×H100, our running example): 29–69% lower inter-token latency than compiler backends, 28–30% lower latency for long-context inference, 13–17% faster parallel generation.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ FA2 on Hopper

Correct and portable, but written for Ampere: no TMA, no WGMMA, softmax and matmul take turns. 335 TF, about 35% of the H100; our prefill example takes 52.5 ms at that rate.

✗ FA3 (the winner on H100)

740 TF (75%) and ~1.2 PF FP8 by overlapping loads, GEMMs and exp. But it's built on Hopper instructions: it does not run on a B200, and its C++ templates take ~55 s per kernel to compile.

✗ FA4 on its own (the winner on B200)

1,613 TF (71%) on B200 by emulating exp and skipping rescales. But it's a training-shaped prefill/backward kernel: it doesn't give you paged decode, shared prefixes or a CUDA-graph-friendly scheduler, and it's Blackwell-only.

✓ FlashInfer (what engines call)

A library that picks FA2/FA3/cuDNN/CUTLASS/TRT-LLM kernels per GPU and adds what serving needs: paged and ragged KV, decode, MLA, cascade, POD, FP8/FP4, a deterministic scheduler. From T4 to Blackwell, in SGLang, vLLM and TensorRT-LLM.

Why is the exponential suddenly the bottleneck?

Because matmul throughput grew much faster than the special-function units. FA2's lesson on the A100 was "non-matmul FLOPs are 16× more expensive, so do fewer of them". On the H100 the exp unit alone is 256× slower than the tensor cores, and on the B200 512×. At d = 128 a tile does only 512 matmul FLOPs per exponential, so on a B200 the exps take as long as the matmuls (our 7.8 ms vs 7.8 ms). The lesson gets sharper each generation; it didn't change.

When this is not what matters: in decode, attention reads the whole KV cache for one query token and does about 4 FLOPs per byte (perf-2 attention intensity), so it's memory-bound and neither the exp unit nor FA4's pipelines are the limit: page layout, KV bytes (GQA, MLA, FP8 KV) and batching are. Larger head dimensions also dilute the exp cost (4d FLOPs per exp), which is why the exp problem is worst at d = 64–128. And at 11.8% of the FLOPs in our 8k prefill, even a perfect attention kernel speeds up the whole prefill by at most about 12%. Attention's share grows with N.
