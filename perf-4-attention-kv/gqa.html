Before this: Tricks that shrink the KV cache (perf-2)

Grouped-query attention

Also called GQA or GQA-G, with multi-query attention (MQA) as the one-group case; "uptraining" is the paper's recipe for converting an existing checkpoint, and "KV heads" is what gets grouped. This card walks the whole GQA paper (Ainslie et al., Google, EMNLP 2023) section by section. The paper's experiments are T5 encoder-decoders timed on TPUv4. The running example, Llama-3.1-8B on one H100, has 32 query heads in 8 KV groups, 4 query heads per group.

In one breath

Split the query heads into G groups and give each group one shared key head and one shared value head. The KV cache, and the bytes every decode step must read, shrink by H/G. G = H is ordinary multi-head attention and G = 1 is multi-query attention. The paper also shows you can convert an existing multi-head model by averaging its K and V heads within each group and then training for about 5% of the original steps.

The paper, section by section

§1 Introduction: why bother

Decoding is slow because every step loads the decoder weights and "all attention keys and values". MQA cuts the key/value loads sharply, but "can lead to quality degradation and training instability", and training a separate fast model may not be feasible. Two contributions: (1) uptrain existing MHA checkpoints into MQA with about 5% of the original pre-training compute; (2) GQA, an interpolation between the two.

§2.1 Uptraining

Two steps: convert the checkpoint (mean-pool the K and V projection matrices), then continue pre-training for a proportion α of the original steps on the same recipe. Mean-pooling "works better than selecting a single key and value head or randomly initializing new key and value heads from scratch". For α = 0.05, uptraining took about 600 TPUv3 chip-days.

§2.2 Grouped-query attention

GQA-1 = MQA, GQA-H = MHA; the conversion mean-pools "all the original heads within that group". Going from MHA to MQA cuts the KV cache, and the data loaded, by a factor of H. Three arguments for an intermediate G: bigger models have more heads, so MQA becomes an ever more aggressive cut, while GQA keeps the same proportional cut; the KV cache grows with model dimension while FLOPs and parameters grow with its square, so bigger models suffer less from KV bandwidth anyway; and standard sharding replicates a single KV head across partitions, which GQA avoids. GQA is not applied to encoder self-attention, which runs in parallel and isn't bandwidth-bound; it is applied to decoder self-attention and cross-attention.

§4 Related work, §5 conclusion

Shazeer (2019) proposed MQA; Rabe (2023) independently implemented GQA. The paper lists FlashAttention, quantization, distillation, layer-sparse cross-attention and speculative sampling as other attacks on the same bandwidth overhead. Uptraining is borrowed from sparse upcycling (Komatsuzaki et al. 2022). Conclusion: uptrained GQA gets "quality close to multi-head at comparable speed to multi-query".

Limitations and Appendix A

Long-output quality is hard to evaluate and ROUGE is "a flawed evaluation". There is no comparison against a GQA-XXL trained from scratch, so uptraining vs training from scratch is unknown. Only encoder-decoder models were tested; for decoder-only models they "expect GQA to have a stronger advantage over MQA". Appendix A: T5-Large MQA models trained from scratch had "frequent loss spikes" and diverged immediately on long-input fine-tuning. Uptrained MQA was more stable but high-variance (averaged over 3 runs). Uptrained GQA "appear[s] to be stable".

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ MHA (GQA-H)

Best quality, but the largest cache: 512 KiB per token for a Llama-3.1-8B shape, so only 14 sequences of 8k fit on an H100. In the paper, T5-XXL MHA takes 1.51 s per sample vs 0.28 for GQA-8.

✗ MQA (GQA-1)

Smallest cache and fastest (0.24 s), but −0.6 average vs MHA-XXL even after uptraining, useless without uptraining (53.93 at α = 0), and loss spikes when trained from scratch (App A).

✗ MLA (DeepSeek)

An even smaller cache through a different mechanism: a compressed latent per token, not shared heads. It's a training-time architecture with its own RoPE workaround, not a mean-pool conversion of an existing checkpoint. See multi-head latent attention.

✓ GQA-8

Within 0.1 points of MHA-XXL at 5.4× its speed, only 17% slower than MQA, stable, and reachable from an existing MHA checkpoint with 5% extra training. On Llama-3.1-8B, 8 groups make the cache 4× smaller than MHA.

Can you convert an existing MHA model to GQA for free?

The conversion is free; the quality isn't. Mean-pooling the K and V heads is a few array operations. But in Fig 5 the converted-only (α = 0) GQA-8 T5-XXL scores 56.71 vs MHA's 57.54, and MQA drops to 53.93. The paper's recipe is conversion plus about 5% of the original pre-training steps: about 600 TPUv3 chip-days for T5-XXL. That brings GQA to 57.40.

  • Not tested: decoder-only models, and uptraining vs training GQA from scratch. The paper says so itself. Llama-3.1-8B simply ships with 8 KV heads, so a serving team doesn't choose G; the lab did.
  • Where GQA doesn't help: encoder self-attention (the paper skips it), and short-context, small-batch decode, where weight reads dominate and the KV cut barely moves the step (see attention intensity).
  • What people use instead or on top: a serving team that can't retrain shrinks the cache post hoc with FP8 or 2-bit KV. A lab designing a new model may pick MLA, whose cache DeepSeek-V2 puts at the size of GQA with just 2.25 groups while reporting it stronger than MHA.

Kernels need nothing special: FlashAttention-2 handles MQA/GQA by indexing each query head to its KV head, then sums dK and dV over the heads that shared them in the backward pass.
