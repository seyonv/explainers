Before this: FlashAttention I: tiling and recomputation

FlashAttention II: the IO-complexity argument

Also called HBM accesses, the Θ(N²d²/M) bound, IO-awareness, and block-sparse FlashAttention. This card is FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) §3.2: Theorem 2, Proposition 3 and Fig 2. It also covers §3.3 (block-sparse attention, Proposition 4) and the Appendix C proofs. The paper's numbers are for GPT-2 medium on an A100. The byte counts, the H100 figures and the Llama-3.1-8B figures are our recomputation.

In one breath

Attention's speed is set by how many elements cross between HBM and on-chip SRAM, not by how many FLOPs it does. Standard attention writes and re-reads the N×N score matrix, so it makes Θ(Nd + N²) HBM accesses. FlashAttention keeps K and V blocks in SRAM and streams Q past them. That takes Θ(N²d²/M) accesses, where M is the SRAM size. Because d² (4,096 for d = 64) is many times smaller than M (about 100K), this is many times less traffic. On GPT-2 medium it measured 9.2× less HBM traffic and ran 5.7× faster, even though it did 13% more FLOPs.

Where the counts come from, loop by loop

Red is the N×N matrix that must not be materialised. Green is the K, V block held on chip. Grey is Q and O, which are streamed from HBM once per outer-loop pass. The paper's own words: "given the SRAM size of M, we can load blocks of K, V of size Θ(M) each … resulting in Θ(NdM⁻¹) passes over Q. Each pass loads Θ(Nd) elements."

Appendix C: why Tc = Θ(Nd/M). Three things must fit in SRAM: the Kj, Vj blocks (Bc·d = O(M)), the Qi, Oi blocks (Br·d = O(M)), and the score tile Sij (Br·Bc = O(M)). So Bc = Θ(M/d) and Br = Θ(min(M/d, d)). Alg 1 uses Bc = ⌈M/4d⌉ and Br = min(⌈M/4d⌉, d). Then Tc = N/Bc = Θ(Nd/M), and the total is Θ(Nd·Tc) = Θ(N²d²/M). The backward pass has the same two counts (Appendix B, Theorem 5).

Fill it in, and watch the units

M has to be in elements. You can see this in Alg 1, which sets Bc = ⌈M/4d⌉: four blocks of Bc × d elements have to fit. With bf16 or fp16, 2 bytes per element, a 192 KB SRAM holds 98,304 elements. If you plug in the byte count by mistake, every ratio doubles. Byte counts (elements × 2) give the same ratio as element counts, because both sides scale by 2.

Proposition 3: a lower bound, and what it doesn't say

Claim: no algorithm that computes exact attention can make o(N²d²/M) HBM accesses for all M in [d, Nd]. Proof: at the top of that range, M = Θ(Nd), the bound becomes Ω(N²d²/Nd) = Ω(Nd). Any algorithm has to read Q, K, V and write O, which already costs Ω(Nd) accesses. So beating N²d²/M everywhere would contradict this. The paper notes that "this type of lower bound over a subrange of M is common in the streaming algorithms literature."

What it doesn't claim. It does not prove that FlashAttention is optimal at the M you actually have, such as 100 KB. The argument uses only the M = Θ(Nd) endpoint, where the whole problem fits on chip. A lower bound parameterised by M is "left as exciting future work." It is also a statement up to constants, so it doesn't rule out an exact algorithm with a smaller constant. FlashAttention-2 later cuts non-matmul work, not the Θ.

The paper's FlashAttention does 13% more FLOPs because the backward pass recomputes S and P. It still runs 5.7× faster, because it moves 9.2× fewer bytes. Our count gives 5.57× less traffic, not 9.2×. It covers the forward pass only and counts only Alg 0's three lines, while the measurement covers forward + backward and PyTorch's separate mask, softmax and dropout kernels, each of which also reads and writes N² elements. One quirk we can't reconcile: from the stated shapes, the forward matmuls alone come to 4N²d × 1,024 = 274.9 GFLOP, more than the 66.6 in the figure. The paper doesn't say how it counted.

Our framing: tiling raises attention's arithmetic intensity. The attention-intensity card models a fused Flash kernel that reads Q, K, V once, which gives N/2 = 4,096 FLOP/B, far above the H100's 295. That is the M = Θ(Nd) end of Proposition 3. Alg 1 taken literally, with a 228 KB SRAM, no L2 cache and d = 128, needs 36 passes and gets only 146, which is still below the ridge. Real kernels land much closer to the ideal. FlashAttention-2 swaps the loops, so Q and O are touched once and K, V are re-read. The H100's 50 MB L2 absorbs most repeat reads. Under GQA the 4 query heads that share a KV head can share its loads. This is our inference: Theorem 2 counts HBM traffic under a two-level model (HBM and SRAM), with no L2.

  • Scaling: with s = N−1/2 or N−1log N, as in earlier sparse-attention work, IO becomes Θ(N√N) or Θ(N log N).
  • Pattern: the experiments use a fixed butterfly sparsity pattern, which has "been shown to be able to approximate arbitrary sparsity."
  • Fig 2 right: at sequence length 4K, runtime falls in proportion to sparsity. The figure gives no per-point values.
  • LRA: block-sparse FlashAttention runs 2.8× faster than standard attention (FlashAttention: 2.4×). Average accuracy is 59.6 vs 59.3 for the standard Transformer (Table 3).

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Standard attention

Θ(Nd + N²) accesses. S and P each make a full N×N round trip through HBM. That's 134 MB per head at N = 8k, and it dominates as soon as N ≫ d. GPT-2 medium: 40.3 GB, 41.7 ms.

✗ Bigger SRAM

More M means bigger blocks and fewer passes: M/d² goes from 24 (A100) to 28.5 (H100) at d = 64. That's a 1.19× gain from 192 → 228 KB. It helps, but the hardware grows slowly, and d = 128 still loses 4× to d².

✗ Block-sparse

Traffic is cut by s on top of FlashAttention (Θ(Nd + N²d²s/M)), and it's 2.8× on LRA. But it is approximate: it skips blocks, so the output isn't softmax(QKᵀ)V, and it needs a mask that suits the task.

✓ FlashAttention

Exact output with Θ(N²d²/M) accesses. It moves 9.2× less and runs 5.7× faster on GPT-2 medium despite 13% more FLOPs. By Proposition 3, no exact method beats it for every SRAM size.

Is FlashAttention memory-bound or compute-bound?

It depends on the block size and the phase. For training and prefill, the paper's Fig 2 (middle) shows the forward pass stops getting faster beyond a block size of ~256, where it is "bottlenecked by other factors (e.g., arithmetic operations)." So with large enough blocks it becomes compute-bound (arithmetic-bound). With small blocks it is still memory-bound: our count shows that Bc = 32 moves more bytes than standard attention.

Decode is different. This part is our framing, not the paper's. Generating one token means one query row (N = 1 for Q) against the whole KV cache. There is no N×N matrix to avoid, and FlashAttention can't help reading the entire cache: 1.07 GB per 8k-token Llama-3.1-8B sequence. Intensity stays around 1 FLOP/byte (≈ 4 with GQA), far below the ridge. See attention intensity. Decode attention is memory-bound with or without FlashAttention. Engines instead shrink or share the cache (GQA, MLA, FP8 KV, paging, prefix caching), which later cards in this course cover.

When the Θ bound misleads. At d = 128 the headroom M/d² is only ~7, and our Alg 1 constants leave about 2.3×. Short sequences (N = 256: 3.3×) gain less, because the Nd terms matter there. The two-level model also ignores L2 cache, which is why measured kernels beat the literal count.
