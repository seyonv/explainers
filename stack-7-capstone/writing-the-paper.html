Write it against the evidence

Step 7 of the capstone, also called drafting the paper. You fix the outline from the series spec, write related work one verified neighbour at a time, list the three figures, and state the limitations a reviewer will look for before they look. Most of it can be written while the sweep runs; only the results and the abstract wait for results/main.md.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A draft written from memory and enthusiasm drifts from the data: numbers get rounded the hopeful way, an exploratory contrast becomes the headline, related work is a list of summaries, and the limitations a reviewer will find are missing. Each of these is a reason to reject an otherwise sound study.

Where it fits: Step 7 of the capstone. It takes the related-work table from How the question got sharp, Figure 1 from Lab: audit 21,000 public runs, and the confirmatory/exploratory split from Analyse what you registered, and turns them into sections.

In one breath

A paper is a chain of claims, and each claim should point at one piece of evidence: a table in pilot/results.md, a line in results/main.md, a commit. Write the parts whose evidence exists now (setup, related work, the pilot, the limitations), and write the results last, from the registered analysis, in the order the pre-registration names: confirmatory first, then secondary, then exploratory. The paper says what the data supports and states plainly what it can't.

Step 5 · Draft the results last

Pending: filled when the sweep completes. Section 5.2–5.4 numbers (H1a, H1b, H2, families T, S, M, the mechanism and per-model tables), Figures 2 and 3, Table 2's exclusions, the sweep's cost line in the appendix, and the abstract's verdict sentence. Source: results/main.md.

Order the results as the analysis was registered: confirmatory, then secondary with their family corrections, then a clearly titled exploratory section. Report every null with its interval and the smallest effect the sweep could have detected. The title follows the result: the spec's candidates ("Done, Tests Pass": Where Coding Agents' False Success Claims Come From, and How Compaction Manufactures Them, or Untested, Mistaken, or Forgotten?) assume an effect on claims; if H2 holds and H1 doesn't, the headline is about what compaction does to tests, not to claims.

Done when
  • every number in the draft has a file and line (or commit) next to it in a comment, and none comes from memory;
  • related work has a difference sentence for every paper in step 2, and the novelty re-check is dated within a week of submission;
  • all nine limitations are in section 7, and the abstract says which hypotheses were confirmatory and whether they held.

Why writing against the evidence beats the alternatives

✗ Story first, numbers later

A draft written around "compaction makes agents lie" before the sweep pulls you toward the exploratory contrast that fits. The pilot already showed that story is probably wrong.

✗ Related work as summaries

Ten paragraphs of "X did Y" leave the reviewer to find the gap. With OverclaimBench and Silence Is Endorsement this close, they'll decide it isn't there.

✗ One hedging limitations paragraph

"Future work could use more models" hides the specific problems (the Codex prompt isn't what Codex runs with OpenAI models) that a careful reader will find anyway.

✓ Claim, evidence, difference

Each section points at a file, each neighbour gets one sentence of difference, and each limitation cites where it's recorded. The results slot in from the registered analysis.

Honest caveat. The outline assumes the sweep runs to its registered n. If the $120 cap or the first-chunk projection stops it early, section 5 reports a truncated sweep with its achieved n and smallest detectable effect, as §6 of the pre-registration requires. And the decomposition half rests on one annotator's reading of 49 audited cases; until a second annotator and the re-run in official SWE-bench images exist (both required by the spec before submission), its 0.17% is a lower bound with a ceiling near 0.4%, and the paper should say so.

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Analyse what you registered: The analysis card separates confirmatory from exploratory results. How does that separation show up in the written paper, and where is it easiest to blur?

Check
  • separate results subsections, and the abstract names which hypotheses were confirmatory
  • easiest to blur in the title, the abstract and the discussion, where a striking per-model or mechanism result is tempting
  • the registered rule (p < 0.025 on pooled H2) decides what can be claimed

vs Penalize the monitor, teach the hiding: The discussion section will say what the result means for training on claim checkers. What does that card warn about, and how should the paper phrase it?

Check
  • penalizing what a monitor detects can teach the model to hide the behaviour instead of dropping it
  • a claim checker used as a reward could make claims vaguer or tampering quieter
  • phrase it as a risk the paper's evidence motivates, not as something it measured

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

A reviewer writes: 'The Codex arm doesn't reflect how Codex behaves in practice.' Before reading the reply, predict whether that sinks the prompt comparison, and what the paper should already contain to answer it.

Reveal

It doesn't sink it, but it narrows it. The paper should already say, in limitations, that Codex with an OpenAI model compacts server-side and that the arm is the published prompt Codex uses with other providers (PROVENANCE.md). The comparison then reads as 'two published compaction prompts, plus a truncation control', which is still what harness builders can act on; the claim about Codex the product has to go.
