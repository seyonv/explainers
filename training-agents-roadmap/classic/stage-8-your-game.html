Stage 8 · Capstone · the deliverable

Teach an agent a game nobody has trained on

Design a small original game, beat it with classical RL first, then turn it into an environment and train a small LLM to play it with GRPO. You finish with a Hub Space, a model and a result you can defend.

By the end of this stage you can
  • Design a game that makes a good RL environment, and defend each design choice against the GeoGuessr article's criteria.
  • Beat it with tabular Q-learning, and explain exactly why that success doesn't transfer to a new lock.
  • Measure an LLM baseline with repeats and error bars, and tune the difficulty so groups have mixed rewards.
  • Train Qwen3 with GRPO against your environment, and show a held-out gain larger than its error bar.
  • Publish the environment as a Space and write up what the model learned, including any hack you found.

Prime 5–10 min, before anything else

Why both kinds of RL? You asked whether to apply RL directly or with an LLM in the loop. Do both, in that order. Classical RL (a Q-table, then PPO) takes an afternoon on your Mac. It makes the RL day concrete and gives you a real baseline. The LLM version is the actual skill the series teaches: a policy that reads a new lock's wiring from text and plans. The comparison is the most interesting part of your write-up. Q-learning needs thousands of episodes to master one wiring; the LLM has to generalise to wirings it has never seen.

Predict before you start, and write it down: after GRPO, will Qwen3-1.7B beat the greedy baseline (73%)? What will it learn first?

Primer

Read these in order. Each block is short on purpose: the diagram and the “best first” link carry the idea; “Go deeper” is only for when a block doesn't land.

  1. What makes a game a good RL environment

    ~1.5 h

Adithya Kolavi's GeoGuessr article is the playbook for this stage. It's the story of turning a game into an OpenEnv environment and training Qwen3.5-4B on it, with the dead ends left in. Its criteria for an environment idea: a continuous, verifiable reward; multi-turn play; not saturated (strong models don't already ace it); and cheap to simulate on a CPU. Then: never leak the answer through feedback. Its pin tool never says how close a guess is, or the best policy would just be binary search.

Its war stories are exactly what you'll hit. The first reward scored 77 of 200 episodes at exactly zero, which means zero GRPO advantage, so they added a second, slower decay. The trained model learned to stop exploring: turns fell from 6.7 to 1.1 and it committed from the first frame. That came from the reward's flat region, not a bug. Before training they ran a concurrency test, a no-gradient rollout simulation (which found three bugs) and an overfit-4-tasks run, and watched frac_reward_zero_std. Result: 0.4825 → 0.6445 on 200 held-out tasks, 2nd of 11 models. It cost about $100 on 4×A100s, and it also runs on one.

FineEnvs's six-step recipe, in short: in-process or HTTP? single- or multi-turn? where does the reward come from? stateful tools? build the smallest version first; validate with a rollout, not a training run.

  1. The game: Tumblers

    30 min

A lock has 3 dials (0–9). Turning a dial ±1 also turns every dial wired to it by the same amount, mod 10. Each lock has a random start, a random target and random wiring. The agent sees the code, the target, the wiring and the moves left, and has one tool, turn(dial, direction). It gets 2 × the optimal number of moves. Breadth-first search solves any lock exactly, so you always know the optimal move count, and you can reject locks that are too easy.

Why it's a good environment: the reward is checkable (solved or not, plus a graded variant: 1 − distance/max), it's multi-turn (7 moves on median), and difficulty has knobs (dials, links, minimum optimal length). It can't be ground out: a greedy player who always moves toward the target digits wins 73%, and the wiring traps the other 27%. A random policy wins 0%. That gap between greedy and optimal is where learning has to happen. With 4 dials and 3 links, greedy falls to 52%.

One insight you'll earn in phase B: with the wiring fixed, a lock looks the same from any target if you track (code − target) mod 10. Tabular Q-learning on that state reaches 100% on fresh locks after about 5,000 episodes (29% after 1,000). Test the same table on a different wiring and it falls to 4–49%, often below greedy. The LLM gets a new wiring every episode and must read it.

You know it when you can explain why the difference state makes Q-learning generalise across targets but not across wirings.

Phase C · LLM baselines and the difficulty dial

Time ~3 hCost ~$1–3 in API or a small jobWhere Mac + hosted models

No training yet. This is the GeoGuessr article's “sweep the field before you train anything” chapter, and the no-gradient rollout that finds your bugs.

  1. Write the TRL environment class: reset(**row) + a turn tool with a typed docstringModel it on TRL's grpo_2048.py↗: an in-process pure-Python game, so no server is needed yet. Store self.reward for the reward function to read. The dataset row carries the lock's seed.You should see one class of about 60 lines.
  2. Roll out 3 models × 50 held-out locks × 4 tries each: a strong hosted model, Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-0.6BUse vLLM on a small Job (or a hosted endpoint) for the Qwen models. Report pass@1 with an error bar, and the share of locks where the 4 tries disagree (mixed groups).You should see the strong model near the top, Qwen3-0.6B near the floor. Pick the model whose mixed-group share is highest: that's where GRPO has signal.
  3. Turn the difficulty dial until about half the groups are mixedKnobs: minimum optimal length, number of links, move budget. The GeoGuessr lesson: a reward that's flat for most episodes teaches nothing.You should see a training config where frac_reward_zero_std in a 5-step smoke run is below ~50%.
  4. Overfit 4 locks, then run a 5-step smoke testIf the model can't learn 4 fixed locks, something is broken (tool parsing, reward reading, masking). Check completions with --log_completions.You should see reward on the 4 locks climbing toward 1 within a few dozen steps.

Phase D · Train with GRPO and measure honestly

Time ~4 h (mostly waiting)Cost ~$3–6Where HF Jobs a100-large ($2.50/h)

Write train_tumblers.py by adapting Class 4's train_coding_whitebox_eval.py: swap the environment class for your Tumblers one and the dataset for lock seeds. Class 3's settings are the starting point: vLLM colocated, num_generations 8, lr around 1e-6 to 5e-6 (full model) or 1e-5 to 3e-5 (LoRA), temperature 0.7–1.0, beta 0, and a fixed held-out eval every few steps, as in Class 4.

  1. Run GRPO on 1,000–2,000 training locks with a 64-lock held-out eval curve

  -e VLLM_USE_FLASHINFER_SAMPLER=0 --with "trl[vllm]" train_tumblers.py \
  -- --model Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B --num-generations 8 --max-steps 300 \
     --trackio-space-id <you>/tumblers-trackio --push-to-hub --hub-model-id <you>/qwen3-1.7b-tumblers-grpo
    You should see eval/reward rising while train/reward is noisy, as in Class 4. Watch frac_reward_zero_std, completion length and the tool-call count.
  2. Final eval: 200 held-out locks with unseen wirings, base vs trained, same promptYou should see two win rates with 95% intervals. Pass test: the trained model's interval sits above the base model's, and you've compared it against greedy (73%).
  3. One ablation: binary vs graded reward (or 4 vs 8 generations)TRL's docs say binary beats partial credit for Wordle and Sudoku. Is that true for Tumblers?You should see a second curve on the same dashboard, and one sentence on why.
  4. Read 20 trajectories and name what it learnedDid it learn to handle wiring, or just greedy with fewer mistakes? Did it find a hack (for example, exploiting the budget or a parsing quirk)?You should see a named behaviour, with two trajectories as evidence.

Checkpoint

Closed book. Write your answer before you open each one. If you miss one, reread the block it names, not the whole stage.

  1. The observation shows both the code and the target, so closeness is always computable. What feedback must the game still never give, and why?

    Answer

    Anything that makes a trivial policy optimal, like the number of optimal moves remaining or a “warmer/colder after this move” hint. Then following the hint beats reasoning about the wiring, just as the GeoGuessr pin tool never reports distance.

    Reread: What makes a game a good RL environment

  2. Tabular Q-learning reached 100% on fresh locks. Why isn't that the end of the story?

    Answer

    It only holds for the one wiring it trained on (the difference state makes it generalise across targets, not wirings). A new wiring needs a new table; an LLM policy has to read the wiring and generalise.

    Reread: The game: Tumblers

  3. Your 5-step smoke test shows frac_reward_zero_std = 0.9. What do you do?

    Answer

    Nine in ten groups teach nothing. Make the task easier for this model (shorter optimal paths, fewer links, bigger budget), switch to a graded reward, or use more generations per prompt.

    Reread: Phase C

  4. Train reward is flat but the held-out eval curve rises. Believe it?

    Answer

    Yes, if the eval set is fixed and disjoint: per-step training reward mixes in which locks were drawn (large noise), while a fixed set removes it. That's exactly what Class 4 saw.

    Reread: Phase D
