Primer

A 10-minute map to hold while you watch and read. It isn't a substitute for either; it's there so each new idea has somewhere to land.

  1. The map: from a maze to a language model

    10 min

You need this because today's Q-table is the maze idea from this map with dials instead of squares, and the afternoon's GRPO is the method you'll train with in Stage 6.

The morning is classical RL on one running example, a grid maze. An MDP (states, actions, rewards, transitions, discount γ) describes the problem. The return is the discounted sum of rewards. Value functions V(s) and Q(s, a) predict the return. Monte Carlo learns them by waiting for the episode to end; temporal difference (TD) learns from one step and its own estimate of the next (“bootstrapping”). Q-learning is TD for Q. Policy gradients skip values and push the policy directly toward actions that did better than expected: the advantage.

The afternoon moves all of that onto an LLM. The policy is the model, one episode is one completion, and the reward comes from a reward model (RLHF) or a verifier (RLVR). REINFORCE is the plain policy gradient; PPO adds a learned critic, an importance ratio and a clip; GRPO replaces the critic with the average of a group of samples. KL divergence to a reference model keeps the policy from drifting. That's the whole arc; everything in the series is a detail on it.

You know it when you can tell the story above in two minutes without notes. If you can, you're ready for sitting 1.

import gymnasium as gym
env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
returns = []
for ep in range(20):
    obs, info = env.reset(seed=ep)
    total, done = 0.0, False
    while not done:
        action = env.action_space.sample()          # the policy: a coin flip
        obs, reward, terminated, truncated, info = env.step(action)
        total += reward
        done = terminated or truncated
    returns.append(total)
print("mean return of a random policy:", sum(returns) / len(returns))

Checkpoint

Closed book. Write your answer before you open each one. If you miss one, reread the block it names, not the whole stage.

  1. Rewards [−1, −1, −1, −1], γ = 0.8. What is G₀?

    Answer

    Backwards: −1, −1.8, −2.44, −2.952. So G₀ ≈ −2.95.

    Reread: Sitting 1

  2. What's the difference between Monte Carlo and TD learning, in one sentence each?

    Answer

    Monte Carlo waits for the episode to end and uses the actual return. TD updates after one step, using the reward plus its own estimate of the next state's value (bootstrapping).

    Reread: Sitting 3

  3. Q(s, a) = 0.5, r = −1, max Q(s′) = 2.0, α = 0.1, γ = 0.9. New Q(s, a)?

    Answer

    0.5 + 0.1 × (−1 + 1.8 − 0.5) = 0.53.

    Reread: Sitting 3

  4. Why subtract a baseline in the policy gradient, and why doesn't it bias the gradient?

    Answer

    It cuts variance: only better-than-expected actions get pushed up. It's unbiased because the baseline doesn't depend on the action, and E[∇log π] = 0.

    Reread: Sitting 5 / time-permitting post

  5. PPO: A = +1, ratio 1.5, ε = 0.2. Gradient or not?

    Answer

    No. min(1.5·1, 1.2·1) = 1.2 takes the clipped branch, which is constant in θ.

    Reread: Sitting 9

  6. Name what PPO has that GRPO drops, and what GRPO uses instead.

    Answer

    The learned value network (critic). GRPO uses the mean (and std) of a group of samples for the same prompt as the baseline.

    Reread: Sitting 10

  7. Where does KL divergence appear in LLM RL, and what does it protect against?

    Answer

    As a penalty (or a monitored metric) between the policy and a frozen reference model. It stops the policy drifting into text the reward model or verifier scores well but that isn't really better, and keeps general ability.

    Reread: Sitting 8
