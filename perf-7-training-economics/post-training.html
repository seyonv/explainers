From pretrained to served: SFT, RL and distillation

Also called post-training, alignment, fine-tuning, RLHF, RL with verifiable rewards, or knowledge distillation. The last training card of "Training and the cost of a token". It follows DeepSeek-V3 and DeepSeek-R1 (arXiv 2412.19437; arXiv 2501.12948 v2, Jan 2026) from base model to served model, with Llama 3 §4 and §6 as the second example and Hinton et al. 2015 for the original meaning of distillation. Breadth, not depth: for each stage, what it is, what it costs, and what it hands to inference.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A pretrained base model only continues text; you can't ship it. Turning it into a chat or reasoning model takes SFT, RL and sometimes distillation, and each stage hands something different to the serving team: new weights, a context length, and an output-length distribution that can make traffic far more decode-heavy.

Where it fits: Last training card. Builds on compute-budgets (post-training is tiny next to 6ND, except when it isn't) and the training-step memory count (a trained model costs 16 bytes/param, a frozen one 2). It hands off to the economics half: what serving receives.

In one breath

Pretraining makes a base model that continues text. Post-training turns it into something you can serve: SFT teaches it the answer format by imitating curated examples. RL (or a preference method like DPO) rewards good answers instead of copying them, and for a reasoning model that is where the long chains of thought come from. Distillation optionally pours the result into a smaller model. Post-training is cheap next to pretraining. But RL is mostly inference: most of the work is generating answers to score. And what comes out of it (long reasoning outputs) changes the traffic your servers see.

RL, part 1: PPO needs four models, GRPO drops one

RLHF with PPO keeps four models in play. The policy writes answers and is trained. The reward model scores them. A frozen reference copy keeps the policy from drifting (a KL penalty). A value model (critic) estimates, token by token, the reward still to come, so PPO can work out which tokens helped. R1 §A.3: "Since the value model is usually of similar size as the policy model, it introduces a significant memory and computational overhead." It is also hard to train when the only reward comes at the end of a long chain of thought that may revise itself halfway. GRPO deletes the value model. It samples a group of G answers to the same question and uses the group as its own baseline.

R1 doesn't say whether std is the population or sample version; with the sample version the values are +1.68 / −0.56. Our note: if all 16 answers are right (or all wrong), every Ai is 0 (implementations guard the divide-by-zero), so that question's 16 rollouts teach nothing. That is generation time spent for no gradient. The full objective clips the probability ratio (PPO-style, ε) and subtracts β·KL(πθ‖πref) inside the loss. PPO instead adds the KL as a per-token reward, which R1 argues quietly penalizes long answers.

Does GRPO actually beat PPO? R1 §A.3, on DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite (16B MoE, 2.4B active) on MATH: PPO with the common default GAE λ = 0.95 did "considerably worse". Tuned to λ = 1.0 it came close to GRPO. So the win is mostly practical: no critic to hold in memory, and no λ to tune.

RL, part 2: the inference inside RL

R1's Supplementary B.1 splits each RL step into four modules. Two of them are pure inference, one is CPU work, and only one trains:

  • Rollout. Prompts go to vLLM workers, each holding the actor (policy), which sample the group of answers. For the MoE they use expert parallelism across nodes, redundant copies of hot experts to balance load, and the MTP head for self-speculative decoding, "effectively minimizing the completion time for the longest samples."
  • Inference. The reward model and the reference model run a forward pass (prefill only) over every sample.
  • Rule-based reward. Code execution and answer matching, run asynchronously so they overlap with the other two.
  • Training. The only module with a backward pass: length-sorted, best-fit packing into fixed-length chunks, and DualPipe. Each module's models are offloaded from VRAM to memory or disk when it finishes.

Every tool on that list is a serving technique from earlier courses: the engine (anatomy of vLLM and SGLang), expert parallelism and redundant experts, and speculative decoding (speculative decoding II). V3 reports its MTP draft token is accepted 85–90% of the time, for 1.8× the decode speed (V3 §5.4.3). Llama 3 did the same in its rejection sampling: PagedAttention, with the prompt's KV pages shared across all K samples, gave "over 2×" throughput (§4.2.2). That is the prefix sharing idea again: GRPO's 16 answers per question share one prompt.

Why the longest answer matters. The policy update can't start until the whole rollout is scored, so the step waits for its longest answer. By then most answers are finished and the GPU is decoding a tiny batch. Here it is in the course's step formula, for a Llama-3.1-8B-sized policy on one H100 at the batch-1 floor (the running example, not DeepSeek's setup):

time to write n tokens alone ≈ n × W/BW + KV × n(n−1)/2 ÷ BW W = 16.06 GB, BW = 3.35 TB/s, KV = 128 KiB per token n = 8,793 R1's average thinking length, hard math (Fig 18) → 43.7 s n = 65,536 the max length cap → 398.2 s (9.1×) n = 65,536 with MTP's 1.8× (V3's figure) → 221.2 s

Our model: 100% of bandwidth, no batching gain in the tail, so this is a floor. It shows the shape, not DeepSeek's timings. Our framing, for your future job: an RL run for a reasoning model is an inference job with a training step attached. Every token per second an inference team wins (kernels, speculative decoding, better scheduling) shortens the rollout, and the rollout is most of the RL bill. That is why R1's framework hands rollouts to vLLM, a serving engine, instead of generating inside the training code.

Distillation: two different things share the name

Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015) train a small model to match the big ("cumbersome") model's whole output distribution, softened with a temperature T:

Hinton's own example: one handwritten 2 gets probability 10⁻⁶ of being a 3 and 10⁻⁹ of being a 7. Those ratios say which 2s look like 3s, but at T = 1 they barely affect the loss. Raising T brings them out. When mixed with a hard-label loss, the soft-target gradients "scale as 1/T²", so they are multiplied by T².

DeepSeek-R1's "distillation" is not this. It is plain SFT: the student is fine-tuned "for 2–3 epochs using the 800k data" of R1-generated (and filtered) text, max context 32,768, batch 64. The student sees one sampled sequence per example and never sees the teacher's probabilities:

Sequence-level SFT works across model families: the teacher and student don't have to share a vocabulary, and the teacher is run once to make the data, not at every training step. Those two points are our reading, not claims the paper makes. Hinton-style matching needs the teacher's distribution at every training position.

The last row is the one that reaches your scheduler. Long, variable-length outputs make traffic decode-heavy: each request holds its KV for thousands of steps, the KV budget rather than compute limits the batch, and preemption gets more expensive (the scheduler: KV budget, admission and preemption). And a stream of long decodes is exactly the load that suffers when new prompts' prefills cut in, which is the case for separate decode GPUs (DistServe I). V3 Table 9 states the trade-off directly: distilling R1's reasoning "leads to better performance but also substantially increases the average response length", so DeepSeek tuned it for "accuracy and computational efficiency".

Why SFT on teacher samples beats the alternatives for a cheap reasoning model

✗ Serve the big RL model

R1 is the 671B-total MoE: ~671 GB of FP8 weights, so at least one 8-GPU node per replica before any KV. Best quality, but every token pays for a node.

✗ Run RL on the small model

R1 §F.1: Qwen2.5-32B after 10K+ RL steps scored 47.0 on AIME 2024 (pass@1) against 72.6 for R1-Distill-Qwen-32B. Small models doing large-scale RL "require enormous computational power and may not even achieve the performance of distillation."

✗ Hinton-style logit matching

Needs the teacher's full distribution at every training position, so a teacher forward pass for each student token and a shared output vocabulary. The Qwen and Llama bases come from other families with their own tokenizers (our reading).

✓ SFT on 800k teacher samples

Generate once with the teacher, then run an ordinary SFT for 2–3 epochs. The result is a dense 1.5B–70B model; Distill-Llama-8B is the running example's 16.06 GB, one H100.

Is post-training always cheap, and always RL?

Not always cheap. V3's 5K GPU-hours (0.18% of its bill) made a chat model. R1's reasoning RL took 147K, and it grows with answer length because the rollouts are decode. The more you ask a model to think, the more of its training becomes an inference problem.

Not always RL. Llama 3 used SFT plus DPO, which learns from ranked pairs offline, with no rollouts during the update and no value model, because DPO "required less compute" than PPO at its scale. Plain SFT is enough to teach format. Distillation only works once a strong teacher exists: R1 §F.1 also says pushing past the teacher "may still require more powerful base models and larger-scale reinforcement learning."

When a distilled reasoning model is the wrong choice to serve: if answers must be short and fast, a non-reasoning model can cost less per request even with more parameters, because the student inherits the teacher's long outputs. The cost that matters is $/M tokens × tokens per answer ($ per million tokens).

Understand and remember

  • PPO needs a value model to estimate reward-to-go per token → it's about policy-sized (another 128.5 GB for an 8B-sized policy, ours) and hard to train when reward comes only at the end → GRPO samples G answers per question and uses (r − mean)/std as the advantage → no critic, and all-equal groups give zero gradient
  • An RL step = rollout (generate) + score + one training update → at 8,192 answers per rollout and up to 65,536 tokens each, generation dominates → the step waits for its longest answer, decoding a tiny batch → RL for reasoning is an inference job with a training step attached, run on vLLM with MTP speculation
  • Reward rewards correct answers, not short ones → R1-Zero's response length rose through training → the served model writes thousands of thinking tokens (8,793 on hard math) → traffic becomes decode-heavy: KV held for thousands of steps, KV budget caps the batch, preemption gets expensive
  • Sequence-level SFT on 800k teacher samples → teacher runs once, no shared vocabulary needed → small dense students (1.5B–70B) that beat RL on the same small model (72.6 vs 47.0 AIME for 32B) → but inherit the teacher's long outputs, so $/answer = $/M × tokens per answer
  • detail that matters GRPO's 16 answers share one prompt → prefix sharing (Llama 3's PagedAttention reuse gave over 2× in rejection sampling) and MTP self-speculation (1.8× decode) cut rollout time → every serving-side speedup shortens the RL bill

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Halve the answer-length cap from 65,536 to 32,768 tokens for an 8B-sized policy on one H100 at the batch-1 floor. Does the time to write the longest answer halve? Commit, then check with the card's formula.

Reveal

It more than halves. Time ≈ n·W/BW + KV·n(n−1)/2 ÷ BW; the second term grows with n², so 65,536 → 32,768 takes 398.2 s → 178.1 s (2.24× faster, our arithmetic). With MTP's 1.8×, 98.9 s. The quadratic KV-read term is why the tail of long answers dominates rollout time.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

(1) A GRPO group has G = 8 answers, 3 correct (reward 1) and 5 wrong (0). Compute the mean, the population std and every advantage, and check they sum to zero. (2) Now all 8 are correct. What is every advantage, and what did those 8 rollouts buy? (3) For an 8B-architecture student serving 32,768-token answers on one H100 (128 KiB of KV per token, 63.9 GB free after the weights), how much KV does one sequence hold, and how many fit?

Done when: (1) mean 0.375, std 0.484, correct +1.29, wrong −0.775, and 3 × 1.29 − 5 × 0.775 ≈ 0. (2) Every advantage is 0 (std 0, guarded), so the group produces no gradient: 8 generations for nothing. (3) 4.29 GB per sequence, 14 sequences, against 238 at 2k context.
