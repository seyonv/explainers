Mixed precision, FP8 and activation checkpointing

Also called AMP or mixed-precision training, loss scaling, BF16 and FP8 training, gradient checkpointing, recomputation or remat, and selective recompute. These are two ways to make a training step fit and run faster: use fewer bits per number, and keep fewer activations. This card follows Micikevicius et al. §3 (the 2017 mixed-precision paper), DeepSeek-V3 §3.3 (briefly), Korthikanti et al. §4–5 and Chen et al. It applies them to the course's Llama-3.1-8B training example. The number formats themselves are covered in Tensor cores and low precision.

In one breath

Mixed precision runs the big matmuls in 16 bits (or 8) for speed, but keeps a 32-bit copy of the weights, because small updates vanish in 16 bits. With FP16 you also multiply the loss by a large constant so tiny gradients don't round to zero. BF16 has FP32's exponent range, so it mostly doesn't need that step. Activation checkpointing throws away most of what the forward pass saved and recomputes it during the backward pass. Recomputing everything costs one extra forward pass (6ND becomes about 8ND). Recomputing only attention's large but cheap part costs a few percent.

2 · Why FP16 needs loss scaling: gradients sit below its range

FP16's normal numbers run from 2−14 to 65,504. Subnormals continue down to 2−24, with fewer bits of precision, and anything smaller becomes zero. Gradients are mostly tiny. In the paper's Mandarin model, "approximately 5% of weight gradient values have exponents smaller than −24". In the SSD detector, 67% of activation gradients are zero, and values in [2−27, 2−24) "were important to preserve". Scaling the loss shifts every gradient up by the same number of binades (chain rule), so the whole distribution moves into range:

Dynamic loss scaling. A fixed S has to be guessed. The paper's conclusion proposes adjusting it automatically: inspect the gradients for overflow, skip the update when one occurs, and move S up or down. The lab runs an illustrative policy: start at 65,536, halve and skip on overflow, double after 200 clean steps. Over 2,000 steps whose gradients shrink and sometimes spike, it skips 9 steps (0.4%), and S drifts from 65,536 down to 2,048 and back up to 16,384. Framework defaults for this policy aren't in our sources.

BF16 removes loss scaling, not master weights. Coarser rounding makes lost updates worse, not better:

That is why the training step's 16 bytes per parameter still include a 4-byte FP32 master copy: 2 (BF16 weights) + 2 (grads) + 4 (master) + 4 + 4 (Adam m, v). The Playbook's point: "mixed precision training itself doesn't save memory". It pays off in faster math and half-size activations.

3 · FP8 training: one scale per tile, not per tensor brief

FP8 matmuls run at twice the BF16 rate on an H100 (1,979 vs 989 dense TFLOPS), but E4M3 tops out at 448. With one scale per tensor, a single outlier sets the scale and squeezes every other value toward zero. The two approaches:

4 · Activation checkpointing: keep less, recompute in the backward pass

The backward pass needs tensors the forward pass produced. Korthikanti et al. count them in bytes per layer, for 16-bit values and a GPT-style layer:

Alayer=sbh ( 34everything else: kept + 5ashscores, softmax, dropout: recomputed by "selective" ) bytes
ssequence length8,192 bmicro-batch (sequences)1 hhidden size4,096 aattention heads32 Llayers (multiply Alayer by L)32

Filled in for Llama-3.1-8B: 5as/h = 5 · 32 · 8,192 / 4,096 = 320. For GPT-3 in the paper it is 80. The score term grows with s, so at 8K tokens it is 90% of all activations.

Assumptions. The formula describes a GPT layer (4h GeLU MLP, dropout, full multi-head K and V, micro-batch 1). Llama uses SwiGLU (14,336 wide), GQA (8 KV heads) and no dropout, so its true bytes differ. These are our estimates, not measurements. FlashAttention never stores the s×s scores at all. With it, "none" already costs about the selective 36.5 GB. As the Playbook puts it, "most people using FlashAttention are already making use of selective recomputation". Llama 3 pretrained at 8K "without activation checkpointing" by sharding with TP and PP and freeing tensors early. For this 8B shape, TP8 + sequence parallelism would take selective's 36.5 GB to 4.6 GB per GPU (ours).

What the papers report.

Korthikanti, GPT-3 175B (a=96, s=2,048, h=12,288): 5as/h = 80 selective saves 70% of activation memory for 2.7% more FLOPs (MT-NLG: 65% for 1.6%) measured, 22B, one layer (Table 4): full +39% time · selective +7% · selective+SP +4% headline "5×" less memory = sequence parallelism AND selective together, vs TP alone full recompute "one extra forward": Chen · 33%: ZeRO · 30–40%: Korthikanti, Playbook · 6ND → ~8ND: Scaling Book. All the same thing: the forward pass is ~1/3 of a step

Two flags. (1) The "5×" is not selective recompute alone. Each technique "nearly" halves memory on its own. (2) The paper's 2.7% equals its Eq. 9, s/6h. By its own Appendix A term count (4Bs²h per layer for the recomputed QKᵀ and ·V), we get 0.9% for GPT-3. We could not reconcile the two, and our 7.0% uses the Appendix A count. Measured time overheads (7%) exceed either figure, because softmax and dropout are memory-bound, not FLOP-bound.

Chen's √n segments. Chen et al. (2016) train an n-layer network in "O(√n) memory … with only the computational cost of an extra forward pass per mini-batch". A 1,000-layer ResNet went "from 48G to 7G". Store a checkpoint every g layers, then in the backward pass rebuild one g-layer segment at a time. Memory is about n/g + g layer-units, which is smallest at g = √n. For n = 32 that means g ≈ 6 and 11.3 units instead of 33. A transformer's checkpoint (its layer input, 2sbh) is 177× smaller than that layer's activations (354sbh). So checkpointing every layer (g = 1) is already close to the minimum, and that is exactly "full".

At run scale (our recomputation): full recompute turns the 8B run's 7.2e23 FLOPs into 9.6e23. At 40% of peak, 0.51M H100-hours becomes 0.68M. The MFU you'd report drops to 30% while the HFU stays at 40%, because MFU doesn't count recompute (see compute budgets).

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ All FP32

Safe, but gives up the tensor cores' 16-bit rate (67 vs 989 TFLOPS on an H100) and stores activations at 4 bytes instead of 2.

✗ Pure FP16, no master copy or scaling

Small gradients round to zero (3% here) and small updates vanish. The Mandarin model lost 80% relative accuracy, and SSD diverged.

✗ Full recompute everywhere

Minimum memory (2.1 GB) but +33% FLOPs on every step. On the 8B run that's 0.17M extra H100-hours spent redoing work.

✓ BF16 + FP32 master + selective

No loss scaling to tune. Activations fall from 380 to 36.5 GB for about 7% more FLOPs, and FlashAttention gives you the selective part for free.

Is this always the right setup?

No. Four cases where something else wins:

  • No BF16 on the chip (T4, V100): you're back to FP16 with dynamic loss scaling and FP32 master weights, as in 2017.
  • FP8 doubles matmul speed on Hopper and Blackwell, but only with fine-grained scales and FP32 promotion. DeepSeek-V3 is the public large-scale success. For most teams it was still experimental in early 2025, and BF16 is the default.
  • Full recompute wins when memory is the limit, not FLOPs: few GPUs, long sequences, or a bigger batch. In Megatron (145B, 128 A100s) recompute cost "up to 33% lower throughput" at small batch. It allowed batches "up to 2× higher" in throughput because the pipeline bubble shrank.
  • Precision at serving time is a separate choice. Llama 3 trained in BF16 and is served in BF16 or FP8. See quantization in serving.
