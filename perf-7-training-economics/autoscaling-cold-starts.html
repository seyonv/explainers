Before this: $ per million tokens and the batch dial

Autoscaling, cold starts and scale to zero

Also called replica autoscaling or horizontal scaling; a cold start is also "startup latency" or "time to first token after scale-up"; scale to zero is also "serverless inference". This card follows Philip Kiely's Inference Engineering §7.2 (book pages 183–192) for the knobs and the four parts of a cold start, and ServerlessLLM (OSDI '24) for how fast loading can get. The running example is Llama-3.1-8B in BF16 (16 GB of weights) on H100s at $3.99 per GPU-hour.

In one breath

Traffic rises and falls, but a GPU you rent costs the same whether it is busy or idle. So you add replicas when load rises and remove them when it falls. You can only safely remove them if a new one can start quickly. A cold start is GPU, then container image, then 16–140 GB of weights, then engine warm-up. It can take seconds or ten minutes, and that time decides how few warm replicas you can get away with.

Kiely's autoscaling knobs

Kiely sets the goal as "enough resources to serve all incoming requests while maintaining your latency SLAs without wasting money on idle GPUs" (p.183). He names two signals (p.185). Utilization is GPU memory or compute usage. Traffic is the number of requests in the system. They can disagree. His example: a few prefill requests with "hundreds of thousands of uncached input tokens" load the GPU far more than many small, cache-hitting ones. His advice (p.186): "Traffic-based scaling decisions can be made proactively, while utilization is a lagging indicator. Use both in combination." A traffic-based autoscaler then has five settings (p.186):

KnobKiely's questionWhat it trades
Min replicas"the minimum number of replicas that stay running, regardless of traffic"Idle $ at night vs cold starts for the first users. 0 is scale to zero.
Max replicas"the maximum number of replicas that you can allocate when traffic is high"A spend cap vs queueing at the peak.
Autoscaling window"How long is the sliding timeframe that you use to measure traffic"A short window reacts fast but chases noise.
Scale-down delay"How long after a scale down is suggested do you wait in case of another traffic spike?"Longer "prevents premature scale-downs for spikey traffic, but could result in unnecessary spend" (p.186).
Concurrency target"How many requests can each replica handle at once?"Set it equal to the engine's batch size (p.188). Bigger means cheaper tokens but slower ones for each user.

The scale rule that follows (p.188): "Once every active replica reaches its maximum concurrency, the autoscaling system knows to spin up more replicas. If enough replicas are kicking off half-full batches, it's time to scale back down." Kiely gives no default numbers for any knob, and says nothing more about min replicas than its definition. The trade-off below is our worked example, not his.

The four parts of a cold start

"A cold start is the time it takes to spin up a new replica of a model" (p.188). It matters because "If you can't spin up replicas fast, it's hard to confidently scale down, leading to over-provisioning." Kiely splits it into four steps that add up one after another (Fig. 7.9, p.189), and says "Each of these factors needs to be optimized separately." Think of opening a restaurant for a rush: you rent the room, bring in the kitchen equipment, stock the pantry, then heat the ovens.

Two checks against the paper: 130 GB at 5 GB/s = 26 s, which matches ServerlessLLM's "a minimum of 26 seconds" for LLaMA-2-70B. OPT-6.7B's ~13.4 GB at 1 Gbps = 107 s, against the 114 s KServe actually took. The exact 8B is 16.06 GB, which takes 128.5 s at 1 Gbps.

That's why Kiely says a public hub is limited "by their egress speed", S3 adds "network latency and data transfer costs", and multi-hundred-billion-parameter models need "gigabytes per second of bandwidth". The fix he gives is loading "from a source cached physically near the GPU instance within the same datacenter" (p.190). FP8 halves every row, because it halves the bytes.

Engine startup: compile and CUDA graph capture

Before it serves a request, an engine allocates the KV cache, may run torch.compile (with kernel auto-tuning), and captures one CUDA graph per batch size (Kernel fusion, launch overhead and CUDA graphs). The vLLM docs promise that "all the compilation finishes before we serve any requests", so all of it lands inside the cold start. They also say auto-tuning "takes quite a long time (from seconds to minutes, depending on the model size and the batch size)". The cure is the same as for weights: cache it. vLLM writes to ~/.cache/vllm/torch_compile_cache, which you can "directly copy … in your deployment scenario to save a great amount of compilation time". Kiely adds the catch: a cached engine only runs on "exactly the GPU type, CUDA version, and software dependencies" it was built with (p.190). How long engine startup takes varies with engine, model and settings. We didn't find a published end-to-end number, so the 10–180 s above is illustrative.

ServerlessLLM: making the cold start mostly a local read

Fu et al. (OSDI '24) start from two observations. First, even from local NVMe, "loading LLaMA-2-70B into 8 GPUs takes 84 seconds" with PyTorch (OPT-30B into 4 GPUs takes 34 s), while generating a token usually takes "less than 100ms". Second, GPU servers have lots of unused DRAM and SSD. An 8-GPU PCIe 5.0 server moves "512 GB/s between the host memory and GPUs, and around 60 GB/s from NVMe SSDs (RAID 0)". Their system has three parts:

  • Loading-optimized checkpoints and multi-tier loading. Tensors are regrouped into one binary partition per GPU, read sequentially in large chunks (16 MB), with a tensor index for "direct tensor addressing". Loading then streams through a pinned-memory chunk pool, pipelined across SSD → DRAM → GPU. On a 12 GB/s NVMe RAID 0 it is "3.6 - 8.2X faster" than Safetensors and PyTorch: 6× / 3.6× for OPT-2.7B and 8.2× / 4.7× for LLaMA-2-70B. Safetensors took "112K" page faults on LLaMA-2-7B. LoRA adapters load 4.4× faster.
  • Live migration that moves tokens, not KV. Suppose the server holding a model's checkpoint is busy. ServerlessLLM loads the model on that server anyway, then moves the running request somewhere else. It sends the request's "tokens (typically 10-100s KB) instead of the large KV-Cache (typically 1-10s GB)", and the destination recomputes the KV. That works because "recompute the KV-Cache for 1000 tokens equals to the time to generate about 100 new tokens". It runs in rounds until the source's new tokens are caught up. For our 8B, a 2,000-token request is 8 KB of token ids vs 0.26 GB of KV (our arithmetic, 128 KiB/token).
  • Locality-aware (startup-time-optimized) scheduling. Cost models estimate load time from each tier on each server, plus migration time. The scheduler picks the server with the lowest estimated startup.

The headline is "reducing latency by 10 - 200X". The caveat: the testbeds are old, small GPUs on slow links, an 8×A5000 server on a 1 Gbps storage network and 4 servers × 4 A40s on 10 Gbps Ethernet. At 1 Gbps the baseline is mostly a download, so the ratio balloons. The paper's own 100 Gbps what-if cuts the gap to 4.7×. On H100 nodes with 100+ Gbps links, expect gains nearer that end (our inference). Newer systems attack the same step, for example NVIDIA Dynamo's ModelExpress, which streams weights GPU-to-GPU for "7x faster cold-start" (README claim).

Autoscaling at all is the big win: 48% cheaper than provisioning for the peak. Going from min 1 to zero saves only 6 GPU-hours ($23.94/day), and it costs you the first morning user, who waits a whole cold start (33 s tuned, 10 min naive). This day is also hour-grained. Real traffic spikes within minutes, which is where cold-start time turns into queueing:

Backlog = (18,000 − 12,900) tok/s × cold start. After the third replica is up, the spare capacity is 3 × 6,450 − 18,000 = 1,350 tok/s, so drain = backlog ÷ 1,350. A 10-minute cold start leaves users queued for about 49 minutes in all. So you either keep a spare warm or make the cold start short. That's Kiely's point about over-provisioning.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Provision for the peak

No cold starts ever, but the fleet runs at 35.6% utilization in our day: $383/day and $0.48/M, 2.8× the busy-GPU price. It also still breaks on a peak bigger than the one you planned for.

✗ Scale to zero with a naive start

Cheapest on paper ($176/day), but the first user after a quiet spell waits the whole cold start, up to 10 minutes with a fresh node, a 1 Gbps download and an uncached compile. Kiely: it needs "Fast cold starts" and "Robust queueing" first.

✗ Bake weights into the image, or keep all models warm

Baking worked for small models, but "the weights dwarf the image" now. Keeping every model warm is the "over-subscribing GPUs" approach ServerlessLLM calls challenging for LLMs, "which require substantially greater resources from costly GPUs".

✓ Autoscale with a floor and a fast cold start

Scale on traffic plus utilization, set the concurrency target to the batch size, keep a small min-replica floor, and cut the cold start to seconds (warm nodes, cached image and engine, weights close by). In our day: $199.50/day, 68% utilization, no user ever waits a cold start.

Is scale to zero (or even autoscaling) always right?

No. Kiely says scale to zero "is not a fit for all workloads" (p.192). It fits:

  • Development, "when testing is bursty and latency for the first request is unimportant".
  • Periodic production traffic, "like an agent that's only accessed during business hours in one country or an offline system designed for daily batch processing jobs".

It doesn't fit light, unscheduled, latency-sensitive traffic. For that, use a pay-per-token API. Autoscaling itself matters less when traffic is flat (every policy converges on the same replica count) or when the GPUs are reserved for months anyway. Then idle hours are already paid for, and the question becomes filling them with batch work, as DeepSeek does with its night-time nodes (cost-per-token card). Very large models (70B+ across 8 GPUs, big MoEs) have cold starts long enough that most teams keep a floor of warm replicas and scale only between the floor and the max.
