Before this: The step-time formula (perf-2)

$ per million tokens and the batch dial

Also called cost per token, $/M tokens, unit economics or (when input and output are mixed into one number) the blended price. This is the number an inference company sells, and here it's worked out for the course's running example: Llama-3.1-8B in BF16 on one H100 at $3.99 per GPU-hour, with a real P&L from DeepSeek to check it against.

In one breath

A GPU costs the same per hour whether it serves one user or two hundred. Decode reads all the weights once per step no matter how many sequences share that step, so batching more users into each step multiplies the tokens you get from that hour. That is the batch dial: turn it up and each token gets about 49× cheaper, while each user's stream gets about 5× slower.

Filled in at the biggest batch: 3.99 × 1 ÷ (9,973 × 3600 × 1) × 106 = $0.111 per million output tokens. At batch 1: 3.99 ÷ (205 × 3600) × 106 = $5.40.

The step time comes from course 2's step-time formula: read every sequence's KV cache, then either read the weights or do the math, whichever is slower.

t_step = B × KV_seq / BW + max( 2·B·N / C , W / BW ) N = 8.03e9 params W = 16.06 GB (BF16) BW = 3.35 TB/s C = 989 TFLOP/s (dense BF16) KV_seq = 2,048 tokens × 128 KiB = 268.4 MB → 0.080 ms of reading per sequence W / BW = 16.06e9 / 3.35e12 = 4.79 ms 2·B·N / C passes that only above B ≈ 295 B = 238: 238 × 0.080 + max(3.87, 4.79) = 19.07 + 4.79 = 23.86 ms 238 / 23.86 ms = 9,973 tok/s

The book: "At the cost of doubling per-token latency, we can achieve a roughly 100x reduction in per-token cost." The 8B curve is flatter. Our reading (an inference, not the book's): the 8B's 16 GB of weights weigh the same as the KV cache of only 123k tokens (16.06 GB ÷ 128 KiB), while the 70B's int8 weights weigh as much as 438k tokens of its 160 kB KV. At 2k context the 8B's steps become KV-bound by B ≈ 64, so adding users adds reading time almost one-for-one.

Why output tokens cost more than input tokens

Input (prompt) tokens go through prefill: the whole prompt passes through the model in one go, so each weight read from memory is reused by every prompt token. A 1,000-token prompt is far above the H100's critical batch (Bcrit ≈ 295), so prefill is limited by FLOPs, not bytes. Output tokens come from decode, one per sequence per step, which is the table above.

prefill floor, 1,000 tokens = 2 × 8.03e9 × 1,000 / 989e12 = 16.2 ms → 61,582 input tok/s $/M input = 3.99 / (61,582 × 3600) × 1e6 = $0.018 (100% of peak FLOPs; $0.036 at 50%) $/M output = $0.111 at B = 238 → 6.2× the input ceiling; $5.40 at B = 1 → 300×

Our model. Real prefill runs well below peak FLOPs, so treat $0.018 as a floor. Price lists show the same ordering: DeepInfra charges $0.02 in / $0.04 out for this model, and DeepSeek R1 charged $0.55 per M input on a cache miss against $2.19 per M output (4×).

Utilization: an idle GPU still bills

The GPU-hour is paid whether or not requests arrive. Tokens only come in the busy hours, so $/M is divided by the busy share u. A GPU busy half the day doubles every price in the table.

Our model. Low traffic also shrinks the batch, which pushes you up the table as well; the two effects multiply. That is why providers pool many customers onto one fleet and why scaling down at night matters (autoscaling and cold starts).

Other levers are multipliers on the same table

Every other trick changes one term of the formula: fewer bytes per step (FP8), more sequences in memory (smaller KV), a cheaper hour (P), or more tokens per step (speculation). Starting from the B = 238 row at $0.111:

Our model (labs/cost-model.py). The batch-that-fits column ignores activation memory, so real engines fit somewhat fewer sequences. FP8 matters most at small batch, where weight reads dominate: at B = 1 it takes $5.40 to $2.75 (1.97×). Kiely's rule of thumb for real engines is smaller: one step down in precision "generally offers 30 to 50 percent better performance" (see quantization in serving).

  • Speculative decoding raises the tokens produced per step. It helps most at small batch, where steps are bound by weight reads, and fades as the batch approaches the compute roof (speculation at batch 1 vs batch 64). So it mostly makes the fast, expensive rows cheaper.
  • Prefix caching skips prefill for tokens whose KV cache already exists, so it cuts the input side and time to first token (RadixAttention, prompt caching). DeepSeek hits its cache on 56.3% of input tokens.

OUR REASONING, NOT FROM A SOURCE

DeepInfra sells output at 0.36× our BF16 ceiling, and our ceiling already assumes a perfect engine that is busy all day. So selling at $0.04 without a loss needs several levers stacked. FP8 weights and KV bring the model to $0.053. A below-list GPU price (spot at $2.60, or a reserved contract) brings it to $0.035. Then you need batches that stay full all day, and probably shorter average contexts or newer GPUs with more bandwidth per dollar (a B200 costs $0.84 per TB/s-hour vs $1.19 for an H100). We don't know any provider's actual setup, and a price may also be set below cost to win traffic. CoreWeave's BF16 $0.22 happens to equal our BF16 ceiling at 50% utilization.

A real P&L: DeepSeek's Day 6 numbers

DeepSeek published one day (Feb 27–28, 2025) of serving V3 and R1 on H800s, with prefill and decode on separate nodes (disaggregation). Their numbers, with our derived $/M below the line:

cost = 226.75 nodes × 8 H800 × $2/hr × 24 h = $87,072 / day (average nodes) (assumed lease) revenue = 342B × $0.14 + 266B × $0.55 + 168B × $2.19 ≈ $562,027 / day (cache-hit in) (cache-miss in) (output) (theoretical, R1 prices) profit ÷ cost = (562,027 − 87,072) ÷ 87,072 = 545% ← their "cost profit margin" profit ÷ revenue = 474,955 ÷ 562,027 = 84.5% ← what "margin" usually means ──────────────────────────── ours ──────────────────────────── decode node: $16/hr ÷ (14.8k tok/s × 3600) × 1e6 = $0.30 per M output prefill node: $16/hr ÷ (73.7k tok/s × 3600) × 1e6 = $0.06 per M input all-in: $87,072 ÷ 168B output tokens = $0.52 per M output

Their caveats: revenue assumes every token is billed at R1 prices, but V3 is cheaper, web and app use is free, and night discounts apply. The $2/hr is an assumed lease price, not what they pay. Output ran at 20–22 tok/s per user, so DeepSeek chose a point far down the batch dial.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Price per GPU-hour

Says nothing about tokens. The same $3.99 hour costs $5.40 or $0.11 per million tokens depending on batch, a 49× spread that no shopping around can match.

✗ Throughput alone

9,973 tok/s sounds great, but each user then reads at 42 tok/s. A throughput number without the per-user speed hides which end of the dial you're on.

✗ One blended price

Mixing input and output into one number hides a 6–300× per-token gap (our model). A prompt-heavy workload and an answer-heavy one cost very different amounts at the same blended price.

✓ $/M in and out, with the dial stated

Quote $/M for input and output at a stated batch, context, busy share and tok/s per user. Every lever is then a multiplier on one line, so you can compare them directly.

Is the biggest batch always the right price?

No. B = 238 is the cheapest row, but it gives each user 42 tok/s. A company that sells fast inference is selling per-user speed. If its promise is 100 tok/s per user, it has to stop at about B = 64 and charge $0.17 or more; the gap between those rows is what speed costs. Other cases where the cheapest row is wrong:

  • These are ceilings, not measurements. They assume 100% of bandwidth and no prefill. Real engines land below them; labs/h100-cost-sweep.sh measures how far.
  • Long contexts or long prompts. At 8k context only 59 sequences fit, and the cheapest row costs $0.45; prefill then takes a real share of the hour.
  • Light or bursty traffic never fills the batch, and utilization drops too. Kiely: that is "probably a sign that your AI application is not yet ready for dedicated infrastructure and should use pay-per-token APIs" (§7.2.4, p.192).
