The whole stack, every lever ranked

Overview of course 7, Training and the cost of a token, and the capstone of the Fast LLM inference series. Also called the $/token map or the unit-economics view. The first half of the course is training breadth: a training step, compute budgets, ZeRO, 4D parallelism, precision and post-training. The second half is production economics: $ per million tokens, hardware, autoscaling, routing, reliability and AI-written kernels. This card ranks every lever from all seven courses by what it does to cost per token and to latency. Numbers are for Llama-3.1-8B on one H100 at $3.99 per GPU-hour (Lambda, on demand, seen 2026-09-24) unless a row names another setup. They are our ceilings from the course labs, not measurements.

In one breath

A token's price is the GPU's hourly price divided by how many tokens that GPU turns out in an hour. Everything in this series changes one of three things. The price of the hour: which GPU, on-demand or spot. The tokens each step produces: batching, fewer bytes, speculative decoding, better kernels, smarter splitting across GPUs. The share of hours the GPU is actually busy: autoscaling, routing, reliability. Batching is by far the biggest dial. It makes tokens about 49× cheaper, but each user gets them about 5× slower. So a serving business is a choice of operating point: the latency you promise sets the batch, and the batch sets the price. Training is a one-time bill, 6ND FLOPs, that the serving bill overtakes once you've served about 3D tokens. That is why production models are trained far past "compute-optimal": a smaller model costs less on every token it will ever serve.

The mental model: the stack, and which course moves which term

Every layer changes one term of the formula above. The engine and the model change the most. The GPU sets both the price and the ceiling. The router and autoscaler decide how many of the paid hours do useful work.

The words people use

$/M tokens, blended price

Dollars per million tokens. APIs price input and output separately; a "blended" price mixes them at an assumed ratio (DeepSeek served about 3.6 input tokens per output token). Output costs more because decode is memory-bound.

MFU vs HFU

Model FLOPs utilization counts only the FLOPs the model needs (6ND). Hardware FLOPs utilization also counts recomputation. PaLM: 46.2% MFU, 57.8% HFU.

Compute-optimal vs over-trained

Chinchilla's fit picks the model size that minimizes loss for a training budget, about 20 tokens per parameter as read off its Table 3. Llama-3.1-8B saw about 1,870 tokens per parameter, because serving cost follows model size.

Operating point

The batch size (and so the per-user speed) you run at. It sets $/token more than any other single choice: 48.6× across one H100's range.

Cold start, scale to zero

The time from "we need another replica" to its first token: GPU, image, weights, engine. Weights alone take 128 s at 1 Gbps and 0.25 s over PCIe 5 for 16 GB.

fastp

KernelBench's metric: the share of tasks where an AI-written kernel is correct and more than p× faster than PyTorch. One shot, frontier models managed this less than 20% of the time; with feedback, DeepSeek-R1 went from 36% to 72% on Level 2.

The takeaway

Pick the latency you promise, then make every paid GPU-hour produce as many tokens as that promise allows. In order: batch as many users as your per-token SLO allows; cut bytes per step with FP8 and a KV-efficient model; keep GPUs busy with autoscaling and prefix-aware routing; buy the GPU that is cheapest per byte moved at your batch; split across GPUs inside NVLink first. Before that, the training choice that matters most to serving is model size. A smaller model trained longer is cheaper on every token it will ever serve.

Where this doesn't apply as-is: our $/M figures are bandwidth ceilings for one model on list-price GPUs, which real engines fall short of (KernelBench card: at 70% of the ceiling the batch-64 price rises from $0.172 to $0.245), and real traffic mixes prompt and output lengths. Market prices already sit below our BF16 ceiling, so treat the table as a map of levers, not a price sheet. Training numbers are breadth: the MFU, memory and cost figures depend on sequence length, parallel layout and failures. Meta's own 8B figure (1.46M GPU-hours) is almost 3× our 40% MFU estimate.

Understand and remember

  • A token's price = the hour's price ÷ tokens made in that hour → every optimization changes the hour's price (GPU, spot), tokens per step (batch, bytes, speculation, kernels, layout) or the busy share (autoscaling, routing, reliability) → rank levers as multipliers on one line
  • Decode reads the weights once per step for the whole batch → batching B = 1 → 238 makes tokens 48.6× cheaper but each user 4.9× slower → the latency you promise sets the batch, and the batch sets the price
  • Levers compete for the same slack → speculation fills idle compute at small batch and fades as the batch grows; FP8 weights halve the weight read, which dominates only at small batch → multipliers don't stack cleanly, so re-run the formula at your operating point
  • Training is paid once (6ND) and serving 2N per token forever → after ~3D served tokens serving costs more → a smaller model trained longer (8B on 15T) is ~4.4× cheaper per served token than the Chinchilla-optimal size for the same training budget
  • detail that matters Input tokens go through compute-bound prefill (~$0.018/M at peak) and output tokens through bandwidth-bound decode ($0.111/M at best) → prices are split in and out, and levers that cut prefill (prefix routing, caching) act on the cheap side unless prompts are long

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

FP8 weights only (KV cache stays BF16) on the 8B. Commit before reading on: is the price cut bigger at B = 1 or at the largest batch that fits, and roughly how big is each?

Reveal

Bigger at B = 1: about 1.97× ($5.40 → $2.75), because the step is almost all weight reading. At the largest batch it's only 1.13× ($0.111 → $0.099): KV reads dominate the step there, and the freed 8 GB fits only 268 sequences instead of 238. Adding FP8 KV too (536 sequences) makes it 2.1× at the largest batch.
