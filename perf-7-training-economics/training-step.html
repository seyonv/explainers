Before this: FLOPs per token (perf-1)

A training step and where its memory goes

Also called one iteration, one optimizer step, or the training loop. ZeRO splits the memory into "model states" (weights, gradients, optimizer state) and "residual states" (mainly activations). This card follows the Ultra-Scale Playbook's "First Steps: Training on One GPU", ZeRO §3 and Korthikanti et al. §4. It works through Llama-3.1-8B, the same model courses 1–6 served on one H100.

In one breath

A training step runs the batch forward (2N FLOPs per token), then backward to get a gradient for every weight (4N), then an optimizer step that nudges every weight. To do that, the GPU holds far more than the weights. The standard mixed-precision Adam recipe keeps 16 bytes per parameter (BF16 weights and gradients, an FP32 master copy, and Adam's two FP32 moments), plus activations that grow with batch × sequence. That is why an 8B model that serves comfortably from 16 GB can't train on one 80 GB H100.

The three phases of a step

Two micro-batches (gradient accumulation 2), then one optimizer step. Widths follow FLOPs: backward is twice forward. The optimizer is a cheap elementwise pass over every parameter. The memory panel follows the Playbook's profile: activations climb during forward and are freed layer by layer during backward, gradients build up and persist, and Adam's state appears after the first optimizer step. "Ever noticed how sometimes the training succeeds in the first step but then OOMs during the following training steps?" That is the reason.

Why backward costs twice forward. For each weight matrix, the backward pass does two matmuls the size of the forward one: one for the gradient with respect to the weights (to update them) and one for the gradient with respect to the input (to pass back to the layer below). That gives 2N forward + 4N backward = 6N FLOPs per parameter per token (Scaling Book Part 4). The full derivation, and why serving costs only 2N, is in course 1: FLOPs per token.

Llama-3.1-8B, N = 8.03e9, one 8,192-token sequence: forward 2 × 8.03e9 × 8,192 = 1.32e14 FLOPs backward 4 × 8.03e9 × 8,192 = 2.63e14 FLOPs total 6 × 8.03e9 × 8,192 = 3.95e14 FLOPs ÷ (40% MFU × 989 TFLOP/s dense BF16) ≈ 1.0 s per sequence (40% MFU is an assumption, not a measurement)

What lives on the GPU, and why

Everyday version: the weights are the recipe the kitchen cooks from. The gradients are the tasting notes from this round. Adam's m and v are a running memory of all past tasting notes. The master copy is the chef's precise notebook, which gets rounded before it goes to the line cooks. The activations are the dirty dishes kept until the tasting is done.

ZeRO §3.1, verbatim: "Mixed-precision Adam has K = 12. In total, this results in 2Ψ + 2Ψ + KΨ = 16Ψ bytes". Filled in for 8B: 16.06 + 16.06 + 32.12 + 32.12 + 32.12 = 128.5 GB before a single activation.

Why Adam needs m and v. Adam (Kingma & Ba, Algorithm 1) keeps two running averages for every parameter: the gradient's mean (m) and its uncentred variance (v). Each step divides the mean by the square root of the variance, so every weight gets its own step size. Both have to persist from step to step, and at 4 bytes each they are 8 of the 16 bytes. Llama 3 uses AdamW, which is Adam plus decoupled weight decay. It keeps the same two moments.

Gradient accumulation: micro-batch vs global batch

The global batch is set by the training recipe (the Playbook's "sweet spot" is 4–60M tokens per batch). The micro-batch is set by memory. Gradient accumulation bridges the two. You run forward and backward on one micro-batch, add its gradients into a buffer that persists, repeat, and then average the sum and take one optimizer step. Activations only ever hold one micro-batch, so memory stays flat as the global batch grows. The cost is time, because the passes run one after another.

global batch = micro-batch × grad_acc × DP (data-parallel GPUs) Playbook example: 4M tokens ÷ 4,096 seq = 1,024 sequences micro-batch 2 × 128 GPUs → grad_acc = 1,024 ÷ 256 = 4 (512 GPUs → grad_acc 1) Llama 3 405B: 32 seqs per DP group × DP 64 = 2,048 × 8,192 = 16M tokens 8B on one GPU: micro-batch 1 at 8k → 36.5 GB activations, any grad_acc micro-batch 8 at 8k → 292 GB

The accumulation buffer is why the FP32 gradient convention exists: summing many small BF16 gradients loses precision, so Llama 3 accumulates "in FP32" (§3.3.2). That's the extra 4 bytes that turns 16 into 20. Data parallelism, the "DP" factor, is "just a parallel version of gradient accumulation" (Playbook). It's the next card.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ "It's a 16 GB model"

That's the serving footprint. It counts 2 of the 16 bytes per parameter and none of the activations, which puts it about 10× short for training.

✗ Weights + gradients

32 GB for 8B looks like it fits an H100. It forgets Adam's m and v and the FP32 master copy, which are 12 of the 16 bytes per parameter.

✗ States only, no activations

128.5 GB is fixed, but activations are the part that moves: 9 GB at 2k tokens, 146 GB at 32k, times the micro-batch. Ignore them and long-context runs go out of memory.

✓ 16Ψ (or 20Ψ) + sbhL(34 + 5as/h)

A fixed part per parameter plus a part that scales with batch × sequence. It tells you which lever to pull: shard the fixed part (ZeRO), and shrink the moving part (recompute, micro-batches, parallelism).

Is it always 16 bytes per parameter plus activations?

No. 16 is the standard for BF16 mixed precision with Adam, but it's a convention, not a law:

  • Different recipes store different things. FP32 gradient accumulation makes it 20 (Llama 3). DeepSeek-V3's FP8 recipe with BF16 moments makes it 15. The Scaling Book counts 10 because it leaves out the master copy and gradients.
  • It's per model, not per GPU. ZeRO-3/FSDP divides all 16 bytes by the number of data-parallel GPUs (next card). Activations can't be sharded that way, because each GPU sees different data. Tensor, sequence, context and pipeline parallelism split them instead.
  • Serving is the other end. Inference keeps only the weights (2 bytes BF16, 1 byte FP8) plus a KV cache. The optimizer state is thrown away when the checkpoint ships.
  • Activations are a recipe choice too. Llama 3 trained 405B on 8K sequences "without activation checkpointing", relying on TP and PP to split activations. Other runs use full recompute and pay ~30–40% more time.
