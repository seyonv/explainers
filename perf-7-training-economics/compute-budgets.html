Before this: A training step and where its memory goes

Compute budgets: 6ND, Chinchilla and MFU

Also called training FLOPs, the "6ND rule", compute-optimal (Chinchilla) scaling, and model FLOPs utilization. Three tools that turn "we want to train this model" into GPU-hours and dollars: the FLOP count from Scaling Book Part 4 and PaLM Appendix B, the Chinchilla paper's rule for splitting a budget between model size and data (Hoffmann et al. 2022), and PaLM's MFU, which Llama 3 uses to report 38–43% on 8K–16K H100s.

In one breath

Training costs about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token: 2 for the forward pass and 4 for the backward pass. So a run costs C ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs. Divide that by what your GPUs actually sustain (peak × MFU, typically 35–45%) and you get GPU-hours. Chinchilla says how to split a fixed C between parameters N and tokens D to get the lowest loss. Production models deliberately break that rule. They train a small model far longer because every token served later costs about 2N FLOPs, and training is paid only once.

1 · The budget: C ≈ 6ND Scaling Book Part 4, "Transformer FLOPs"

Every weight matrix does one matmul in the forward pass: 2 FLOPs per weight per token (a multiply and an add). The backward pass does two matmuls of the same size, one for the gradient of the weights and one for the gradient of the input, which the layer below needs. That's 4 more FLOPs. The book's words: "a total of 6NPM FLOPs, compared to 2NPM during inference". Summed over every layer, that's 6 × tokens × parameters.

The 8B's ~15T tokens come from Meta's model card ("~15 trillion tokens"), so treat them as approximate. The Ultra-Scale Playbook writes the same correction per sequence: 6·s·N + 12·L·h·s², where h = H·Q. Divide by s and you get PaLM's per-token form.

When the attention term matters: only when T is large next to 8D

6N counts the weight matmuls. The attention scores (QKᵀ and the weighted sum over V) have no weights, and their cost grows with sequence length T. Scaling Book Part 4 works out that attention FLOPs ÷ matmul FLOPs = T / 8D (D = model width here), so "dot-product attention FLOPs only become dominant during training once T > 8D".

Big models at 8K barely notice. The 8B at 128K context is dominated by attention, so 6ND undercounts badly there. That's why the long-context stage is priced separately.

3 · MFU vs HFU: what "utilization" counts PaLM §4.1 and Appendix B

You need a utilization number to go from FLOPs to hours, and there are two of them. Everyday analogy: MFU is the distance on the map ÷ (top speed × time). HFU is the distance on the odometer, detours included.

4 · Chinchilla: how to split a fixed budget Hoffmann et al. 2022, §3

Given C, should you train a bigger model on fewer tokens or a smaller one on more? Chinchilla trained "over 400 language models ranging from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters on 5 to 500 billion tokens" and answered it three ways:

5 · Why production models over-train Llama-3.1-8B: 1,868 tokens per parameter

Take the 8B's own budget, C = 7.23e23, and let Chinchilla spend it. Along one isoFLOP curve, a bigger model means fewer tokens (D = C ÷ 6N):

$0.17/M is the 8B's decode ceiling at batch 64 on one H100 at $3.99/hr (course facts, "our model"). The other two rows scale it by N, because decode reads every weight each step. That's our rough scaling: a 77.6B model doesn't even fit one H100 in BF16. The eq. 10 optimum comes from the rounded exponents in the lab, and Table 2's 0.46/0.54 would move it slightly.

Our framing: Chinchilla minimises training compute for a target loss. A model that's served costs 2N FLOPs (and N weight reads) on every token, forever. Training costs 6N per token, once. At equal N, serving 3D tokens (6ND ÷ 2N = 45T for the 8B) matches the whole training bill. So a lab that expects to serve trillions of tokens buys a small N with lots of D. In the fit, 8B at 15T gives up 0.025 in loss against the optimum and serves each token 4.4× cheaper. Meta says it directly: "we also train our smaller models for much longer than is compute-optimal. The resulting models perform better than compute-optimal models at the same inference budget."

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ "GPUs × days" as the budget

It depends on the cluster, the MFU and the precision, so it doesn't carry over. 2.788M H800-hours and 30.84M H100-hours only compare once you turn both into FLOPs (3.3e24 vs 3.8e25).

✗ Quoting HFU or sparse peak

Counting recomputed FLOPs, or dividing by NVIDIA's 2:4-sparse 1,979 TF, flatters the number. Megatron's "52%" is about 39% as MFU, and 430 TF on an H100 is 43% of dense peak, not 22%.

✗ Kaplan-style "grow the model"

With a = 0.73, most new compute went into parameters: GPT-3 175B, Gopher 280B and MT-NLG 530B all trained on 270–300B tokens. Chinchilla 70B on 1.4T beat Gopher with the same compute.

✓ 6ND ÷ (peak × MFU), split by purpose

A one-line FLOP count that holds to within about 10% (Chinchilla App. F: the detailed count is 0.99–1.10× of 6ND), a utilization that compares across systems, and a Chinchilla split you then shift toward small N when serving cost matters more.

Is 6ND with Chinchilla always the right plan?

No. Each piece has a known edge:

  • Long context: once T approaches 8D, add 12·L·H·Q·T per token. For the 8B at 128K, attention costs 4× the weight matmuls.
  • MoE: N means active parameters (37B of DeepSeek-V3's 671B). Memory and communication follow the total, so MFU tends to come out lower.
  • Recompute: activation checkpointing makes the hardware do roughly 8N per token while MFU still credits 6N. Lower MFU can mean a faster run if it allows a bigger batch (PaLM chose remat for exactly that reason).
  • Chinchilla-optimal is the wrong goal when the model will be served heavily (train smaller and longer, as Llama 3 did) or when unique data runs out (Chinchilla assumes you have enough of it to avoid repeating).
  • A budget isn't a bill: Meta's card hours are 1.2–2.9× our 40%-MFU estimate, and DeepSeek's $5.576M excludes prior research. Failures, restarts and ablations are real costs (see the reliability card).
