Reliability and quality: failures, canaries and evals

Also called fault tolerance, high availability, safe rollouts, progressive delivery, or quality regression testing. GPUs fail often enough that you plan for failure: Llama 3 lost its training job 419 times in 54 days, and Kiely turns that into about one failure per 50,000 GPU-hours for inference. Every speed change (quantization, a new draft model, a new kernel) can also quietly change the answers. This card covers both, and ends with our checklist for shipping a speed change.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: At fleet scale GPUs fail every few hours, and every speed trick (quantization, a new draft model, a new kernel) can quietly change the answers. Without a design for both, a training run spends its time restarting and a serving fleet ships a faster model that is subtly worse.

Where it fits: Builds on course 6's sizing card (blast radius by TP group size) and on the cold starts of the autoscaling card. It closes the loop on every speed lever in the series: each one needs a quality check before it ships.

In one breath

Hardware breaks at a steady rate, so what you design is how much each failure costs. A training job loses the work since its last checkpoint plus a restart, a serving fleet loses the replica (or node) that failed until a spare takes over. Software changes break things too, so new versions reach users gradually (a canary), and a speed change only ships once an eval shows the outputs stayed the same.

1. How often GPUs fail: Llama 3's 54 days

Meta pre-trained Llama 3 405B on up to 16,384 H100s. Over a 54-day snapshot the job was interrupted 466 times: 47 planned (firmware upgrades, config or dataset changes) and 419 unexpected. 2. What that means for training: checkpoint and restart

Training is synchronous. In Llama 3's words, "a single GPU failure may require a restart of the entire job". So the job writes a checkpoint every τ minutes, and a failure costs the work since the last one plus the time to restart all 16k GPUs. Meta reports "higher than 90% effective training time", meaning useful training time over elapsed time, and got there by cutting "job startup and checkpointing time". The standard first-order model (Young's approximation, our addition, not from the paper) shows why those two times matter:

waste≈ Cτ+Cwriting checkpoints + τ/2+RMlost work + restart, per failure τ*=2CMbest checkpoint interval
Ctime the job pauses to write one checkpoint0.5–1 min (illustrative) τminutes of training between checkpoints13.6 · 19.3 · 60 min Rtime to restart the whole job after a failure2 … 30 min (illustrative) Mmean time between unexpected failures of the job186 min (Llama 3)

Filled in for C = 1, R = 5: τ* = √(2 × 1 × 186) = 19.3 min, waste = 1 ÷ 20.3 + (9.65 + 5) ÷ 186 + 0.3% for the 47 planned stops = 13.1%, so 86.9% effective. Only M comes from Llama 3; C and R are illustrative.

3. What that means for serving: expect failures, contain them

Kiely (§7.3.3) takes the same data to inference: "approximately one failure per 50,000 GPU-hours", and "running a single node of eight GPUs for inference for an entire year is over 70,000 GPU-hours" (8 × 8,760 = 70,080, so 1.40 failures per node-year). Our arithmetic: GPUs × 24 ÷ 50,000 per day, assuming independent failures at Kiely's average rate. The 16,384 row gives back Llama 3's 7.76 unexpected failures a day, as it should.

The difference from training is that a serving fleet doesn't have to stop. Each replica is independent, so a failure takes down only the group that runs in lockstep with the dead GPU. Sizing a deployment (course 6) already worked this out for 500 users of Llama-3.1-70B on 16 H100s: a TP8 group's mean time between failures is 6,250 h, and after one failure TP8 × 2 still holds 386 of 500 users while TP4 × 4 holds 498. Two things change that picture.

  • GPU health is node-level. Kiely: "When a single GPU fails, other GPUs on the node often fail next or need to be taken offline for maintenance. Proactively noting failures, cordoning nodes, and cycling pods keeps individual clusters healthy." If the whole node is drained, TP4 × 4 loses two replicas and keeps only 332 users, fewer than TP8 × 2's 386.
  • Spares decide availability more than layout does. A warm spare node that already holds the weights turns a day-long outage into minutes (see cold starts for why "warm" matters).

labs/failure-sim.py, checked against 20,000 simulated years (seeded). Availability here means the share of user-hours served. Illustrative: 24 h to repair or replace a node with no spare, and 15 min to fail over to a warm spare. Users per replica come from the sizing card. Kiely's preface puts closed APIs at "two nines of uptime" and dedicated deployments at "four nines or better".

Availability isn't the only cost. Every request in flight on the failed group is cut off: with load spread evenly that's 250 requests for a TP8 replica and 125 for a TP4 one. Those clients retry, and the retries land in your tail latency (see retries and timeouts).

At the scale of a whole cluster or cloud, Kiely names two postures. Active-active: "multiple regions or clusters actively serve live traffic at the same time. If any plane fails, traffic seamlessly continues on the others." Active-passive: "a 'hot standby' cluster or region is kept ready but idle", and traffic is cut over when the active one fails. Active-active uses every GPU but needs spare headroom in each region; active-passive pays for idle GPUs to get a simpler failover. Geography adds latency either way: Kiely's rule of thumb is "five milliseconds for a request to pass through a time zone".

4. Rolling out a new version: blue-green, canary, shadow

A new engine version, quantized checkpoint or kernel is a change like any other, and the way you release it decides how many users see a bad one. Kiely (§7.4.1): blue-green keeps two full environments, and "If the blue deployment is using 100 GPUs, the green deployment requires another 100 GPUs before traffic can cut over." A canary sends "a small percentage" of live traffic to the new version, watches it, reverts on trouble, and ramps up to 100%. "With autoscaling, canary deployments don't increase cost much at scale", because the old deployment scales down as traffic leaves it.

Shadow responses are never returned to users, so a shadow can't hurt anyone, but it costs GPUs. Kiely suggests starting with "a random sample of production traffic".

5. Quality: a speed change can change the answers

This course is about making inference faster. Most speed tricks change arithmetic, and some change which tokens come out. Kiely (§4.5.1, p.113) says eval datasets such as MMLU, gsm8k, SWE-bench and HumanEval serve for "spot checking that performance optimizations haven't impacted model output quality", ideally one that matches your use, like "HumanEval when reducing latency for a code completion system".

Speed changeSame outputs?Published evidence
Quantization (course 5)No: weights and activations are roundedRed Hat, over half a million evaluations of Llama 3.1 8B/70B/405B: on OpenLLM v1 "all quantization schemes … recover over 99%"; on v2 "close to 99% … with all models maintaining at least 96% recovery"; HumanEval "8-bit … 99.9% … 4-bit … 98.9%". Speedups: W8A8 "an average 1.8×", W4A16 "2.4× for single-stream".
Speculative decoding, exact (course 5)Yes, in distributionLeviathan et al.'s accept rule min(1, p/q) plus resampling gives exactly the target model's distribution. EAGLE-style drafts are verified the same way and stay exact.
Medusa typical acceptance (course 5)No, when samplingAccepts a draft token if it is "plausible enough" under an entropy-scaled threshold. At temperature 0 it is plain greedy decoding; with sampling it is not distribution-exact, and the paper's Figure 5 shows a quality vs speed trade-off.
New kernels, batch sizes, TP degreeBit-for-bit: no. Usually closeFloating-point addition isn't associative, so a different reduction order changes the last bits. Thinking Machines (He, 2025): 1,000 temperature-0 completions of Qwen3-235B on one vLLM server gave 80 unique outputs, identical for 102 tokens, because batch size varies with load.

Two warnings from the sources. Llama 3 (§6.2) found that for FP8 "standard benchmarks do not adequately reflect the effects": without capping the scaling factors, "the model occasionally produces corrupted responses even though the benchmark performance is strong". They checked instead with the reward-model score distribution of 100,000 responses in FP8 vs BF16. And because of the nondeterminism above, a raw "outputs changed" diff always shows some change. Measure the baseline against itself first, then compare the candidate against that noise floor.

OUR CHECKLIST FOR SHIPPING A SPEED CHANGE, NOT FROM A SOURCE
  1. Pick the eval set before the change. One general set (gsm8k or MMLU) plus one matched to your traffic (HumanEval for code), and a few hundred real prompts sampled from production.
  2. Run lm-eval against both servers, same prompts, same settings: lm_eval --model local-completions --tasks gsm8k --model_args model=M,base_url=http://HOST:8000/v1/completions. Ship only if recovery is within your bar (for example ≥ 99% for an 8-bit change) on each task, not just on average.
  3. Diff greedy outputs. Temperature 0 on the production sample: first baseline vs baseline to measure the noise floor, then baseline vs candidate. Read the prompts that diverge early.
  4. For quantization, also look at a quality distribution (a reward model or judge over many responses) and at long inputs with outliers such as dates and numbers, which is where Llama 3 saw FP8 break.
  5. Check the latency SLO at P50, P90 and P99 for TTFT, TPS and end-to-end, at the batch sizes you run (benchmarking). Kiely's §7.4.3 dashboard adds error rates, queue depth and utilization.
  6. Canary it: 1% then 5% with at least two warm replicas, watch errors, latency and your quality signal for a full daily cycle, then 25%, 50%, 100%. Keep the old version ready to take traffic back.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Big-bang cut-over

Every user hits a bad version at once, and in-flight requests die mid-answer. At 7.9 GPU failures a day on 16k GPUs, you also can't wait for a quiet moment.

✗ Blue-green on a big fleet

Safe and instant to roll back, but it needs a second full fleet: another 100 GPUs for 100. Fine for one node, too expensive at scale.

✗ Ship on a benchmark score

An average can hide a broken slice. Llama 3's uncapped FP8 kept strong benchmark scores while "occasionally" producing corrupted responses.

✓ Spares, canary and eval gates

Small replicas plus a warm spare keep the fleet above four nines (our model). A canary costs about 4 extra GPUs in 100, and evals plus a greedy diff catch quality changes before users do.

Is a canary always the right rollout?

No. A canary only catches what it can measure at the traffic it gets. Low traffic: 1% of a few hundred requests an hour is too little to see a 1-point quality drop, so use shadow traffic or an offline eval on logged prompts. Tiny deployments: blue-green on one 8-GPU node costs 8 GPUs for an hour and gives a clean, instant rollback, which is often simpler. Changes that are exact by construction, like switching to Leviathan-style speculative decoding with verified rejection sampling, need a latency check more than a quality one, though a buggy implementation is still a reason to run the evals.

The availability numbers above are also only as good as their inputs. Kiely's 50,000 GPU-hours is one training cluster's average, the repair and failover times are illustrative, and failures in real fleets cluster by node, batch of hardware and age. Use the lab to test your own numbers, and trust your own fleet's failure log once you have one.

Understand and remember

  • Failures arrive at a steady rate per GPU-hour → a 16,384-GPU job fails every ~3.1 hours → training is synchronous, so each failure costs the work since the last checkpoint plus a full restart → checkpoint every τ* ≈ √(2CM) and cut C and R (Llama 3 > 90% effective)
  • Serving replicas are independent → a failure removes only the group in lockstep with the dead GPU → smaller groups lose fewer users, but GPU health is node-level (cordon the node) → spares, not layout, decide availability (TP8 × 2: 99.825% → 99.998% with one spare node)
  • Every speed trick changes arithmetic or token choice → quantization and Medusa-style typical acceptance can change answers; verified speculative decoding can't (in distribution) → evals on general + matched tasks, a greedy diff against a baseline-vs-baseline noise floor, then a canary
  • A canary shifts a growing share of live traffic to the new version while autoscaling shrinks the old → only a few extra replicas (+4 GPUs of 100) vs blue-green's full second fleet (+100) → but it only catches what its traffic volume can measure
  • detail that matters Floating-point addition isn't associative → batch size and kernel choice change reduction order → temperature-0 outputs vary run to run (80 unique completions of 1,000) → measure the baseline against itself before judging a candidate

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Double the training fleet from 16,384 to 32,768 GPUs, same failure rate per GPU, C = 1 min and R = 5 min. Commit: does effective training time stay near 87%, and what happens to the best checkpoint interval?

Reveal

It drops to about 80%. The job's mean time between failures halves (186 → 93 min), so τ* = √(2 × 1 × 93) = 13.6 min (from 19.3), and the waste is 1 ÷ 14.6 + (6.8 + 5) ÷ 93 ≈ 19.6% before planned stops (our arithmetic with the card's model). Bigger fleets must shrink checkpoint and restart times just to stand still.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

(1) A 64-GPU serving fleet at Kiely's rate: expected failures per year, and the chance of a failure-free year (Poisson, e^−λ). (2) A 4,096-GPU training job at the same rate: the job's mean time between failures in minutes, the best checkpoint interval for C = 1 min, and the effective training time for R = 5 and R = 15 min (ignore planned stops). (3) A canary at 5% on Kiely's 100 GPUs split into 25 TP4 replicas with a 2-replica floor: how many GPUs are in use?

Done when: (1) 64 × 8,760 ÷ 50,000 = 11.2 failures per year; e^−11.2 ≈ 0.001%, effectively 0. (2) M = 50,000 ÷ 4,096 h = 732 min; τ* = √(2 × 732) = 38.3 min; effective 94.2% (R = 5) and 92.8% (R = 15). (3) 24 old + 2 new replicas = 104 GPUs, +4.

Hint

Waste ≈ C ÷ (τ + C) + (τ/2 + R) ÷ M, the card's form.
