Before this: $ per million tokens and the batch dial

Choosing hardware: FLOPS, bandwidth, memory and price

Also called GPU selection, reading a spec sheet, or "which instance should we rent?". The consumer-side card, Inference hardware, showed that bandwidth sets a single user's speed. This card goes deeper, from the operator's side. It covers the four numbers on a datacenter spec sheet, the traps in how vendors print them, which number binds in which regime, and what a token costs on each GPU. It follows Kiely's Inference Engineering §3.1–3.4 and the vendor datasheets. Every tok/s and $/M figure is our ceiling from course 2's step formula (labs/hw-compare.py), not a measurement.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Spec sheets print four numbers, vendors print them in ways that flatter (sparse FLOPS, mixed footnotes), and the cheapest GPU per hour can make the most expensive tokens. Without knowing which spec your workload runs out of, you'll rent the wrong chip.

Where it fits: Builds on cost-per-token ($/M = price ÷ tokens per hour) and course 2's step formula, where each term is paid in a different resource. This card runs that formula on eight GPUs and prices the resource that binds.

In one breath

A GPU sells you four things: dense FLOPS (math), HBM bandwidth (bytes per second), HBM capacity (how many sequences fit), and a scale-up link (how fast GPUs talk to each other). Work out which one your workload runs out of first, then divide the hourly price by that resource. Decode runs out of bandwidth and memory, prefill runs out of FLOPS, and big MoE models run out of memory and interconnect.

Which spec binds, in one formula

Course 2's step-time formula already says which spec matters. Each term is paid in a different resource:

Where Bkv < Bcrit (H100, L40S), HBM fills before the weight matmuls can become compute-bound. On H200, B200, GB200 and the MI3xx cards it's the other way round. But the KV read still takes 77–84% of the step at Bkv, so bandwidth stays the main cost. Attention never becomes compute-bound in decode (course 2).

B200 costs 1.68× an H100 per hour ($6.69 ÷ $3.99) and gives 2.46× the tokens at Bkv (24,575 ÷ 9,973), so each token is 1.46× cheaper. The cheapest card per hour, the L40S, ties the A100 for the most expensive tokens here. Its bandwidth is a quarter of an H100's, and its 48 GB fits half the batch.

Published numbers, and how to read them

MLPerf Inference v5.0, Llama-2-70B, offline (NVIDIA blog, Table 1) 8×H200 34,988 tok/s = 126.0M tok/hr 8 × $4.59 = $36.72/hr → $0.29/M 8×B200 98,858 tok/s = 355.9M tok/hr 8 × $6.69 = $53.52/hr → $0.15/M B200 ÷ H200: 2.83× the throughput for 1.46× the price NVIDIA H100 and DGX B200 pages, citing SemiAnalysis InferenceX, GPT-OSS-120B H100 + vLLM $0.09/M at 66 tok/s per user B200 + TensorRT-LLM $0.02/M at 55 tok/s per user → "roughly 4.5x cheaper" $ per TB/s-hour alone: $1.19 ÷ $0.84 = 1.42× (our inference)

The MLPerf $/M figures are our arithmetic (the H200 line is the source map's), and they are benchmark best cases: tuned engine, fixed dataset, full batches. The vendor claim also swaps the engine and the per-user speed. The same pages credit software with a 5× drop on Blackwell alone ("over Blackwell's own launch-day costs"), so price per byte explains only about 1.4× of the 4.5×. Precision, engine, batch and interactivity can each move $/M as much as the chip does. Compare two GPUs on the same engine and settings, measured yourself (benchmarking). We don't record which precision each MLPerf submission used, so check the results table before you compare them.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Pick the biggest headline TFLOPS

Headline numbers are 2:4 sparse (2× too high), and decode barely uses FLOPS anyway. H100 and H200 have identical FLOPS but decode 205 vs 294 tok/s.

✗ Pick the cheapest $/GPU-hour

The $1.09 L40S ties the A100 for the most expensive tokens in the table ($0.142/M vs B200's $0.076). Price per hour means nothing without tokens per hour.

✗ Trust vendor $/M comparisons

The 4.5× B200-vs-H100 claim also swaps vLLM for TensorRT-LLM and changes per-user speed. Only part of it is the hardware.

✓ Price the binding resource, then measure

Name the regime, divide $/hr by the spec that binds (TB/s, GB, FLOPS, link), and shortlist. Then benchmark your model on your engine at your batch.

So is the newest, biggest GPU always the right buy?

No. Our ceilings assume 100% of peak bandwidth, a mature software stack and a full batch, and several things break those assumptions:

  • Low or bursty traffic. If you can't fill Bkv, you pay batch-1 prices ($3.79–6.21/M) against market API prices of $0.04–0.22/M output for this model ($ per million tokens). Use an API, or a fraction of a GPU: Kiely §3.2.2 says an H100 MIG slice beats an L40 "for the same memory footprint".
  • Small models. A 3B model can't use a B200's 180 GB or FLOPS. Kiely suggests MIG, or L4-class cards for embeddings.
  • New or non-CUDA hardware. Kiely §3.2.4: new architectures "take a year or so to fully saturate", and §3.4 lists "Software" as the common problem for non-NVIDIA chips. MI300X/MI325X memory looks great on paper; price your own engine's measured tok/s on them, not our ceiling.
  • How you buy. Spot, reserved and multi-node prices reorder the table. Lambda's 16-GPU H100 cluster is $6.16/GPU-hr vs $3.99 for one 8× instance, and AWS p5 on demand is $6.88 (autoscaling covers paying for idle).

Understand and remember

  • Decode at small batch pays W/BW per step → tok/s ≈ BW ÷ W → buy TB/s (H100 205 vs B200 490 tok/s at B = 1)
  • Decode at big batch is capped by HBM left after the weights (B_kv) and spends 66–84% of each step reading KV → buy GB, then TB/s → $/M falls with $ per TB/s-hour (B200 $0.84 vs H100 $1.19: $0.076 vs $0.111 per M)
  • Prefill shares each weight read across thousands of prompt tokens → compute-bound, tok/s ≈ C ÷ 2N → buy dense FLOPS; a big MoE or dense model first has to fit, and split models talk every layer → buy GB and interconnect
  • NVIDIA's headline FLOPS are 2:4-sparse, but inference is dense → divide by 2 before any comparison → otherwise B_crit, speed-of-light floors and utilization claims are all off by 2×
  • detail that matters B_crit = C ÷ BW for BF16 because the 2 FLOPs and 2 bytes per parameter cancel → FP8 weights halve it → where B_kv < B_crit (H100, L40S) the batch hits memory before compute, but KV reads dominate either way

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

A workload is almost all prefill: long documents in, a few tokens out. Which is cheaper per million input tokens for the 8B, an H100 at $3.99 or an L40S at $1.09? Commit before computing.

Reveal

The L40S. Prefill is compute-bound, so input tok/s ≈ C ÷ 2N: 362 TF gives 22,540 tok/s and $0.0134/M, against the H100's 61,582 tok/s and $0.0180/M (both at 100% of dense peak, our arithmetic). The card's $ per BF16 PF-hour says the same (3.01 vs 4.03). The same L40S ties for the most expensive decode tokens: the binding resource flips the ranking.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Redo the worked example at 8k context (KV per sequence = 8,192 × 128 KiB = 1.074 GB) for three GPUs: H100 ($3.99, 80 GB, 3.35 TB/s), H200 ($4.59, 141 GB, 4.8 TB/s) and B200 ($6.69, 180 GB, 8.0 TB/s, 2,250 TF). For each, compute B_kv, the step at B_kv, tok/s and $/M. Which GPU wins, and by how much more or less than at 2k?

Done when: H100: 59 sequences, 23.7 ms, 2,489 tok/s, $0.445/M. H200: 116, 29.3 ms, 3,960 tok/s, $0.322/M. B200: 152, 22.4 ms, 6,783 tok/s, $0.274/M. The B200 still wins, by 1.62× over the H100 against 1.46× at 2k, because longer context makes memory capacity and bandwidth matter even more.

Hint

B_kv = ⌊(HBM − 16.06 GB) ÷ 1.074 GB⌋; step = B_kv × 1.074 GB ÷ BW + max(2·B·N/C, 16.06 GB ÷ BW).
