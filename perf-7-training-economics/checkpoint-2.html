Checkpoint 2: what a served token really costs

Ties together $ per million tokens, choosing hardware, autoscaling and cold starts, KV-aware routing, reliability and quality and AI-written kernels: every lever that moves the price of a token after the model is trained, and what each one risks.

How to use this page. Close the six cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure; it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you have to work out which idea applies before you can answer.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?A customer runs Llama-3.1-8B on its own dedicated H100 replica, all day, and averages 200 output tokens per second. Their finance team computes $5.54 per million tokens and asks why a public API charges $0.04–0.08. Which idea explains the gap, and what do you recommend?
    Check
    • utilization: $3.99 an hour is paid whether the GPU makes 200 or 6,450 tok/s, so $/M = $3.99 ÷ (200 × 3,600 ÷ 10⁶) = $5.54
    • light traffic also keeps the batch tiny, so each step makes few tokens
    • recommendation: a pay-per-token API until volume fills a replica (Kiely's advice); a pooled provider batches many customers onto one fleet

    Missed it? Reopen autoscaling, the one-warm-replica table, and $ per million tokens, "Utilization".

  2. At batch 128 and 2k context, what is the 8B's step time, total and per-user tokens per second, and $/M on an H100 at $3.99?
    Check
    • step = 128 × 0.080 + max(2.08, 4.79) ≈ 15.05 ms
    • 128 ÷ 15.05 ms = 8,505 tok/s in total, 66 tok/s per user
    • $3.99 ÷ (8,505 × 3,600) × 10⁶ = $0.13 per M

    Missed it? Reopen $ per million tokens, "Worked example: the batch dial".

  3. which concept?To fix one overloaded replica, an ops team switches from hashing on the system prompt to round-robin. The hot spot disappears, but mean TTFT on multi-turn chats gets worse and prefill GPU time rises. What happened, and what router would keep both wins?
    Check
    • round-robin sends a conversation's next turn to the replica holding its history only 1/N of the time (12.5% at N = 8), so most turns recompute their history
    • prefix hashing kept caches warm but piled 53% of traffic on one replica
    • a cost-function router scores cached blocks against queued and running work: most of the hits without hot spots (the lab's best p99)

    Missed it? Reopen KV-aware routing, the four-router table.

  4. H100 and H200 have identical dense FLOPS. Why does the H200 decode the 8B at 294 tok/s at batch 1 against the H100's 205, and which of them is cheaper per token at its full batch?
    Check
    • at batch 1 each step is mostly the weight read, so tok/s ≈ bandwidth ÷ weight bytes: 4.8 vs 3.35 TB/s
    • at the KV-limited batch the H200 fits 465 sequences vs 238, and costs $0.092 vs $0.111 per M
    • it's cheaper per TB/s-hour ($0.96 vs $1.19), which is the price that binds for decode

    Missed it? Reopen choosing hardware, "Which spec binds" and the worked example.

  5. which concept?An AI-written decode-attention kernel for one layer of the 8B at batch 64 and 2k context (537 MB of KV to read) reports 0.05 ms on an H100. The reviewer checks it against the dense BF16 peak and then the sparse one. What does each check say?
    Check
    • decode attention is memory-bound (about 4 FLOP/byte), so the floor is bytes ÷ bandwidth: 537 MB ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 0.160 ms
    • 0.05 ms is below the floor: a bug or a cheat, flag it
    • the peak FLOPS, dense or sparse, doesn't enter a memory-bound floor, so both checks agree

    Missed it? Reopen AI-written kernels, "The speed-of-light check".

  6. You're rolling an FP8 version of a model onto 100 GPUs (25 TP4 replicas). Compare blue-green with a canary in extra GPUs, and list what must pass before the canary reaches 100%.
    Check
    • blue-green: a second full fleet, +100 GPUs
    • canary at 5% with a 2-replica floor: 24 old + 2 new replicas, 104 GPUs, +4
    • before ramping: evals on a general and a matched task within your recovery bar, a greedy diff against a baseline-vs-baseline noise floor, latency at P50/P90/P99, and a quality distribution on long inputs with outliers (where uncapped FP8 broke for Llama 3)

    Missed it? Reopen reliability and quality, sections 4 and 5.

  7. Why are input tokens priced far below output tokens, and how big is the gap in our 8B model at the largest batch?
    Check
    • prefill runs a whole prompt per weight read, far above B_crit, so it's compute-bound: about 61.6k tok/s, $0.018 per M at peak
    • decode makes one token per sequence per step and is bandwidth-bound: $0.111 per M at B = 238
    • 6.2× at the largest batch, 300× at batch 1

    Missed it? Reopen $ per million tokens, "Why output tokens cost more than input tokens".

  8. which concept?The weights now load from an in-region store in 3.2 seconds, yet a new replica still takes over half a minute to serve. Where is the rest going, and what fixes it?
    Check
    • engine startup: KV-cache allocation, torch.compile and CUDA-graph capture (30 s in the card's tuned setup, illustrative)
    • it runs before the first request is served, so all of it is in the cold start
    • fix: cache the compiled artifacts and graphs (valid only for the same GPU type, CUDA and dependencies), and keep a warm node pool and cached image

    Missed it? Reopen autoscaling and cold starts, "The four parts of a cold start" and "Engine startup".

3 · Project: a cost model for one real service

What it reinforces: the $/M formula and the batch dial ($ per million tokens), the binding resource per GPU (choosing hardware), utilization under autoscaling (autoscaling), the cost of a spare (reliability) and kernel efficiency as a multiplier (AI-written kernels). Routing enters as the input-side saving (KV-aware routing).

service: Llama-3.1-8B BF16, 2k context, promise ≥ 100 output tok/s per user step = B·KV_seq/BW + max(2·B·N/C, W/BW) N = 8.03e9, W = 16.06 GB, KV_seq = 268.4 MB H100: 3.35 TB/s, 989 TF, 80 GB, $3.99/hr B200: 8.0 TB/s, 2,250 TF, 180 GB, $6.69/hr $/M = price × 10⁶ ÷ (3,600 × tok/s × u)

Write a script of about 40 lines that takes a GPU, a per-user promise, a utilization and a kernel efficiency, and prints $/M. Then:

  1. Operating point. For each GPU, find the largest batch that still gives ≥ 100 tok/s per user (and fits in memory). Report the step, total tok/s and $/M at u = 1.
  2. Utilization. Apply the autoscaled day's 68.4% utilization (min 1 replica) to both. (For the B200 this is an assumption: fewer, larger replicas would change the real figure.)
  3. Kernels. Now assume your engine reaches 85% of the ceiling (step ÷ 0.85). New $/M for both.
  4. Reliability. On the H100 day (50 GPU-hours for 794.16M tokens), add one warm spare H100 all day. New $ per day and $/M.
  5. Routing. From the routing card: what does a cost-function router save per million requests on this model, and why is it small next to the lines above?
Done when: your script reproduces the H100's B = 64, $0.172 row from the course, prints all the numbers above, and you can rank the five effects by size in one sentence. Then compare with the check.
Check: the numbers (our arithmetic with the cards' formulas)
  • Operating point: H100 B = 64, step 9.92 ms, 6,450 tok/s, $0.172/M. B200 B = 238 (it fits 610), step 9.99 ms, 23,816 tok/s, $0.078/M.
  • At 68.4% utilization: H100 $0.251/M, B200 $0.114/M.
  • With 85% kernels: H100 $0.296/M, B200 $0.134/M.
  • A spare H100: 50 + 24 = 74 GPU-hours, $295.26 a day, $0.372/M (from $0.251): reliability is not free, so size the spare to the fleet.
  • Routing: about $10 per million requests at the prefill floor (0.56B prompt tokens avoided), because 8B prefill costs only $0.018 per M input; its real value here is TTFT and freed prefill capacity.
  • Ranking for this service: the GPU choice at a fixed per-user promise (~2.2×) and utilization (~1.5×) beat kernel efficiency (~1.18×), and the spare costs ~1.5× on a small fleet.
