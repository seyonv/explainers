4D parallelism: TP, PP, CP and EP in training

Also called model parallelism, 3D parallelism or PTD-P (Megatron: pipeline, tensor, data), 4D (Llama 3 adds context) or 5D (the Ultra-Scale Playbook adds experts). Layouts are written like "TP8 PP16 DP64". This card follows the Playbook's TP, CP, PP and EP chapters and its "5D parallelism in a nutshell", the Scaling Book Part 5, Narayanan et al. 2021 §2, Llama 3 §3.3.2 and DeepSeek-V3 §3.2. Course 6 already covered each split for serving; this card is about what changes when there's a backward pass.

In one breath

One GPU can't hold a big model's training state, and one GPU would take centuries to do the FLOPs. So training splits the work along every axis it has: tensor (inside each matrix, over fast NVLink), sequence/context (along the tokens), pipeline (blocks of layers, across nodes), expert (whole experts, for MoE) and data (copies of all of the above, working on different batches). Each axis runs on the network tier that fits its traffic, and the job is choosing the degrees so memory fits, communication hides behind compute, and the pipeline rarely stands idle.

What training adds to the course 6 picture

  • A backward pass. It's about 2× the forward FLOPs (tb ≈ 2 tf, the Playbook's assumption), and every tensor-parallel collective in the forward pass has a partner in the backward pass. In Megatron I that's the f/g pair: identity forward and all-reduce backward, and the reverse.
  • Activations held until the backward pass. Serving discards activations after each layer and keeps only the KV cache. Training has to keep them (or recompute them) until the gradient comes back, so a pipeline stage holds one set per micro-batch still in flight. That's why pipeline schedules exist.
  • Optimizer state. 16 bytes per parameter (ZeRO's convention: 2 BF16 weights + 2 BF16 grads + 12 for FP32 master weights and Adam m, v; 20 with FP32 gradients), not the 2 bytes of BF16 serving. 405B needs 6,480 GB of state against 810 GB of weights. Data-parallel ranks shard it with ZeRO/FSDP.
  • A synchronized step. Every data-parallel copy has to finish its gradients before the optimizer steps, so the pipeline flushes each step. Serving pipelines never drain (course 6), so "bubbles are not an issue during inference" (Llama 3 §6).

Everyday version: a team copy-editing a huge book. TP puts 8 editors on the same page at one desk. PP gives each desk a range of chapters and passes pages along. CP splits one very long chapter by paragraph. EP sends each paragraph to the specialist for its topic. DP runs many identical teams on different books, and they compare notes at the end of each day.

1 · TP + sequence parallelism, inside a node

Tensor parallelism splits the MLP column-then-row and attention by heads (course 6 has the mechanics). Its training problem is memory: TP shards the matmul activations, but LayerNorm and dropout need the full hidden vector, so every GPU still stores full-size copies of those. Sequence parallelism splits exactly those regions along the sequence instead, so "the maximum activation size is reduced to b·s·h/tp" (Playbook). It costs no extra bandwidth: SP does two all-gathers and two reduce-scatters where TP did two all-reduces, and an all-reduce is a reduce-scatter plus an all-gather. The Playbook fits a 70B model at 16k sequence length with TP+SP = 16.

activation bytes per layer (Korthikanti Table 2, no attention-score term) TP only sbh·(10 + 24/t) = sbh·13.00 at t = 8 → 1.74 GB per layer per sequence TP + SP sbh·34/t = sbh·4.25 at t = 8 → 0.57 GB (3.06× less) s = 8,192, h = 16,384 (405B): GPT's formula on Llama's shape, illustrative

Keep TP inside the node. Both sources say it and give the reason: TP's collectives sit on the critical path and can't be hidden. The Playbook measured "significant drops when moving from TP=8 to TP=16, and an even steeper decline from TP=16 to TP=32" as traffic moves from NVLink to the inter-node network, and Narayanan's takeaway #1 is TP up to the GPUs per server, then pipeline.

2 · Pipeline schedules: the bubble and the memory

With p stages, the first micro-batch has to walk forward through all of them before the last GPU can start, and the backward pass has to walk back. Splitting the batch into m micro-batches spreads that fill and drain over more work. Interleaving gives each GPU v smaller chunks of layers (layers 1–2 and 9–10 instead of 1–4), which makes each fill step v times shorter.

AFAB → 1F1B changes memory, not time. AFAB (the GPipe schedule) runs every forward before any backward, so GPU 0 holds all m = 8 micro-batches' activations. 1F1B starts each backward as early as it can, so it "only need[s] to store activations for p micro-batches … instead of m" (Playbook), and 4 here. Both steps take 33 units. The memory saved is then spent on more micro-batches, which is what shrinks the bubble. PP alone saves no activation memory: each GPU holds "PP × (activs / PP) ≈ activs". Interleaving cuts the bubble by v, but it costs v× more point-to-point traffic, requires m to be a multiple of p, and in our sim it holds 5.5 micro-batches' worth at GPU 0 instead of 4.

The Playbook calls near-zero-bubble schedules ILP problems: "too complex for us to give code snippets". DualPipe's two-way schedule is on the DeepSeek-V3 II card.

Why PP is the cross-node axis. PP sends one activation per stage boundary; TP sends collectives in every layer. The Playbook's benchmark: with few micro-batches, 1F1B "only drops by 14% when scaling from one node (p = 8) to two nodes (p = 16)", while TP "typically sees around 43% performance degradation in similar cross-node scenarios".

3 · Context parallelism for long sequences

Activation memory grows with sequence length, and at 128k tokens even TP+SP with full recompute runs out, because each layer-boundary checkpoint still grows linearly with s. CP splits the sequence across GPUs for the whole model. MLPs and LayerNorms don't care, because tokens are independent there; only attention needs every earlier token's K and V. Ring attention passes K/V chunks around a ring while each GPU computes on the chunk it already has. With a causal mask, a naive split is lopsided: the GPU with the last tokens does the most work. Zig-zag gives GPU i chunks i and 2·CP−1−i, pairing an early chunk with a late one.

causal attention work per GPU, CP = 4, in (S/4)² score blocks (lab §3) sequential chunks 0.5 1.5 2.5 3.5 slowest / mean = 1.75 zig-zag (i, 7−i) 2.0 2.0 2.0 2.0 slowest / mean = 1.00

Llama 3 uses exactly that 2×CP chunk assignment, but all-gathers K/V instead of passing it around a ring. The gather sits on the critical path, but GQA's K/V are small compared with Q and attention is O(S²) against O(S) for the gather, and it copes more easily with document masks. Its CP16 row keeps 131,072 / 16 = 8,192 tokens per GPU, the same as the 8K-sequence rows. Course 4's long-context card has ring attention's overlap condition.

4 · Expert parallelism in training

EP puts whole experts on different GPUs and routes tokens to them with all-to-all. It's lighter than TP (no matmul is split), but it only touches the MoE layers, so it's paired with DP for everything else (Playbook). Three things differ from serving (course 6). The backward pass sends the dispatch and combine traffic again, in reverse (our reading of the forward/backward symmetry). Routing balance affects convergence as well as speed (MoE basics). And the layout is chosen for training throughput: DeepSeek-V3 trained with PP16 × EP64 (8 nodes) × ZeRO-1 DP on 2,048 H800s, "without using costly Tensor Parallelism", capping each token at 4 nodes and hiding the all-to-all inside DualPipe. Its serving layout is different: TP4 + SP attention, EP32 prefill and EP320 decode.

The spare memory is why Llama 3 could train 8K sequences "without activation checkpointing". (3) Megatron's "52% of peak" is HFU-like, so don't put it next to PaLM's 46.2% MFU (compute budgets). (4) DeepSeek-V3 pre-trained at a 4K sequence length (its §4.2), half of Llama 3's 8K.

6 · When each axis runs out of bandwidth (Scaling Book Part 5)

The Scaling Book derives, for a TPU ring, that TP stays compute-bound only while F > Y · C/W (F = MLP width, Y = TP degree, C = FLOP/s per chip, W = bidirectional link bandwidth). That puts the limit at "between 8 and 16-way" for most models, whatever the batch or precision. FSDP stays compute-bound only while the tokens per chip exceed C/W, about 2,550 on TPU v5p; mixing in TP drops that to 2550²/2F, "as little as 100 per chip". The book's GPU chapter redoes the TP bound with H100 numbers: "Within a node, this gives us about F / 2200 or F / 2475 beyond a node. For F=28000 like LLaMA-3, this is about 11-way TP (or rounding down, about 8-way, which is how large a node is)." Course 6's Megatron II walks through that derivation.

Rules of thumb, all from the Playbook: TP ≤ 8, inside a node. ZeRO-3 or PP across nodes: "TP will typically be kept for high-speed intra-node communications, while ZeRO-3 or PP can be used for … inter-node". DP ≤ ~512: "as a rule of thumb, DP shouldn't exceed 512", because past that the ring latency stops overlapping. ZeRO-1/2 combine easily with PP, but ZeRO-3 with PP rarely does. ZeRO-3 moves weights and wants a big micro-batch and sequence; PP moves activations and wants many accumulation steps.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Pure DP / ZeRO-3 on 16K GPUs

A fixed 16.8M-token batch leaves only 1,024 tokens per GPU to hide each step's weight and gradient traffic. The Playbook says pure DP/ZeRO-3 starts to become inefficient past ~512 GPUs.

✗ TP across nodes

Its collectives sit on the critical path of every layer. The Playbook measures ~43% degradation going cross-node, where PP loses 14%.

✗ One very deep pipeline

Stretching PP to fit 16K GPUs means few micro-batches per pipeline: PP16 with m = 16 idles 93.8% as long as it computes (1F1B, v = 1).

✓ Layered 4D: [TP, CP, PP, DP]

Each axis on the link that fits its traffic: TP on NVLink, CP and PP across nodes, FSDP outermost because it can prefetch. Llama 3 405B: 38–43% MFU on up to 16,384 H100s.

Does every training run need all four?

No. Under ~10B, the Playbook uses a single technique (ZeRO-3 or TP across 8 GPUs). DeepSeek-V3 skipped TP entirely: its memory savings let it run PP16 × EP64 × ZeRO-1. TPUs lean on FSDP + TP and treat pipelining as "somewhat less essential" (Scaling Book), because a pod's ICI mesh is large and uniform where GPU clusters have a fast node and a slow network. Each axis you add is another collective to tune and another way to fail.

The bubble formulas assume balanced stages and tb ≈ 2tf. Real stages aren't balanced: the first stage holds the embedding and the last computes the loss, which is why Llama 3 removes one layer from each. Zero-bubble schedules also cost memory (DualPipe keeps 2× parameters). And the 52% vs 43% comparison shows why to ask whether a utilization number counts recompute before you compare two runs.
