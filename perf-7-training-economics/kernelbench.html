AI-written kernels: KernelBench and the frontier

Also called LLM kernel generation, automated kernel optimization, or "can a model write CUDA?". KernelBench (Ouyang et al., Stanford and Princeton, arXiv 2502.10517, Feb 2025) is the standard yardstick. It has 250 PyTorch tasks in three levels, a metric called fastp, and a v0.1 release that closed the loopholes models found. This is the frontier your kernel-company job sits on. The hand-tuning ladder is in 3 · Writing fast GPU kernels. This card covers what happens when a model climbs it for you, and how to tell whether it really did.

In one breath

A language model gets a PyTorch module and must write a custom GPU kernel that does the same thing faster. The harness runs both on random inputs to check that the outputs match, times both, and counts a task as solved only if the new kernel is correct and faster than PyTorch by more than a factor p. One-shot, the best frontier models managed this on fewer than 20% of tasks. With compiler and profiler feedback they do much better. The hard part now is making sure a "win" isn't a cheat.

The task · from paper §3.1–3.3

The model may rewrite any part of the module in any language. Correctness is a random test, not a proof: 5 input sets per task (Appendix B). Timing is wall-clock over repeated trials. The paper's results use an L40S and FP32 (README: "all of our reported results are fp32").

The speed-of-light check

A kernel can't beat the hardware. Count the FLOPs and bytes the task must do, divide each by the GPU's peak rate, and whichever takes longer is the floor (course 2's roofline, as a time):

The sparse-vs-dense trap halves the floor only for compute-bound shapes. Decode attention is memory-bound (4 FLOP/byte), so the wrong peak changes nothing there. For it you need the bandwidth floor instead. Our arithmetic (labs/fast-p.py).

A result below tSoL is a bug or a cheat, never a win. A result above it isn't proven honest: a subtle cheat at 0.150 ms passes the check. So v0.1 pairs this check with scanning for torch/cuBLAS calls. The README points to an experimental roofline tool for the same purpose.

Why an inference company cares · our framing

Every ceiling in courses 2–7 assumes kernels that run at 100% of peak. Real kernels don't, and the gap between "our model" and a measured engine is mostly kernel quality: attention (FlashAttention-3/4, FlashInfer), fused GEMMs, quantized matmuls (quantization in serving). $/M is linear in step time, so each point of efficiency is a point off the price:

Llama-3.1-8B, H100, B = 64, 2k context, $3.99/GPU-hr (our model) step = B·KV_seq/BW + max(2BN/C, W/BW) kernels at 100% of ceiling step 9.92 ms 6,450 tok/s $0.172/M kernels at 90% step 11.02 ms 5,805 tok/s $0.191/M kernels at 80% step 12.40 ms 5,160 tok/s $0.215/M kernels at 70% step 14.17 ms 4,515 tok/s $0.245/M (+43%)

The 70–90% rows are illustrative efficiencies, not measurements of any engine. Course 3's ladder shows what closing that gap costs a human: siboehm's hand-written matmul went from 1.3% to 93.7% of cuBLAS over several weekends of work (matmul I → matmul IV). The bet behind AI-written kernels is that a model with a harness and a profiler can do that search cheaply, for every new model, shape and GPU. The paper's example: FlashAttention came five years after the Transformer, and FlashAttention-2 "degrades 47% in hardware utilization" moving from A100 to H100.

What's still hard

  • Level 3. The best result stays at 18% even with feedback. Whole models mean many kernels, choosing which to replace, and keeping numerics consistent across all of them.
  • Numerics. 5 random inputs catch most bugs (Appendix B saw no partial correctness) but prove nothing about edge values, other distributions or lower precision. v0.1 added mixed input distributions (torch.rand_mix). The paper's results are FP32; BF16/FP8 kernels need tolerances and correctness decisions that FP32 hides (our inference; see tensor cores and low precision).
  • Hardware-specific features. Among its standout kernels, the paper did not find a successful use of tensor-core instructions. Hopper's TMA and warp specialization, which course 3's overview names as the modern top of the ladder, go beyond wmma, so we infer they're harder still. A good kernel for one GPU isn't one for the next (36% vs 47% above).
  • The baseline moves. fast1 against PyTorch Eager is a floor. Production engines already ship tuned kernels, so the bar that matters to a serving company is "faster than what vLLM or SGLang runs today". This is our framing: the paper says p and the baseline should rise over time.

Why fastp beats the alternatives

✗ Correctness only (pass@k)

This is how code benchmarks usually score. A correct kernel that's 0.6× as fast as PyTorch passes, but it's useless: here, that's the layernorm row.

✗ Average speedup

One 9× outlier or one cheat inflates the mean, and failed tasks drop out of it. The flagged 2.40× matmul would have lifted it.

✗ Showcase kernels

"13× on a diagonal matmul" is real but hand-picked. The median correct kernel is slower than Eager on Levels 1 and 3.

✓ fastp + speed-of-light check

Correct AND faster, over every task including failures, at a bar you can raise. The SoL check keeps "faster" physically possible.

So can AI write our kernels now?

Not reliably, and not by itself. KernelBench's scores are fast1 against PyTorch Eager, in FP32, on an L40S, from before v0.1 fixed reward hacking and 43 flawed tasks. That's a lower bar than beating a tuned serving engine on an H100 in BF16 or FP8. When not to reach for it: for the standard ops (dense GEMM, attention on mainstream GPUs), a vendor library or an existing kernel (cuBLAS, FlashAttention, FlashInfer) or torch.compile is the first move, and it's hard to beat. Where AI generation looks most promising is the long tail: odd fusions, new architectures, new hardware with no library yet. It works best as a loop with compile, correctness and profiler feedback, plus speed-of-light and anti-cheat checks, and a human reviews what ships. That last part is our reading, not the paper's claim.
