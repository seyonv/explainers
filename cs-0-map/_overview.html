The core CS canon, mapped

Start here. This card shows the ten courses built from ljeng/cheat-sheet: what each one covers, which to read first, which ones to read for which interview, and where every section of the source ended up.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The source guide is 181 cards' worth of terse interview notes spread over ten courses. Without a map you read it in file order, hit a card that assumes heaps or SLO arithmetic you haven't met, and can't tell which third of it your next interview actually tests.

Where it fits: This is the series map, read before cs-1 or after the last course as a recap. It builds on nothing; every later course is one box on it, and each box's arrows are the Needs column from the series index.

Builds on: Choosing a data structure · Choosing an algorithm · Modelling problems as states · Framing and sizing a design · Choosing where to coordinate · Touching less data · Choosing a unit of execution · Counting, then conditioning · Tails, conditions and sums · Pricing and sizing risk

You'll meet: four independent tracks (coding, large-scale design, OS, research engineering) the Needs arrows as a dependency graph interview → reading order how each card is built (definition → example → 2×2 → clarification → Python) where each source page went, and which cards were written from references

By the end: Draw the ten courses as a dependency graph from memory, pick a valid reading order for a given interview, and say which cards rest on the source guide and which on outside references.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Which three courses have no incoming arrows, besides cs-1, and what does that mean for where you can start?
  2. cs-6 lists cs-2's sorting as something that helps, not something it needs. What would change on the map if that became a hard arrow?
  3. Why does cs-7 stand alone in its column, and why does the card still tell design candidates to read it beside cs-4 to cs-6?
  4. cs-10 needs both cs-8 and cs-9. Which ideas travel along each of those two arrows?
  5. 49 cards were written from references rather than the source. Why does the card say so in every such footer instead of blending them in?

In one breath

The source guide is a terse set of interview notes: problem statements, one-line solutions and dense paper summaries. This series turns every section of it into 181 cards across 10 courses, plus one overview card per course. Every code example is Python you can run. The source's Java and C++ were rewritten, not transliterated. Where the source has only a heading (all of Operating Systems, all of Financial Mathematics, and a few smaller gaps), the card is written from standard references and says so in its footer. Every number is either computed exactly or measured on an Apple M3, and the card says which.

When this doesn't apply

This is interview preparation built from one person's study guide, not a textbook. It follows the source's choice of topics and problems, so it leaves out much of what a course would cover (proofs of correctness, security, most of a networking or databases syllabus) and goes deep wherever the source's author did.

Cards marked as supplemented are ours: the topic comes from the source's heading, but the content, examples and numbers were chosen for this series from the references each footer names. Timings are from one laptop and will differ on yours; the ratios are what carry over.

Open each step in order · why this works
1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • Each course lists only the courses it truly needs → the Needs arrows form a small graph with no cycles → any order that respects the arrows is valid (a topological order) → you can start a track at any course with no arrows in (cs-1, cs-4, cs-7, cs-8)
  • The four tracks share no hard arrows → studying one never blocks on another → an interview picks a track (coding → cs-1→2→3, design → cs-4→5→6, quant → cs-8→9→10) → cross-track links are only "also skim" suggestions
  • The source has empty headings (all of OS, all of financial maths, 24 scattered cards) → those cards are written from standard references → the footer says so → you know which claims come from the source guide and which from the series, which is what makes the corrections card possible
  • Every card follows one build (definition → worked example with real numbers → 2×2 of losers vs winner → when it doesn't apply → runnable Python) → you know where on any card to find the mechanism, the tradeoff and the limit → you can skim a course fast and retrieve from the same slots
  • detail that matters Timings come from one Apple M3 in Python → absolute numbers won't match your machine → compare ratios between variants on a card, not raw milliseconds

2 · Compress write it yourself first

Course-level compress: without looking, draw the whole series as one graph. Ten boxes in four columns, the Needs arrows between them, the dashed "helps" links, and a label on each track for the interview it serves. Then write one sentence on why any arrow-respecting order works.

On paper or in your notes, in your own words, without scrolling up. Aim for the smallest thing that still explains it.

Then compare with this checklist
  • coding: cs-1 → cs-2 → cs-3 (cs-1's heaps help cs-3's Dijkstra)
  • design: cs-4 → cs-5 → cs-6 (cs-2's sorting helps cs-6's MapReduce)
  • OS: cs-7 alone, read beside the design track
  • research: cs-8 → cs-9 → cs-10, plus cs-8 → cs-10 directly
  • no arrows between tracks, so the graph has four components and many valid orders
  • which courses are written wholly from references (cs-7, cs-10)

Missing a point? Reread only the section about it, not the whole card.

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain to a friend starting interview prep why the series can say "read anytime" about some courses but insists on order within a track.
    Check
    • the Needs arrows only exist inside tracks
    • a course with no incoming arrow (cs-1, cs-4, cs-7, cs-8) can be read first
    • inside a track each course uses the previous one's tools: Big-O and heaps, requirements and SLO arithmetic, MGFs and conditioning
    • cross-track links are "helps", never "needs"
  2. mechanism Walk through how you'd turn "I have a quant interview and a system design interview" into one reading order using only the map.
    Check
    • pick the two tracks: cs-4 → cs-5 → cs-6 and cs-8 → cs-9 → cs-10
    • the tracks are independent, so interleave them or read one then the other
    • within each, respect the arrows (cs-10 last, after both cs-8 and cs-9)
    • optionally add cs-7 beside the design track; total 53 + 47 = 100 cards, or 113 with cs-7
  3. tradeoff You have one week before a coding round and can't read all 68 coding cards. What does the map suggest you give up, and what does that cost you?
    Check
    • start from the three starter cards (testing, binary search on the answer, recursion to DP) instead of reading in order
    • skip whole groups rather than skimming everything
    • the cost: later cards assume earlier tools, so a skipped prerequisite shows up as a card you can't follow
    • each card's "Needs first" line tells you which gap to repair
  4. concept Which parts of the series would you trust least if the source guide were your only reference, and how does the map tell you?
    Check
    • cards written from references (all of cs-7 and cs-10, plus 24 others) have no source content to check against
    • their footers say the heading was empty
    • the source-derived cards are checked against the source, and its errors listed on the corrections card

Why this step?

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Topological sort and cycle detection: The map's reading orders vs Kahn's algorithm on course prerequisites: what is the map's Needs column in that card's terms, and what would a cycle in it mean for a reader?

Check
  • each course is a node; each Needs entry is an edge that must point forward
  • a valid reading order is a topological order; cs-1, cs-4, cs-7 and cs-8 start with in-degree 0
  • many orders are valid because the four tracks are separate components
  • a cycle (A needs B, B needs A) would leave both stuck with in-degree 1: no valid order, so neither could be read first

vs What the source got wrong: This card maps where each part of the source went; the corrections card audits how right each part was. Which question does each answer, and why do you need the map before reading the audit?

Check
  • map: which course covers a topic and what to read first
  • audit: which cards fix a source error and how it was checked
  • the audit is grouped by course, so it reads as a tour of the map
  • the map's supplemented-card count explains why cs-7 and cs-10 have no rows in the audit

vs Choosing a data structure: The series map vs a single course's overview: what does each organise by, and when would you open one rather than the other?

Check
  • the series map organises courses by track and prerequisite
  • a course overview organises one course's cards by the decision they support (for cs-1: which structure to reach for and what each one costs)
  • open the map to choose what to study; open a course overview to choose which card answers a problem in front of you

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose cs-6 turned cs-2's sorting from "helps" into a hard Need. Before counting: how many cards would a reader have to finish before opening cs-6, compared with today, and what happens to the claim that the four tracks are independent?

Reveal

Today cs-6 needs only cs-4 and cs-5: 16 + 20 = 36 cards. With a hard arrow from cs-2 it also needs cs-2 and, through it, cs-1: 36 + 20 + 21 = 77 cards, more than double. The coding and design tracks would join into one component, so a design-only candidate could no longer skip the coding track.

Why this step?

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Plan a 12-week study schedule for a candidate with a system design round and a quant round, at about 10 cards a week. Use the map's counts and arrows: list the courses in a valid order, total the cards, decide whether cs-7 fits, and name the first card you'd open in each track.

Done when: Your order respects every Needs arrow (cs-4 before cs-5 before cs-6; cs-8 before cs-9 before cs-10); design plus quant is 53 + 47 = 100 cards (10 weeks), and adding cs-7 makes 113 (about 11.3 weeks), which still fits in 12.

Hint

The two tracks share no arrows, so alternating a design week and a quant week is as valid as finishing one track first.
