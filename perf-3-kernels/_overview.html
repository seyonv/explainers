The kernel optimization loop

Overview of perf-3 (also called GPU kernel tuning, the roofline method, or "speed of light" analysis). It ties together the course's four walked-through sources: Harris's transpose and reduction, siboehm's matmul worklog, the Triton tutorials, and Volkov on occupancy. Most source numbers come from older GPUs (G80, M2050, A6000, V100), and each is named with its GPU. H100 and T4 numbers are our recomputation.

In one breath

A kernel can't finish faster than the larger of two floors: the time to do its math at peak, and the time to move the bytes it actually moves at peak bandwidth. Then add whatever the GPU spends idle: launches, stalls, waiting on latency. Almost every trick in this course does one of four things. It moves fewer bytes (coalescing, tiling, fusion, lower precision), makes the math faster (tensor cores, FP8), keeps enough work in flight to hide latency (occupancy, ILP, more work per thread), or removes overhead (CUDA graphs). The loop is always the same: measure, put the kernel on the roofline, fix whichever bound it hits, and measure again.

Tkernel≥ max ( FCmath , QmovedWbytes moved ) + Tidlelaunch, stalls

Filled in for siboehm's 4092² FP32 matmul on an RTX A6000: the math floor is 4.57 ms and the minimum-bytes floor is 0.35 ms, so a good kernel is compute-bound. The naive kernel still takes 0.444 s (309 GFLOP/s) because with no reuse it would move 548 GB, 2,046× the minimum. Warptiling (kernel 10) gets to 6.29 ms (21.8 TFLOP/s), within 1.07× of cuBLAS's 5.89 ms.

The same thing in plain text:

time ≥ max( FLOPs ÷ peak FLOP/s, bytes actually moved ÷ peak bandwidth ) + idle time (launches, stalls) intensity = FLOPs ÷ bytes moved below C/W → memory-bound: move fewer bytes above C/W → compute-bound: faster math units

The mental model: the loop, and which card fixes which bound

Card numbers follow the course order. How to tell the four bounds apart comes from Horace He: an overhead-bound program barely slows when you double the batch. Nsight Compute's stall reasons separate latency (long_scoreboard) from bandwidth (memory throughput near peak). siboehm's kernel 3 was bound neither by DRAM nor by math but by shared-memory instruction throughput (MIO throttle), which is why the course calls it "latency and instruction" work.

The words people use

Coalescing, bank conflicts

Global memory is served to a warp in 32-byte sectors: stride-1 floats use 4 sectors, stride-32 uses 32 (12.5% useful). Shared memory has 32 four-byte banks; 32 threads hitting one bank serialise 32 ways. The fix is a pad ([32][33]) or an XOR swizzle.

Occupancy, ILP

Occupancy is active warps ÷ the maximum, capped by threads, registers and shared memory (siboehm's kernel 3: 66% on A6000, 50% on H100). It is one way to put latency × throughput worth of work in flight; ILP and bigger loads are the others.

Tensor core, MMA; FP8, MX, NVFP4

A warp-wide matrix multiply-accumulate on small tiles (16×16×16 in WMMA). Low-precision formats cut bytes and raise peak: E4M3/E5M2 FP8, MX blocks of 32 with a power-of-two scale, NVFP4 blocks of 16 with an FP8 scale (4.5 bits per value).

Arithmetic intensity at each level

FLOPs per byte, counted separately for DRAM → SMEM (siboehm's loads per result) and for SMEM → registers (loads per FMA: 2 → 1.125 → 0.25). The whole matmul ladder is raising intensity at both levels until the math wins.

TMA, clusters, warp specialization (Hopper)

TMA is a hardware unit that copies tiles between global and shared memory asynchronously. Thread-block clusters (up to 8 portable, 16 opt-in on H100) share distributed shared memory. Warp specialization gives some warps loading and others MMA. siboehm's unfinished kernel 11 and the H100 worklog point here.

Triton, CUTLASS, cuBLAS

Triton writes kernels over blocks, and its compiler does the coalescing, shared memory and tensor-core selection. Its tutorial matmul reaches 99% of cuBLAS at 4096 (fp16, GPU unnamed). CUTLASS is NVIDIA's C++ template library. cuBLAS is many prebuilt kernels picked by shape, including split-K for small sizes.

The takeaway

Don't guess; measure, locate, then fix the bound you're actually at. Most of the speed comes from the first few structural steps: launch enough threads, coalesce, tile, and reuse data from registers. That got siboehm to 80% of cuBLAS in two weekends and Harris 11.8× from the algorithm alone. After that, tuning pays in single-digit percent and doesn't transfer between GPUs: siboehm's best A6000 config is 6% off the best one on an A100. For LLM serving, the kernels that matter are tensor-core GEMMs in BF16/FP8/FP4, attention (perf-4), and fused elementwise and norm kernels. At small batch, launch overhead matters too.

Where this doesn't apply as-is: most inference engineers pick kernels rather than write them. Kiely: "most inference engineers will never need to write their own kernels", but kernel selection matters. At a kernel company that's inverted. The source numbers come from G80 to A6000 GPUs, so the ratios teach the mechanism but aren't predictions for an H100. On Hopper and Blackwell the top of the ladder looks different: TMA, warp specialization and tcgen05 instead of hand-tuned FP32 FMAs. And none of the Colab notebooks has been run by the author, so your T4 numbers are the real result.
