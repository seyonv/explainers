Profiling and benchmarking hygiene

Also called: Nsight Systems (nsys), Nsight Compute (ncu), Compute Sanitizer, do_bench. NVIDIA's two profilers answer two different questions, the sanitizer checks correctness before you time anything, and CUTLASS's measurement guide plus Triton's do_bench define what an honest kernel timing looks like.

In one breath

First make the kernel correct (Compute Sanitizer), then find where the time goes across the whole program (Nsight Systems' timeline), then put the single most expensive kernel under a microscope (Nsight Compute: how close it is to the memory or compute roof, and why its warps stall). Every number you report comes from a timing that runs on the GPU, after warm-up, with cold caches, repeated many times, and summarised as a median plus a spread, never a single time.time() around an asynchronous launch.

The loop these tools serve

Two tools, two questions

Everyday analogy: nsys is the CCTV of the whole factory floor over a shift; ncu is pulling one machine into the lab and running it again and again with sensors attached. The two examples are the running example's two shapes on an H100: a prefill-like BF16 GEMM, 4096 × 4096 × 4096, and the batch-1 decode GEMV through Llama-3.1-8B's up-projection, 1 × 4096 × 14336. Why ncu's duration is not your production duration

To make metrics deterministic, Nsight Compute changes how the kernel runs. The Profiling Guide's FAQ lists the differences from Nsight Systems:

NVIDIA's advice when comparing the two: lock clocks yourself with nvidia-smi and pass --clock-control none to ncu; for cache-sensitive work use --replay-mode application --cache-control none. Use ncu for why, and nsys or CUDA events for how long.

Correctness first: Compute Sanitizer

A fast wrong kernel is worthless, and GPU memory bugs are often silent: an off-by-one write can land in allocation padding and the program still "works". compute-sanitizer ships with the CUDA toolkit. Run it before any timing, and never time under it.

Worked example: the benchmark that lies by 1.6×

A GEMV that reads 16 MB of weights (illustrative size) on an H100 with its 50 MB L2. Time it in a tight loop and, after the first run, the weights are already sitting in L2, so every later run reads them from L2 instead of HBM. NVIDIA doesn't publish H100 L2 bandwidth; the Scaling Book quotes a measured ~5.5 TB/s, "roughly 1.6x the HBM bandwidth".

The tell: a hot-loop GEMV that "achieves" more than 3.35 TB/s is reading from cache. The real up-projection (117.5 MB) is bigger than L2, so it mostly can't stay resident, but smaller per-layer matrices and small batches can (our inference). Two standard cures:

  • Rotate buffers (CUTLASS): allocate copies of every input until they total ≥ 2× L2 (at least two per tensor) and switch copy each iteration. On H100: ≥ 100 MB, so 7 copies of the 16 MB weights (112 MB).
  • Flush L2 (Triton's do_bench): zero a 256 MB buffer before each timed run. 256 MiB = 268 MB, 5.4× the H100's L2.

The rest of the hygiene checklist

time.time() around a launch measures the launch, not the kernel

# ✗ stops the clock as soon as the launch is queued t0 = time.time(); y = W @ x; t1 = time.time() # ✓ timestamps recorded on the GPU stream (ms) s = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True) e = torch.cuda.Event(enable_timing=True) s.record(); y = W @ x; e.record() torch.cuda.synchronize(); ms = s.elapsed_time(e) # ✓ warm-up, L2 flush, events, many runs ms = triton.testing.do_bench(lambda: W @ x, quantiles=[0.5, 0.9])

CUDA launches are asynchronous: the CPU queues the kernel and moves on, so the first line times a few microseconds of Python and driver work however long the matmul takes. Adding torch.cuda.synchronize() makes it correct but folds in launch and sync overhead. Events are recorded on the GPU's own stream.

PracticeWhat the source says
Warm up, separatelyCUTLASS: separate warm-up and timed loops (split by cudaEventRecord or cudaProfilerStart/Stop); all buffers allocated before either loop; no other GPU work between launches. Back-to-back GEMMs saw clocks oscillate for about the first 3 s before settling.
Enough iterationsCUTLASS: > 1,000 timed iterations gave stable results at ~4096³; smaller problems need more. Its Blackwell example: 10,000 warm-ups, 4,000 iterations, 1 s cool-down.
Fixed frequency vs fixed powerFixed-frequency tests lock clocks to study efficiency; use zero-fill there, since it draws less power and helps hold the clock. Fixed-power tests mimic real use (DVFS on); fill with Uniform[−1, 1] (scales [0, 1]), because low-entropy data flips fewer bits and flatters power-limited speed.
do_bench defaultswarmup=25, rep=100, both in milliseconds of runtime, not iterations (it estimates the kernel time from 5 runs, then picks counts). Flushes L2 before every run, times each run with events, returns the mean unless you pass return_mode or quantiles.
Report the spreadGive the median and p90 of many runs (quantiles=[0.5, 0.9]), not the min alone: the min is the luckiest run, and p90 shows clock and contention noise. (Our recommendation; CUTLASS reports averages with monitored clocks.)

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ time.time() around a launch

Measures the asynchronous launch, not the kernel: microseconds whatever the kernel does. With a sync added it's correct but includes launch and sync overhead, and one run tells you nothing about the spread.

✓ CUDA events / do_bench: for timing

GPU-side timestamps, warm-up, many runs, cold L2 (do_bench flushes; CUTLASS rotates buffers ≥ 2× L2). Report the median and p90. This is the number you quote.

✗ nsys alone

The right first step, and CUTLASS accepts its kernel durations, but a timeline only says which kernel is slow and where the gaps are. It can't say why a kernel is slow.

✓ ncu: for diagnosis

Speed of Light, the roofline dot and stall reasons say what bounds the kernel and what to fix. Its duration is taken with replayed passes, flushed caches, serialized launches and locked clocks, so don't quote it as production time.

Two winners on purpose: events and do_bench answer "how long?", ncu answers "why?". You need both.

Why does my kernel run faster in the benchmark than in production?

  • Hot caches. A tight loop re-reads inputs from L2; production reads them from HBM after other kernels have evicted them. On H100 that alone is up to ~1.6× for a bandwidth-bound kernel (worked example above).
  • Clocks. ncu locks clocks (base or boost, by version); a short benchmark may run at boost before power limits pull clocks down, while a busy server runs at its settled, power-limited clock. CUTLASS saw ~3 s of clock oscillation before settling.
  • No contention. Alone on the GPU, the kernel gets every SM and all the bandwidth. In an engine, kernels overlap on streams, share L2, and wait on communication; ncu even serializes launches on purpose.
  • Zero-filled data. torch.zeros inputs flip fewer bits and draw less power, so a power-limited GPU clocks higher than on real activations. Use Uniform[−1, 1] or real data.
  • Launch overhead hidden. Back-to-back launches overlap the CPU launch cost; a decode step with hundreds of small kernels pays it (see CUDA graphs).

When is a hot-cache number the right one? When production really does reuse data that fits in L2, for example a small matrix applied repeatedly inside one fused step. Then measure hot, on purpose, and say so. And for end-to-end serving questions (tokens/s, latency under load), skip kernel microbenchmarks altogether and benchmark the engine with real traffic.
