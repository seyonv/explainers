Before this: Matmul I: the naive kernel and the lower bound

Tensor cores and low precision

Also called MMA or WMMA (the instruction), FP8 (E4M3 and E5M2), microscaling (MXFP8, MXFP4) and NVFP4. This card covers the CUDA guide's WMMA section, the OCP FP8 and MX specs and NVIDIA's NVFP4 post, then puts them on the lab T4, the H100 running example and a B200.

In one breath

A tensor core is a unit that makes one warp do a whole small matrix multiply-accumulate (D = A·B + C on a 16×16×16 tile) in one instruction, and it runs 8–30× faster than the ordinary CUDA cores doing one FMA per thread. Each halving of precision roughly doubles both the tensor-core math rate and the weights you move per byte. Small formats only work if you keep a scale next to the numbers, and the newest formats (MX, NVFP4) keep one scale per block of 16–32 values.

// CUDA Programming Guide 5.4.11.7: a 16x16x16 matmul in one warp wmma::fragment<wmma::matrix_a, 16,16,16, half, wmma::col_major> a_frag; wmma::fragment<wmma::matrix_b, 16,16,16, half, wmma::row_major> b_frag; wmma::fragment<wmma::accumulator, 16,16,16, float> c_frag; wmma::fill_fragment(c_frag, 0.0f); wmma::load_matrix_sync(a_frag, a, 16); wmma::load_matrix_sync(b_frag, b, 16); wmma::mma_sync(c_frag, a_frag, b_frag, c_frag); // D = A*B + C wmma::store_matrix_sync(c, c_frag, 16, wmma::mem_row_major);

The fragments are opaque: which thread holds which element is "unspecified and subject to change", so you load and store through shared or global memory. Every thread in the warp must call these functions. Real kernels (cuBLAS, CUTLASS, the tiles of Matmul IV) wrap this in the same block-, warp- and thread-tiling, just with a matrix instruction at the bottom instead of an FMA.

3. The formats, bit by bit

A float is sign × 2exponent − bias × (1.mantissa). Exponent bits buy range, mantissa bits buy precision: E4M3 has 8 values between 1 and 2 (1, 1.125, … 1.875), E5M2 has 4 (1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75), E2M1 has 2.

Why it beats the alternatives (at about 4 bits)

✗ BF16 (the baseline)

Essentially lossless (0.0017), but 2 bytes per weight: 16.06 GB for Llama-3.1-8B and a 209 tok/s decode ceiling on an H100.

✗ FP8, one scale per tensor

Very accurate here (0.026) and 2× fewer bytes (417 tok/s), but one outlier sets the scale for the whole tensor, and at 4 bits that would push most weights to zero.

✗ MXFP4 (power-of-two scale per 32)

4.25 bits, hardware-scaled on Blackwell. But the scale can only move in ×2 steps, and one outlier coarsens all 32 values: 0.138 error, 11 of 32 example weights zeroed.

✓ NVFP4 (E4M3 scale per 16)

For 0.25 more bits than MXFP4, a fractional scale on half-size blocks cuts error by a third (0.093) and fits the outlier exactly, with ≈ 3.6× fewer bytes than BF16 (the ~742 tok/s ceiling).

If FP8 math is 2× faster, why don't serving engines get 2×?

Because only part of a step is FP8 GEMMs. The 2× applies to tensor-core matmul time, and the byte halving only to weights:

  • Decode is memory-bound, so the faster math barely matters, and the weights are only part of the bytes. Illustrative: at batch 32 with 4,096-token contexts the BF16 KV cache is 17.18 GB per step next to 16.06 GB of weights. FP8 weights take the step from 33.24 to 25.21 GB: 1.32×, not 2× (labs/lowprec.py). An FP8 KV cache is a separate choice with its own accuracy cost.
  • Attention and non-GEMM ops (norms, activations, rotary, sampling, and quantizing activations on the fly) don't get the FP8 rate, and scale handling adds work.
  • Accuracy: some layers stay in BF16. NVIDIA reports "1% or less" accuracy loss going from FP8 to NVFP4 on DeepSeek-R1-0528, but that is NVIDIA's claim on its own benchmarks; your model needs its own check. Quoted end-to-end FP8 gains of tens of percent fit this picture, but we haven't measured one.

When not to use it: on a T4 there is no FP8 or BF16 tensor math at all (FP16, INT8 and INT4 only). At batch 1, weight-only quantization gets nearly all the gain without low-precision math. And NVFP4 math needs Blackwell. The serving-side quantization story (AWQ, GPTQ, FP8 KV cache) is in the perf-5 inference-engines course (not built yet) and the llm-latency quantization card.
