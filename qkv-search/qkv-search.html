Query, key, value

Also called Q, K, V, or the three projections inside attention. They appear in every attention layer of every transformer (GPT, BERT, Llama). This card builds them up from the problem they solve, using a search engine as the picture.

In one breath

Attention lets each word pull in useful information from other words. To do that it runs a small search: the word's query says what it is looking for, every word's key is the label it gets matched by, and every word's value is the content it hands over when picked. Splitting these into three separate jobs is the whole trick.

1The goal: give each word its context

On its own, a word is often ambiguous. Read this sentence:

Theanimaldidn'tcrossthestreetbecauseitwastired

The word it means nothing until you know it points to animal. You get that instantly. A model only sees the word "it" until it pulls in information from the other words. So the goal is this: for every word, build a new version of it that has the useful information from the rest of the sentence mixed in. Doing that means answering two separate questions:

Question 1: where to look

Which other words are relevant to me, and how much? For "it", that's "animal", not "street".

Question 2: what to take

Once I've picked them, what information do they hand over? For "it", that's "a living creature".

2Three attempts that fail, and the one that works

✗ Average every word

Give each of the 10 words a 10% share. "it" ends up as much "street" and "because" as "animal". Nothing answers question 1.

✗ Compare the words as they are

Use each word's single meaning vector for everything. But "it" doesn't resemble "animal"; it is looking for one. One vector can't describe what a word is, what it seeks and what it offers all at once.

✗ Exact lookup, best match only

Like a database: find the one matching entry and copy it. All or nothing, so the model can't hedge between two candidates, and a hard pick gives training nothing smooth to learn from.

✓ A soft search with Q, K, V

Give each word three separate descriptions, one per job. Score every word, share out attention smoothly, and blend what comes back. That answers both questions, and all of it can be learned.

3The picture: attention is a search engine

You already know a system that answers "where to look" and "what to take" separately: a search engine. The thing you type is not the thing you're matched against, and that's not the thing you end up reading either.

Values

Search engine

You read the page itself, not its title.

Attention

"it" takes in each word's content, mixed by its share.

The one difference: a search engine hands you the top result. Attention reads a little of every result, in proportion to how well it matched.

4The three roles, side by side

Query

What am I looking for?

Comes from the word doing the asking.

Used only to score other words.

Key

What should I be found by?

Comes from each word being searched.

Used only to score. It decides how much attention a word gets, and nothing else.

Value

What do I hand over if picked?

Comes from each word being searched.

Used only to build the output. It never affects the scores.

Query and key answer question 1 (where to look). Value answers question 2 (what to take).

Where Q, K and V come from: each word starts as one meaning vector x. The model learns three separate recipes (weight matrices) and applies them to x: query = x · W_Q key = x · W_K value = x · W_V Same word, three different descriptions, one per job.

Are the columns really "is a noun" and "living thing"?

No. Those labels are for intuition. Real Q, K and V have 64 or more columns per attention head, and the model learns what they mean during training. They rarely line up with neat English concepts. Real models also divide the scores by the square root of the column count before softmax, which this example skips, and the new "it" is added on top of the original word rather than replacing it.

Every word runs this search at the same time, so each word is both a searcher (with its query) and a search result (with its key and value). It isn't always the best tool: the cost grows with the square of the sentence length, because every word scores every other word. For very long inputs, people use cheaper variants such as sliding-window attention, or models that drop attention altogether, such as state-space models (Mamba).
