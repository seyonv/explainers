Compute-bound or memory-bound? The roofline intuition

Also called the roofline model, arithmetic intensity, ops:byte, or the ridge point. Anyone who says "decode is memory-bound" or "prefill is compute-bound" is using this idea. Here it's applied to Llama-3.1-8B on one H100.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Knowing an operation's FLOPs and its bytes separately doesn't tell you which one sets its time. So you can't say whether a faster chip, a better kernel or a bigger batch would help, and a wrong guess spends money on the resource that was already idle.

Where it fits: Joins the two cards before it: the FLOPs card's work count and the GPU card's 989 TF/s and 3.35 TB/s. The ridge of 295 you compute here comes back throughout the series, first as the critical batch size in perf-2.

In one breath

Every operation needs some math (FLOPs) and has to move some bytes between memory and the chip. It takes about as long as the slower of the two: FLOPs ÷ peak FLOP/s, or bytes ÷ memory bandwidth. Arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte) tells you which one wins, and the hardware's ridge point is the dividing line: on an H100 it's 989e12 ÷ 3.35e12 ≈ 295 FLOPs per byte.

Kitchen version: a chef who can make 295 cuts in the time a runner brings one ingredient from the pantry sits idle on a recipe that needs one cut per ingredient, and keeps the runner waiting on one that needs 500.

The formula

T≈max( FLOPsCmath time , bytesWmemory time )
I=FLOPsbytes I*=CW I>I*⇔compute-bound
Cpeak compute, FLOP/sH100 BF16 dense: 989e12 Wmemory (HBM) bandwidth, bytes/sH100: 3.35e12 FLOPsmultiplies + adds the operation needsmatmul [B,D]·[D,F]: 2BDF bytesdata read from and written to HBMBF16: 2 bytes per number Iarithmetic intensity of the operationproperty of the work I*ridge point (the chip's ops:byte ratio)property of the hardware: 295
T ≈ max(FLOPs / C, bytes / W) I = FLOPs / bytes I* = C / W = 989e12 / 3.35e12 = 295.2

Why comparing I with I* works: FLOPs/C > bytes/W is the same inequality as FLOPs/bytes > C/W. Taking the max assumes the chip overlaps math with memory traffic perfectly. With no overlap you'd take the sum, which is at most 2× the max (Scaling Book Part 1).

Batching moves you along the roof

For a matmul with B tokens, I = BDF ÷ (BD + DF + BF), which is about B when B is much smaller than D and F. The weights are read once however many tokens share them, so batching tokens raises intensity almost for free. From 1 to 256 tokens, the time barely moves (35 → 38 µs) while the useful work grows 256×: extra tokens ride along on the same weight read. Past the ridge, the time doubles with B. The crossover is at 325, not 295, because the activations X and Z add B·(D + F) bytes, which pulls I below B.

The ridges all sit between about 200 and 600: modern accelerators need a few hundred FLOPs per byte to stay busy. Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Count FLOPs only

16.06 GFLOP ÷ 989 TF/s says a decode step takes 0.016 ms. The real floor is 4.79 ms, so the estimate is about 300× too fast, and it would tell you to buy more compute you can't use.

✗ Count bytes only

Right for decode, wrong for prefill: the 512-token matmul would be predicted at 40.7 µs, but the math takes 60.8 µs. Bytes-only also says batching is free forever, which stops being true at the ridge.

✗ Just benchmark it

You get a number, but not why. A 35 µs kernel could be excellent (it's at the bandwidth roof) or terrible, and the benchmark alone doesn't say whether to fix the bytes or the math.

✓ Roofline: two numbers

Intensity against the ridge tells you which resource is the limit and how far you are from the roof. Below the ridge, cut the bytes or batch more tokens. Above it, cut the FLOPs or get more compute.

Is it the whole truth?

No. It's a first-order model, and it predicts a floor, not a measurement:

  • Real kernels don't reach the roof. The Scaling Book says H100s and B200s usually get about 80–85% of their spec FLOP/s (TPUs get closer to 95%). Bandwidth falls short too: your M3's measured decode implies about 51 of its ~100 GB/s (see prefill vs decode).
  • Caches bend the byte count. Data that stays in L2 or shared memory (50 MB and 256 KB per SM on an H100) doesn't cost HBM bandwidth. Tiling a big matmul re-reads chunks, which adds bytes. Either way, the effective bandwidth ends up higher or lower than the spec.
  • Not every operation runs on the Tensor Cores. A vector add runs on the CUDA cores, which peak at about 66 TF/s on an H100 against the Tensor Cores' 990 (Scaling Book Part 12). Their ridge is about 66e12 ÷ 3.35e12 ≈ 20, not 295. At I = 0.167, the vector add is still far below it.
  • Tiny operations are dominated by overhead. A 1.79 µs vector add is short enough that kernel launch and synchronisation costs, which the roofline doesn't model, can matter as much as the bytes.
  • Other roofs exist. Across several chips, the bottleneck can be the interconnect, with its own and much lower ridge. At long context, decode also reads the KV cache (1.07 GB for an 8k-token sequence), and attention's intensity doesn't grow with batch size.

For the rigorous version (T_math vs T_comms, lower and upper bounds, and the critical batch size for a whole model), see roofline and critical batch size in perf-2.

Understand and remember

  • Every operation needs FLOPs and bytes → with perfect overlap, time ≈ max(FLOPs/C, bytes/W) → which term is larger ⇔ I = FLOPs/bytes vs I* = C/W → on an H100, 989e12 ÷ 3.35e12 ≈ 295
  • The weights are read once per step however many tokens use them → a B-token matmul has I ≈ B (for B much smaller than D and F) → batching raises intensity almost for free → the time stays flat (35 → 38 µs from B = 1 to 256) until the ridge, then grows linearly with B
  • Batch-1 decode does 2 FLOPs per 2-byte weight → I = 1, far below 295 → memory-bound: a 4.79 ms floor against 0.016 ms of math → the only fixes are fewer bytes (FP8, GQA), more bandwidth, or more tokens per read
  • The ridge is C/W, so a new chip moves it → the H200 (more W) lowers it to 206 and decodes 1.43× faster; FP8 doubles C (ridge 591) but FP8 weights also halve the bytes → the batch needed to become compute-bound stays about the same
  • detail that matters Activations add B·(D + F) bytes → I sits a little below B → the matmul reaches the ridge at B ≈ 325, not 295
  • detail that matters Non-matmul work (a vector add, softmax) runs on CUDA cores at about 66 TF/s → their own ridge is near 20 → a different roof for elementwise work

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain why comparing I with I* gives the same answer as comparing the math time with the memory time.
    Check
    • FLOPs/C > bytes/W rearranges to FLOPs/bytes > C/W
    • I belongs to the work, I* to the chip
    • taking the max assumes perfect overlap; with none you'd add them, which is at most 2× the max
  2. mechanism Compute FLOPs, bytes, intensity and the bound for X[512, 4096] · W[4096, 14336] in BF16 on an H100.
    Check
    • FLOPs = 2 · 512 · 4096 · 14336 = 60.13 G
    • bytes = 2 · (2.10M + 58.72M + 7.34M) = 136.3 MB
    • I = 441 > 295: compute-bound
    • math 60.8 µs against memory 40.7 µs
  3. tradeoff Your decode service runs at batch 8. One vendor offers 2× the FLOP/s at the same bandwidth; another offers 1.43× the bandwidth at the same FLOP/s. Which helps, and when would the other one start to matter?
    Check
    • I ≈ 8, far below 295: memory-bound
    • more FLOP/s changes nothing for this step
    • 1.43× bandwidth cuts the weight read (the H200 case: 4.79 → 3.35 ms at batch 1)
    • the FLOPs offer matters for prefill, or once the batch passes the ridge
  4. tradeoff A profiler says a kernel took 35 µs. What would you compute to tell whether that's good or bad, and what would you do in each case?
    Check
    • compute its FLOPs and bytes, hence I, and compare the measured time with the roof at that I
    • if it sits on the bandwidth roof (like the 1-token matmul's 35.1 µs floor) it's already optimal: cut bytes or batch more
    • if it's far below both roofs, the kernel itself is inefficient: fix it before buying hardware

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

An H100 serves Llama-3.1-8B with FP8 weights and FP8 math (1,979 TF/s) at batch 1. Before computing: the decode floor, the math time, and whether the step is any closer to compute-bound than in BF16.

Reveal

Bytes halve, so the floor is 8.03 GB ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 2.40 ms. The math is 16.06 GFLOP ÷ 1,979e12 ≈ 8.1 µs. The ratio is still about 295× (computed): I doubles to 2, but the ridge doubles to 591. Faster, yet exactly as memory-bound.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Reproduce the matmul batch sweep X[B, 4096] · W[4096, 14336] for B = 1, 64, 256 and 512 on the H100 (in the lab or by hand). Then redo it for the H200 (989 TF/s, 4.8 TB/s): where does the ridge crossing move, and how much faster is B = 1?

Done when: H100: 35.07, 35.76, 37.87, 60.80 µs, crossing near B = 325. H200: 24.47, 24.96, 30.40, 60.80 µs, ridge 206, crossing near B = 220 (computed). B = 1 is 1.43× faster, and B = 512 doesn't change because it was compute-bound already.

Hint

time = max(2BDF ÷ C, 2(BD + DF + BF) ÷ W). Find the crossing by solving BDF ÷ (BD + DF + BF) = C/W, or by bisection.
