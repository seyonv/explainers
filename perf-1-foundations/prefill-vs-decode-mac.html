Prefill vs decode, measured on your Mac

Also called prompt processing vs generation, or in Ollama's words "prompt eval rate" vs "eval rate": the two phases of inference. Measured here with qwen3.5:4b on your M3, then projected to Llama-3.1-8B on an H100.

In one breath

Reading the prompt (prefill) pushes all its tokens through the model in one parallel pass, so each weight fetched from memory gets used for many tokens and the limit is compute. Writing the answer (decode) makes one token per full pass over the weights, so the limit is how fast memory can deliver them. On your M3 that's about 190 vs 15 tokens/s.

Why decode is stuck at ~15 tok/s: the bandwidth arithmetic

Every decode step has to stream (roughly) all the weights from memory to the chip, and it does very little math per byte. So the speed is capped by bandwidth:

vdecode≤ Bmemory bandwidth Wbytesbytes read per step so Beffective≈ vmeasured×Wbytes
vdecode speed, tokens per second (one request, batch 1)≈ 15 on your M3 Bmemory bandwidth of the chip~100 GB/s M3 · 3.35 TB/s H100 Wweight bytes read per decode step (plus a little KV cache)~3.4 GB · 16.06 GB
your M3: 15 tok/s × ~3.4 GB ≈ 51 GB/s (≈ half of ~100 GB/s peak) per row: 15.25 → 51.9 · 15.14 → 51.5 · 14.12 → 48.0 GB/s H100, running example (Llama-3.1-8B, BF16, batch 1): 3.35 TB/s ÷ 16.06 GB ≈ 209 tok/s ≈ 4.8 ms/step ← a ceiling, not a measurement

The 3.4 GB is the model file, and it's an approximation: the file also contains a vision encoder that text decoding never reads. Using the ~2.85 GB text-weights estimate from the memory-bandwidth card instead gives 15 × 2.85 ≈ 43 GB/s. Either way, well below peak. The H100 figure is arithmetic from the spec sheet; real engines reach a fraction of it.

The two phases, on two machines

Like a cashier scanning a whole trolley at once (prefill) versus a pen writing a letter one word at a time, where each word depends on the last (decode).

On the H100 the gap between the phases is about 295× at batch 1; on your M3 it's 13× measured, because Ollama's prefill at 244 tokens is still far from saturating the chip (our inference from the rising prefill numbers, not a measured utilisation).

How it differs from related ideas

✗ TTFT

Time to first token is what the user feels before anything appears: ≈ queue + network + prefill. On your Mac, with no queue or network, it's mostly the 0.29–1.30 s of prefill. See serving metrics.

✗ Throughput with batching

A server decodes many users at once: one read of the weights serves the whole batch, so cost per token falls even though each user's speed doesn't rise. See step time.

✗ Prompt caching

Reuses the KV cache from an earlier request with the same prefix, so prefill is skipped for those tokens. It shortens TTFT but does nothing for decode speed. See prompt caching.

✓ Separating the two phases

The first thing every serving system does: prefill is tuned for FLOPs (TTFT), decode for bandwidth and batch size (tok/s). Chunked prefill and disaggregated serving both start from this split.

Why did decode slow down with a longer prompt? And is it always like this?

The dip from 15.25 to 14.12 tok/s (7%) at 244 prompt tokens most likely comes from the KV cache: each decode step reads the weights plus the keys and values of every earlier token, and that second read grows with the prompt. This is our inference; the lab didn't measure the KV reads directly. At 244 tokens the cache is small next to 3.4 GB of weights, so the effect is small; at long contexts it becomes a big part of each step.

Not always memory-bound. Your Mac serves one request at a time (batch 1), the worst case for decode. A datacenter GPU batches many users into each step. Once the batch passes the critical batch size (≈ 295 for an H100 in BF16), decode's matrix multiplies become compute-bound too. See critical batch size. And prefill isn't always compute-bound: very short prompts (like the 27-token row, at half the speed of the 244-token row) don't give the chip enough tokens to work on at once.
