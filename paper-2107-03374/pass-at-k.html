pass@k

Also: pass@1, pass@100, the unbiased pass@k estimator · This paper's primary metric. It draws n = 200 samples per HumanEval task, counts the c that pass all unit tests, and estimates pass@k for k ≤ 100 (Section 2.1, Figure 3, Appendix A).

In one breath

pass@k is the probability that at least one of k generated programs passes the problem's unit tests, averaged over problems. Because sampling k programs directly would be noisy, the paper samples many more (n = 200) and computes an unbiased estimate of what k would have given.

Worked example: one problem, n = 10 samples, c = 2 correct, k = 3

unbiased pass@3 = 1 - C(n-c, k) / C(n, k) = 1 - C(8, 3) / C(10, 3) = 1 - 56 / 120 = 0.5333 -> 53.3% numpy form 1 - prod(1 - k/i) for i = n-c+1 .. n = 1 - (1 - 3/9)(1 - 3/10) = 1 - 0.6667 x 0.7000 = 0.5333 (same value) tempting 1 - (1 - p-hat)^k with p-hat = c/n = 0.2 but biased = 1 - 0.8^3 = 0.4880 -> 48.8%

Illustrative n, c and k chosen small enough to check by hand; the paper uses n = 200 and k ≤ 100. Both the direct form and the numerically stable product form in Figure 3 give exactly the same number, which is the point of Figure 3 — computing C(200, 100) directly overflows.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Exact-match or BLEU against a reference

Scores the text of the program. Fails because infinitely many programs are functionally equivalent to the reference; Figure 8 shows correct and wrong samples have overlapping BLEU.

✗ Generate exactly k samples, report the fraction solved

The estimator Kulal et al. (2019) used. Unbiased, but high variance — at k = 100 you observe one Bernoulli draw per problem.

✗ Plug-in 1 - (1 - p̂)^k

Cheap, and looks right. Appendix A proves it is a consistent underestimate, so results appear to improve just by sampling more.

✓ Unbiased pass@k from n ≥ k samples

Low variance from the large n, unbiased for every n, so numbers are comparable across papers that sampled different amounts. This is why equation (1) exists at all.

Is this always the right metric?

No. pass@k with large k is an oracle metric: it assumes something can tell you which of the k samples is correct. That is fair when you have unit tests, and unfair as a stand-in for what a user sees from an autocomplete box — which is why the paper separately reports mean log-probability ranking (44.5% for Codex-S-12B) alongside pass@100 (77.5%).

pass@k also inherits the test suite's blind spots: a sample counts as correct if it passes HumanEval's average of 7.7 tests, not if it is actually right. The paper cites Qi et al. (2015) on exactly this failure in program repair.
