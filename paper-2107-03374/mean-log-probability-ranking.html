Mean log-probability ranking

Also: mean token log-prob, log-prob sample selection · The paper's answer to “generate many, evaluate one”: from 100 Codex-S-12B samples it solves 44.5% of HumanEval, and averages +11.6 points over random ranking (Section 3.3, Figures 7 and 10, Section 4.5).

In one breath

pass@100 assumes an oracle picks the passing sample. Without unit tests — an autocomplete box, say — you must choose blind. Score each sample by its average per-token log-probability under the model and return the highest. Averaging, not summing, is the whole trick.

Worked example: two samples, same problem

sample A 5 tokens, log-probs -0.1 -0.2 -0.1 -0.3 -0.3 sum = -1.00 mean = -1.00 / 5 = -0.200 sample B 20 tokens, each log-prob -0.15 sum = -3.00 mean = -3.00 / 20 = -0.150
HeuristicScore AScore BPicksWhy
Sum log-probability−1.00−3.00AEvery extra token subtracts. Ranks by brevity, not quality.
Mean log-probability−0.200−0.150BLength cancels out. B's typical token has probability e−0.15 = 0.861 against A's e−0.20 = 0.819.

Log-probabilities are illustrative; the arithmetic and the disagreement they produce are exact. This is the paper's finding in miniature: mean log-probability “outperforms evaluating a random sample, while choosing the sample based on sum log probability can perform slightly worse than picking randomly”.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Pick a random sample

The baseline. Equivalent to pass@1 at the sampling temperature — 37.7% for Codex-S-12B, against 44.5% for mean log-prob ranking.

✗ Sum log-probability

Systematically prefers short completions. The paper reports it can do slightly worse than random.

✗ Back-translation: P(docstring | sample) under Codex-D

Beats random but underperforms mean log-prob, and the paper notes the heuristic “appears to overfit quickly”.

✓ Mean token log-probability

Length-neutral, free (the log-probs already exist), and needs no second model and no tests. +11.6 points over random for Codex-S on average across 1 to 100 samples.

How close does this get you to pass@100?

Not very. For Codex-S-12B the oracle solves 77.5% of problems from 100 samples and mean log-prob ranking solves 44.5% — it recovers roughly half the gap between one sample (37.7%) and the oracle. The paper's framing is that “accurate code samples can be selected via heuristic ranking instead of fully evaluating each sample”, not that ranking replaces testing.

The model's confidence is not correctness: confidently wrong code scores well, which is exactly the failure mode Section 7.1 calls over-reliance. Where you can run tests, run them — on APPS, filtering by the 3 public example tests beats every log-prob heuristic reported here.
