APPS and filtered pass@k

Also: strict accuracy, raw pass@k · The paper's second benchmark. 1-shot Codex-12B samples 1000 solutions per problem and keeps only those passing the 3 public example tests; introductory filtered pass@1 is 22.78% against raw pass@1 of 4.14%, with a 3-second timeout (Section 3.5, Table 2).

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: HumanEval is small and tailored to Codex's docstring format. To check the model isn't just good at one benchmark, you need a larger, independent one, but APPS asks for whole stdin programs and grades by strict accuracy, so the numbers don't line up.

Where it fits: The paper's second benchmark. It re-uses pass@k and the idea of selecting among samples, but swaps log-prob ranking for a real, cheap signal: the 3 example tests in each problem statement.

In one breath

APPS is a 5000-problem competitive-programming benchmark where most tasks are whole programs reading stdin, not single functions — so it is off-distribution for Codex. Its problem statements include 3 worked examples, and the paper turns those into a free filter: sample 1000 times, discard anything failing the visible examples, and evaluate what survives.

All figures are Table 2's. The ratio and the comparison line are computed from them. Table 2 also brackets a second number per cell — programs that fail no unit test but time out on some at 3 seconds; introductory filtered pass@1 rises to 25.10% if those are counted as passing. The paper notes raw pass@1 could be improved with a lower temperature than 0.6.

How the three metrics differ

✗ Strict accuracy (the APPS paper's metric)

Fraction of problems fully solved. The APPS authors also report percentage of unit tests passed; this paper refuses that one and reports pass@k against strict accuracy only.

✗ Raw pass@k

Honest and directly comparable to HumanEval, but wastes the 3 example tests that every competitive-programming problem hands you for free.

✗ Raw pass@1000

The ceiling: 25.02% on introductory. Requires running the full hidden test suite on 1000 programs per problem.

✓ Filtered pass@k

Screen 1000 samples on the 3 visible examples, then measure. Nearly the pass@1000 result while evaluating one sample against the hidden tests — and it is what a contestant would actually do.

Is filtered pass@1 comparable to anyone else's pass@1?

No, and mixing them up is the easy mistake here. Filtered pass@1 still consumed 1000 generations and 3000 test executions; only the hidden-test evaluation budget is one. Compare it to raw pass@1000 (25.02%), not to raw pass@1 (4.14%).

The competition column is the odd one: filtered pass@1 (3.04%) roughly matches raw pass@1000 (3.23%) there, because on the hardest problems the public examples screen out almost everything. And the paper's headline framing — 1-shot Codex-12B is “comparable” to a GPT-Neo model fine-tuned on APPS — holds on raw pass@1 for introductory problems and does not extend to interview problems, where fine-tuned GPT-Neo is 4× better.

Understand and remember

  • Every APPS statement contains 3 worked examples → run 1000 samples on them first → discard failures → evaluating one survivor against the hidden tests captures most of pass@1000 (22.78% vs 25.02% introductory)
  • Public examples are a real, if partial, oracle → they check behaviour, not confidence → filtering beats every log-prob heuristic in the paper
  • Filtered pass@1 still consumes 1000 generations and 3000 example runs → only the hidden-test evaluation budget is 1 → compare it to raw pass@1000, not raw pass@1
  • APPS problems are whole stdin programs and possibly contaminated → off-distribution for a docstring model → Codex-12B's raw pass@1 is low (4.14% introductory, 0.14% interview)
  • detail that matters A 3-second timeout separates 'correct but slow' from 'correct' → counting timeouts as passes lifts introductory filtered pass@1 to 25.10% → functional correctness alone doesn't capture efficiency

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

On competition problems, filtered pass@1 (3.04%) is 94% of raw pass@1000 (3.23%), while on interview problems it's only 71% (2.64% vs 3.70%). Before looking at any explanation, which difficulty would you expect to have the most false positives surviving the public-example filter, and why? Commit, then reveal.

Reveal

Interview problems. Filtered pass@1 falls furthest below raw pass@1000 there, meaning more survivors of the 3 public examples still fail the hidden tests. Plausibly the examples are easier to satisfy with a near-miss program at that level, while on competition problems almost nothing survives the examples unless it's actually right. The paper doesn't analyse this, so treat it as a reading of Table 2, not a finding.
