Nucleus sampling

Also: top-p sampling (Holtzman et al., 2020) · A fixed setting in this paper: every sampling evaluation uses top p = 0.95, applied on top of whatever temperature is in use (Section 3.2).

In one breath

Sort the next-token probabilities highest first, keep just enough of them to cover p of the total mass, throw the rest away, and renormalise. Unlike a fixed cutoff, the size of that kept set — the nucleus — shrinks when the model is confident and grows when it is not.

Worked example: six candidate tokens, top p = 0.95

nucleus size = 4 of 6 tokens mass discarded = 0.03 + 0.02 = 0.05 kept mass sums to 0.5789 + 0.2316 + 0.1263 + 0.0632 = 1.0000

The six probabilities are illustrative; the 0.95 threshold and the renormalisation are the real mechanism. Note what makes it adaptive: if the model had been certain (say 0.97 on return), the nucleus would be a single token; the flatter the distribution, the more tokens survive.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Pure sampling from the full distribution

The long tail is huge. Sooner or later a nonsense token is drawn, and in code one bad token usually breaks the whole program.

✗ Top-k sampling with fixed k

Keeps k tokens whether or not they deserve it: too permissive when the model is certain, too restrictive when it is genuinely undecided.

✗ Temperature alone

Rescales every probability but never removes anything, so the tail stays reachable. That is why the paper uses both, not one.

✓ Nucleus sampling, p = 0.95

Cuts the tail by mass rather than by count, so the nucleus adapts per token. Leaves the 5% least likely mass unreachable while keeping the diversity that large-k pass@k depends on.

Does the paper justify p = 0.95?

No. It states the setting in one sentence, cites Holtzman et al. (2020), and applies it to every sampling result in the paper. There is no top-p sweep and no ablation against pure sampling or top-k, so 0.95 should be read as a fixed experimental condition, not a tuned or validated optimum.

Temperature is the knob the paper actually studies. Nucleus sampling and temperature stack: the 0.95 cut happens on the temperature-scaled distribution, so raising T widens the nucleus as well as flattening it. Every temperature number in this paper — T* = 0.2, 0.8, 1 — was measured inside a 0.95 nucleus.
