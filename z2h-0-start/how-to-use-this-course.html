How to use this course

Also called: the study loop, the routine for each lecture. Start here for Zero to hero: neural networks, built, a build-along companion to Andrej Karpathy's Neural Networks: Zero to Hero.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A two-hour lecture watched straight through feels clear and leaves almost nothing you can rebuild. Without a routine that forces you to build, check and recall, you finish the series able to recognise the code but not write it.

Where it fits: This is the first card of the series. Every later card has the same parts (Prime, Predict first, ▶ Build it, the Study panel), and this card says what each one is for and the order to use them in. The reasons behind that order are in the learning-principles card.

By the end: Run the loop on a lecture without looking it up, and say what each step protects you from.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why does the loop put the big-ideas card before the video rather than after it?
  2. What does a mismatch with "You should see" tell you that a match doesn't, and what should you do before watching on?
  3. Which parts of a card are meant to be done with the card closed, and why closed?
  4. What goes in your notes, what goes in your repo, and what goes nowhere?

In one breath: for each lecture you skim its big-ideas card first, then watch the video with the course's cards open. You stop wherever a card says ▶ Build it, type the code yourself and compare your output with the card's "You should see". Then you work through the card's Study panel, and you move on only when the course's "done when" checklist is true.

Who it's for

You're a comfortable programmer with no deep-learning background, and you want to build every piece yourself and understand the code, the concepts and the math. Karpathy says the first lecture "only assumes basic knowledge of Python and a vague recollection of calculus from high school" (the micrograd video's description). Anything else the lectures assume but never teach, such as the chain rule, matrix shapes, broadcasting, softmax and your Python setup, is in z2h-1, the toolkit.

The series has one course per lecture, in the playlist's order: 9 lectures and 18 h 38 min of video in total (the lengths are in the schedule). The ninth, Let's reproduce GPT-2 (124M), is in the YouTube playlist but not on the course page. It is included here as the capstone. Every "You should see" output was measured on an Apple M3 with 24 GB running torch 2.14, so your last digits may differ where a card says so.

Predict first: you watch a two-hour lecture straight through and take careful notes, then type the final notebook from memory. Where does that plan break first?

At the first bug. Watching makes every step look obvious, but you have nothing to check your own code against until the very end, and by then a mistake could be in any of 40 steps. Stopping at each Build it keeps every check small: one step, one expected output. The careful notes don't help either, because copying what someone says is not the same as recalling it later (principle 2).

Anatomy of a card

Every concept card has the same parts, in the same order, so you always know where to look:

  1. Subtitle: the lecture, the chapter and a timestamp link. Prime block: the problem, where it fits, and the questions to read with.
  2. In one breath, then Predict first: answer it before you open it.
  3. A worked example using Karpathy's own numbers, then the ▶ Build it box: When to pause, the code to type, You should see (the real output, measured), and Done when. Its badge says where it runs: runs on your M3 · time your M3, slow · time needs a rented GPU pencil and paper
  4. A 2×2 grid comparing it with the alternatives, a note on the common misconception, the Study panel, and the footer (Watch, Code, Lab, Related).

The note-taking rule

Write the smallest note that captures a relationship, and add one line of your own: "Why this matters: …". Don't transcribe the lecture.

Not: "BatchNorm normalises the hidden layer." Instead: "Why this matters: normalising with batch statistics keeps tanh out of its flat tails → deeper nets train → but each example now depends on its batch → inference needs running stats."

The code you type goes in your repo, not in your notes. Your notes hold the why, and the arrows between ideas. Pasting is fine for a separate reference file, but it isn't learning (principle 2).

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Watch straight through

Everything looks obvious at 1× speed. You find out what you missed only when your own code fails, hours later and many steps away from the cause.

✗ Read the finished repo

You see the answer, not the order it was built in. micrograd's engine.py doesn't show you the = vs += bug that made += necessary.

✗ Transcribe notes

Long notes feel productive and put off the actual thinking. Recognising your notes later is not the same as recalling the idea.

✓ Prime, build, retrieve

Small, checkable steps with a measured answer, followed by questions you answer from memory. Every gap shows up while it's still one step wide.

When this isn't the right course

If you want to know what LLMs are and how they're trained, without building one, Karpathy's two general-audience talks are a better fit: Where next lists them. If you already know PyTorch and backprop well, you can skip ahead to z2h-8 or z2h-10, and use each course's big-ideas card to find the gaps you have.

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

You follow the loop for micrograd, and at the += card your a.grad for b = a + a prints 1.0 instead of the card's 2.0. Before looking at your code: which line do you expect is wrong, and what should you not do next?

Reveal

A _backward closure that uses = instead of +=. The two paths into a each send 1.0, and = keeps only the last one. What not to do is watch on and hope the lecture fixes it. This mismatch is exactly the bug that card is about, and diffing against the lab finds it in one line.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Plan your first real session with the loop. Pick micrograd: open its big-ideas card and prime for a few minutes. Then find the first ▶ Build it in the course (the derivative-as-nudge card) and do it at its timestamp. Write a single note in the rule's format.

Done when: Your f(3.0) prints 20.0 and your slope with h = 0.001 prints about 14.003 (the card's measured value is 14.003000), and your note is one line with a "why this matters".

Hint

f(x) = 3x² − 4x + 5. The slope is (f(3 + h) − f(3)) / h.
