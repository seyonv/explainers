Reading a noisy loss curve

Also called: smoothing the loss plot, a windowed (block) average of the training loss. Lecture 6 · let's fix the learning rate plot · 0:06:56

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Each training step logs the loss of one random 32-example minibatch, so plotting all 200,000 of them gives a thick smear. You can't tell whether a change (a new lr schedule, a new layer) helped, because the band is wider than the improvement.

Where it fits: This is the first cleanup in the WaveNet lecture. It reuses the minibatch training loop and the lr decay from the MLP course, and view from the tensor-shape cards, to make the loss plot readable before the architecture changes start.

  2. Which dimension does .mean(1) average over after view(-1, 1000), and what would .mean(0) give you instead?
  3. Why does averaging 1000 steps shrink the wiggle by about 31.6× and not 1000×?
  4. What does a bigger window buy, and what kind of event can it hide?
  5. Why is the smoothed curve not a fair number to compare two models with?

In one breath: every training step records the loss of one random minibatch (32 examples), so the raw list lossi jumps around so much that its plot is a thick smear. Chop the list into rows of 1000 steps and average each row: the smear becomes a thin line, and the drop where the learning rate (lr, the size of each weight update) is cut becomes easy to see.

Predict first: near the end of the full run, the lr is tiny (0.01) and the weights barely move from step to step. Yet within the last 1000 steps the raw loss swings between 1.37 and 2.82. What causes a swing that big?

The data, not the model. Each step scores a different random 32 examples. Some minibatches happen to be full of easy next-characters, some full of hard ones, so the same model scores 1.37 on one and 2.82 on another. The loss of 32 examples is a very noisy estimate of the loss on all 182,625 training examples. Averaging 1000 steps averages 32,000 examples, and the noise mostly cancels.

The one-line fix, shape by shape

lossi is a Python list with one float per step. torch.tensor(lossi) makes it a 1-D tensor. .view(-1, 1000) reinterprets the same numbers as a grid with 1000 columns (the -1 means "work out the number of rows yourself"), so row j holds steps 1000j to 1000j + 999. .mean(1) averages along dimension 1, across each row.

lossi 200,000 floats one per step torch.tensor(lossi) (200000,) .view(-1, 1000) (200, 1000) 200 = 200,000 / 1000 .mean(1) (200,) one average per row
sj= 11000average ∑i=1000j1000j+999 lossi[i]log10 loss at step i
s_jthe j-th point of the smoothed curvej = 0 … 199 lossi[i]log10 of the minibatch loss at step iabout 0.14–0.45 late in training 1000window size: steps averaged into one pointmust divide the step count

Why it works: the step-to-step wiggle is mostly random minibatch luck, and averaging n roughly independent wiggles shrinks them by about √n. On the quick run, the raw values in the last 1000 steps have a standard deviation (typical distance from their mean) of 0.043; √1000 ≈ 31.6, so each green point should wobble by only about 0.043 / 31.6 ≈ 0.0014 (computed; it assumes the steps are independent). That is why a real 0.010 drop now stands out.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Plot the raw list

200,000 points. In the last 1000 steps alone the band is 0.451 − 0.137 = 0.314 wide in log10, 3.5× the whole smoothed improvement (0.406 → 0.317 = 0.089), so the trend and the lr drop are buried.

✗ Bigger minibatches

Each step's loss gets less noisy, but every step now costs more compute. You'd be changing the training to fix a plot.

✗ Full training-set loss every step

Exact, but scoring all 182,625 examples each step costs about 5,700 minibatches' worth of work (182,625 / 32). Do it once at the end instead (split_loss).

✓ Average blocks of 1000 steps

Free: it reuses numbers you already logged. One line, no extra knob beyond the window size, and it shrinks the wiggle by about √1000 ≈ 31.6×.

What the smooth line is, and isn't

It is a training-set trend, not a validation score. Each point averages minibatches the model was training on at the time, with weights that moved during those 1000 steps. To compare models, use the train and val losses computed once at the end with training=False.

It averages log10 values. 10mean of logs is the geometric mean of the minibatch losses, a little below the plain average. On the quick run's last 1000 steps: 2.162 vs 2.172 (computed). Fine for reading trends, not a number to report.

When not to use it: the window must divide the step count (200,500 steps gives RuntimeError: shape '[-1, 1000]' is invalid for input of size 200500), and a 1000-step block hides anything faster than 1000 steps, such as a loss spike that blows up training. For those, look at the raw list, or use a sliding or exponential moving average (the "smoothing" slider in tools like TensorBoard).

Understand and remember

  • Each step's loss comes from a different random 32 examples → it measures minibatch luck as much as the model → the raw curve is a band about 0.31 wide in log10 late in training, wider than the whole improvement
  • Average 1000 consecutive steps → random wiggles mostly cancel, shrinking by about √1000 ≈ 31.6× if roughly independent → a real 0.010 drop, like the lr cut at step 150,000, becomes visible
  • view(-1, 1000) lays the 200,000 values out as 200 rows of consecutive steps → mean(1) averages within each row → one point per 1000-step block, in training order
  • Bigger window → less noise but fewer points and blurred timing → fine for trends, but it hides anything shorter than the window (a loss spike) → look at the raw list for those
  • detail that matters lossi holds loss.log10() → averaging logs gives a geometric mean → 10x of a smoothed point sits slightly below the plain average loss (2.162 vs 2.172 on the quick run's last 1000 steps)
  • detail that matters The window must divide the step count → with 200,500 steps view raises RuntimeError: shape '[-1, 1000]' is invalid for input of size 200500 → pick a step count that is a multiple of the window, or trim the list
