Mixed distributions

Also called mixed-type random variables, or a CDF with jumps. Part discrete, part continuous: the CDF climbs smoothly in places and leaps in others. The source guide has two such problems under Continuous Univariate Distributions, one with two jumps and one with a single jump at the left edge.

In one breath

A CDF can rise in two ways. Where it jumps, the variable lands on that exact point with probability equal to the jump height (a point mass). Where it rises smoothly, its slope is an ordinary density. Split the CDF into those two parts, handle each the usual way (sum for the jumps, integrate for the slope) and add the results.

In Python

from fractions import Fraction as Fr

def split(pieces):
    # pieces: [(a, b, c)], F(x) = sum c[k] x**k on [a, b)
    atoms, dens, left = {}, [], Fr(0)    # left = F(x-)
    for a, b, c in pieces:
        if peval(c, a) != left:          # F jumps at a
            atoms[a] = peval(c, a) - left
        dens.append((a, b, [k * ck for k, ck in enumerate(c)][1:]))
        left = peval(c, b)
    if left != 1:                        # final jump up to 1
        atoms[pieces[-1][1]] = 1 - left
    return atoms, dens

def moment(atoms, dens, n):
    total = sum(x**n * p for x, p in atoms.items())
    for a, b, f in dens:                # integral of x**n f(x)
        for k, fk in enumerate(f):
            m = k + n + 1
            total += fk * (Fr(b)**m - Fr(a)**m) / m
    return total

h = Fr(1, 2)
X = [(1, 2, [1, -1, h])]                 # (x**2 - 2x + 2) / 2
atoms, dens = split(X)
E, E2 = moment(atoms, dens, 1), moment(atoms, dens, 2)
print(atoms, E, E2 - E**2)
# → {1: Fraction(1, 2)} 4/3 5/36

peval(c, x) evaluates the polynomial. The full file also samples X by bisecting for the smallest x with F(x) ≥ u, which lands exactly on the jump for every u in (F(x−), F(x)].

Time O(P · d) · Space O(P · d) for P pieces of degree d: one pass to find jumps and differentiate, one integral per coefficient per moment. Fractions keep 547/7200 and 5/36 exact.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Differentiate F and integrate

Treats F′ as the whole density. For X it has total area 0.5, not 1, and gives E = 5/6 ≈ 0.83, below the smallest possible value of X, which is 1.

✗ Sum the jumps only

Treats X as discrete. It keeps the 0.5 at x = 1 and loses the other half of the probability that is spread over (1, 2).

✗ Simulate and read off

Gives 0.1391 with 10⁵ draws, not 5/36, and needs the generalised inverse to sample the jump at all. It's a check, not an answer.

✓ Split, then add

Jumps as a sum, slope as an integral, one row per piece. Every piece is checkable: the masses must total 1 (0.10 + 0.15 + 0.25 + 0.50 for Y).

Is the split always needed?

Not for the mean of a nonnegative variable: E[X] = ∫ (1 − F(x)) dx from 0 to ∞ works on the CDF directly, jumps and all. For X: 1 + ∫₁² (x − x²/2) dx = 1 + 1/3 = 4/3; for Y: 0.4083 + 0.125 = 8/15. Both match the trace, which makes it a good cross-check. For the variance you still need E[X²], and the split is the easiest route there.

Mixed laws show up in insurance: a payment with a deductible is exactly 0 with positive probability, and a policy limit puts a second lump at the cap (see deductibles and limits). "Mixture" usually means something else: a weighted blend of two densities, which is still purely continuous. And in theory there is a third kind, singular continuous (the Cantor distribution), with no jumps and no density; it doesn't come up in exam problems.

Pareto distribution and conditional probabilityAlso called the power-law distribution, or Pareto type I. The source guide's problem is insured home values with density 3x−4 above 1: given a home is insured for at least 1.5, how likely is it under…In one breathA Pareto variable never goes below a minimum xm, and its tail shrinks like a power, (xm/x)α, not exponentially. To condition on "at least a", keep only the probability above a and rescale it to 1: P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). For a Pareto, what's left above a is the same shape again, just stretched by a.
