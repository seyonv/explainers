Checkpoint 1: one curve, the survival function

Ties together mixed distributions, the Pareto and conditional probability, the exponential, the Weibull and the gamma function and deductibles and limits: how the tail S(x) = P(X > x) answers conditioning, means and insurance payments, and how its shape decides the answer.

How to use this page. Close the five cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure; it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you have to work out which idea applies before you can answer.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?A colleague computes G(115) for the health policy (Exp costs with mean 100, deductible 20, 100% up to 120, 50% above) and gets 0.5956. The question asked for the reimbursement's CDF given a reimbursement is made. Which idea did they skip, and what is the right number?
    Check
    • per payment vs per loss: "given Y > 0" means dividing by P(Y > 0) = S(20) = e−0.2 = 0.8187
    • 0.5956 = F(150) − F(20) is the unconditional P(0 < Y ≤ 115); it still counts the 18% of cases with no payment
    • 0.5956 / 0.8187 = 1 − e−1.3 = 0.7275

    Missed it? Reopen deductibles and limits, worked example 2.

  2. An order at the pharmacy (exponential, mean 10 min) has already run 5 minutes. What is the chance it runs at least 10 more? Now a home from the source's Pareto (α = 3, minimum 1) is known to be insured for at least 1.5. What is the chance it is insured for at least 3? Say what each answer depends on.
    Check
    • exponential: S(15)/S(5) = e−1.5/e−0.5 = e−1 = 0.3679; only the gap of 10 matters (memoryless)
    • Pareto: S(3)/S(1.5) = (1.5/3)³ = 1/8; only the ratio 3/1.5 = 2 matters (scale-invariant)
    • both are the same move, a ratio of survivals; the tail's shape decides what cancels

    Missed it? Reopen the exponential, "Memoryless, proven in one line", and Pareto, "The tail, and conditioning on it".

  3. For the source's one-jump CDF F(x) = (x² − 2x + 2)/2 on [1, 2), someone differentiates F, integrates x·F′(x) over (1, 2) and reports E[X] = 5/6. Why is that impossible, and what is E[X]?
    Check
    • X is never below 1, so a mean of 5/6 can't be right
    • F jumps by 0.5 at x = 1: a point mass that F′ doesn't see, so F′ only integrates to 0.5
    • E[X] = 1 × 0.5 + 5/6 = 4/3; or, with no split, 1 + ∫₁² (1 − F) dx = 1 + 1/3 = 4/3

    Missed it? Reopen mixed distributions, the trace for X and "Is the split always needed?".

  4. which concept?Two services log how long requests take. For service A, the log of the fraction still running falls in a straight line against time. For service B, a request that has already run a long time is more likely to finish in the next second than a new one. Which model fits each, and which property of the tail tells them apart?
    Check
    • A: exponential; a straight log-survival line means a constant hazard λ, which is memorylessness
    • B: a Weibull with shape k > 1; its hazard (k/λ)(x/λ)k−1 rises with age
    • the difference is in S: e−λx vs e−(x/λ)k; k = 1 turns B back into A

    Missed it? Reopen the exponential, "Is exponential the right model?", and Weibull, the words table (hazard).

  5. The rat's lifetime is Weibull with shape 3 and scale 120 weeks. Why is its mean not 120, and what is it? Which one substitution gets you there?
    Check
    • substitute y = (x/120)³, so x = 120y1/3 and f(x) dx = e−y dy
    • E[X] = 120 ∫ y1/3e−y dy = 120 Γ(4/3) = 120 × 0.8930 = 107.16 weeks
    • the mean equals the scale only for k = 1 (Γ(2) = 1); for k = 3 deaths cluster just below the scale

    Missed it? Reopen Weibull and the gamma function, part 2 of the worked example.

  6. which concept?A cloud credit pays nothing for the first hour of an outage, then pays the excess, capped at 10 hours. You are asked for the variance of the payment per outage. Which idea warns you that differentiating the payment's CDF won't be enough, and what do you do instead?
    Check
    • the payment function is flat below 1 hour and above 11 hours, so the payment has point masses at 0 and at 10: a mixed distribution
    • split it: atoms at 0 and 10 (their heights are F(1) and S(11)), plus the density in between
    • the mean alone could come from ∫₁¹¹ S(x) dx; the second moment needs the pieces

    Missed it? Reopen deductibles and limits, "Does this always work?", and mixed distributions, "The rule".

  7. The auto policy (deductible 1, car worth 15, in thousands) has expected payment ≈ 328. Why is the partial-damage part 0.04 × 1.2049 and not 0.04 × (E[X] − 1), and which part of the policy supplies most of the 328?
    Check
    • the payment is (X − 1)+, floored at 0; E[X] − 1 = −0.0083 lets small damage pay negative amounts
    • E[(X − 1)+] = ∫₁¹⁵ S(x) dx = 1.2049
    • the rare total loss: 0.02 × 14 = 0.28 thousand, so 280 of the 328

    Missed it? Reopen deductibles and limits, worked example 1.

3 · Project: price one policy layer under three tails

What it reinforces: conditioning as a ratio of survivals (Pareto, exponential), the Weibull's substitution and its Γ-based scale (Weibull), the expected payment as an area under S (deductibles and limits) and the point masses a policy creates (mixed distributions).

three loss models, all with mean 1.5: Pareto S(x) = x^-3 for x >= 1 (the source's homes) Exponential S(x) = exp(-x / 1.5) Weibull S(x) = exp(-(x / lam)^3), lam = 1.5 / gamma(4/3) policy: deductible d = 1.5, payments stop growing at a loss of u = 3 payment Y = min((X - 1.5)+, 1.5)

In about 40 lines of Python, standard library only:

  1. Tails. Write sf(x) for each model. For the Weibull, compute λ first and say why it isn't 1.5.
  2. Condition. For each model compute P(X < 2 | X ≥ 1.5) = 1 − S(2)/S(1.5).
  3. Per loss and per payment. With no cap, compute E[(X − 1.5)+] = ∫1.5∞ S dx (Simpson, or exactly where you can), then divide by S(1.5) to get the mean excess given a payment.
  4. Add the cap. Compute the layer ∫1.53 S dx, and the two point masses of Y: P(Y = 0) = 1 − S(1.5) and P(Y = 1.5) = S(3).
  5. Check. Simulate 10⁶ Pareto losses by inverse transform, X = (1 − U)−1/3, and compare the average payment with your layer.
  6. Explain. In two sentences: which model's mean excess grows with the deductible, which stays flat, which shrinks, and why the cap changes the ranking of the Pareto and the Weibull.
Done when: your program prints a 3-row table (S(1.5), the conditional probability, per loss, per payment, layer, both point masses), the simulation agrees with the Pareto layer to about 0.001, and your two sentences explain the ranking. Then compare with the check.

  • Mean excess: the Pareto's grows with the deductible (above any a it is a/2, so 0.75 here), the exponential's stays at its mean 1.5 (memoryless), and the shape-3 Weibull's shrinks (0.45): wear-out means a loss already past 1.5 is near its end.
