Coupon collector

Also called the coupon collector's problem, or "collect them all". The source guide asks it with a fair die: how many rolls, on average, to see all six faces, and what is the variance? It poses the question without an answer; this card derives both, then scales up to n coupons.

In one breath

Split the wait into stages: the rolls needed to see the 1st new face, then the 2nd, and so on. Each stage is a geometric wait whose success chance shrinks as fewer faces are left, so the total is a sum of independent geometrics. Add their means for E[T] and, because the stages are independent, add their variances for Var(T).

A geometric wait with success chance p has mean 1/p and variance (1 − p)/p². Stage k's chance depends only on how many faces are left, not on the rolls so far, which is why the stages are independent.

The last stage alone is 6 of the 14.7 rolls and 30 of the 38.99 variance. The distribution is right-skewed: the median is 13 rolls, P(T = 6) = 6!/6⁶ = 0.0154, and P(T > 30) = 0.0252 (exact, by inclusion–exclusion). A simulation of 10⁵ runs (seed 0) gives mean 14.700 (standard error ±0.020) and variance 39.19.

Scaling to n coupons

E[T] = nHn, and since Hn ≈ ln n + γ + 1/(2n) with γ = 0.5772, the mean grows like n ln n: each extra coupon costs a little more than the last.

In Python

from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import comb

def stages(n):
    """(p, E, Var) of each geometric stage: a new face w.p. p."""
    out = []
    for seen in range(n):
        p = F(n - seen, n)                 # chance the next is new
        out.append((p, 1 / p, (1 - p) / p**2))
    return out

def mean_var(n):
    st = stages(n)
    return sum(e for _, e, _ in st), sum(v for _, _, v in st)

def cdf(n, t):
    """P(T <= t) by inclusion-exclusion over the missed faces."""
    return sum((-1)**j * comb(n, j) * (1 - F(j, n))**t
               for j in range(n + 1))

E, V = mean_var(6)
print(f"E = {E} = {float(E)},  Var = {V} = {float(V)}")
print(f"SD = {float(V) ** 0.5:.3f}")
# → E = 147/10 = 14.7,  Var = 3899/100 = 38.99
# → SD = 6.244

Time O(n) for the moments (one term per stage) · Space O(n) for the stage list; cdf is O(n) terms per t. The full file adds the median, the 10⁵-run simulation and the unequal-probability mean.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ "Six faces, six rolls"

Treats each roll as if it must be new. That happens with probability 6!/6⁶ = 0.0154; the true mean is 14.7, about 2.45 times as many.

✗ Inclusion–exclusion for the mean

Summing P(T > t) = 1 − P(T ≤ t) over all t also gives 14.7, but through an alternating sum of large binomials. Good for tail questions, heavy for a mean.

✗ Just simulate

10⁵ runs gave 14.700 and 39.19: close, but noisy (±0.02 on the mean) and no formula to reuse for n = 52 or to see the n ln n growth.

✓ A sum of geometrics

Linearity gives the mean, independence gives the variance, both exact: 147/10 and 3899/100. It generalises to nHn in one line.

Does nHn always apply?

Only when every coupon is equally likely and draws are independent with replacement. With a loaded die, say one face at 1/2 and five at 1/10 (illustrative), a stage's chance depends on which faces are missing, so the stages no longer have fixed p. Inclusion–exclusion over the missing set still works, E[T] = Σ over nonempty sets S of (−1)|S|+1 / P(S), and gives 1439/63 ≈ 22.84 rolls, not 14.7. Rare coupons dominate.

If you draw without replacement (dealing a deck), it's exactly n draws, no randomness. And the mean says little about a bad day: for the die P(T > 30) = 2.5%. For large n the tail has a known limit (Erdős–Rényi): P(T < n ln n + cn) → e−e−c, a Gumbel shape. That same "wait for the last of n" structure is the expected maximum of n exponentials on the order-statistics card.
