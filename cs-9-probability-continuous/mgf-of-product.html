The MGF of a product

Also called the moment-generating function of Y1Y2, or the normal product distribution. The source guide asks for the MGF of U = Y1Y2 for two independent standard normals, then E(U) and V(U) from it, then a direct check. It states the problem only; this card solves all three parts.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: MGFs make sums easy (multiply them), so people reach for the same move on a product of random variables, and get the MGF of the sum instead. Products show up in signal × gain, price × quantity and interaction terms, and they need a different trick.

Where it fits: It combines cs-8's MGF toolkit (moments from derivatives) with the hierarchical card's tower rule: fix one factor, solve the easy problem, average over the other. It sits in the sums part as the case where "multiply the MGFs" does not apply.

In one breath

Multiplying MGFs works for a sum of independent variables, not a product. For a product, hold one factor fixed: once Y2 = y is known, U = yY1 is just a normal with variance y2, and you know its MGF. Then average that over Y2. That leaves a single Gaussian integral, and the answer is (1 − t2)−1/2.

The four words, two examples each

An MGF is a fingerprint of a distribution: moments fall out of its derivatives, and where it stops being finite tells you how fat the tails are.

In Python

import math, random

def mgf(t):
    """M_U(t) = E[e^(tU)]; finite only for |t| < 1."""
    if abs(t) >= 1:
        return math.inf
    return (1 - t * t) ** -0.5

def derivative_moments(h=1e-4):
    """M'(0) and M''(0) by central differences."""
    m1 = (mgf(h) - mgf(-h)) / (2 * h)
    m2 = (mgf(h) - 2 * mgf(0) + mgf(-h)) / h ** 2
    return m1, m2

m1, m2 = derivative_moments()
print(f"M'(0) = {m1:.6f} -> E[U] = 0")
print(f"M''(0) = {m2:.6f} -> E[U^2] = 1, Var = 1 - 0^2 = 1")

rng = random.Random(0)
us = [rng.gauss(0, 1) * rng.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(10 ** 6)]
mean = sum(us) / len(us)
var = sum((u - mean) ** 2 for u in us) / (len(us) - 1)
print(f"sim: mean {mean:+.4f}, var {var:.4f}")
# → M'(0) = 0.000000 -> E[U] = 0
#   M''(0) = 1.000000 -> E[U^2] = 1, Var = 1 - 0^2 = 1
#   sim: mean +0.0007, var 1.0005

Time O(1) for the formula; the simulation is O(n) with n = 106, about 3 s of CPU on 3.10. code/mgf-of-product.py also prints the Simpson check of step 5, the series moments and the tail comparison.

Why conditioning beats the alternatives

✗ Multiply the two MGFs

et²/2 × et²/2 = et² is the MGF of the sum Y1 + Y2, a N(0, 2) with variance 2. Products don't factor that way; the classic slip.

✗ Find U's density first

It is K0(|u|)/π, a modified Bessel function with a log spike at 0. Correct, but you would need Bessel integrals to get the MGF from it.

✗ The raw double integral

∫∫ ety₁y₂φ(y₁)φ(y₂) is a 2-D Gaussian with matrix [[1, −t], [−t, 1]] and determinant 1 − t2. Same answer, but only if you know the det−1/2 formula.

✓ Condition on one factor

Fixing Y2 turns the product into a normal whose MGF you already know. What's left is one 1-D Gaussian integral, and the domain |t| < 1 falls out of it.

Do you even need the MGF here?

Not for E(U) and V(U). Independence gives both in one line (steps 9 and 10), which is exactly the source's "check". The MGF earns its keep when you want the whole distribution: every moment at once, the tail weight (E[U4] = 9 vs 3), or recognising it. (1 − t2)−1/2 = (1 − t)−1/2(1 + t)−1/2 is the MGF of (A2 − B2)/2 for independent standard normals A, B: a difference of two chi-squares. That fits, since Y1Y2 = [(Y1+Y2)2 − (Y1−Y2)2]/4. It is not a Laplace: that one's MGF is (1 − t2)−1.

When the MGF is the wrong tool: it only exists on |t| < 1 here, and for the ratio Y1/Y2 (a Cauchy) it doesn't exist for any t ≠ 0. Then use the characteristic function E[eitU], which always exists; the same conditioning gives (1 + t2)−1/2 for this U (our derivation, not the source's).

Understand and remember

  • Products don't factor like sums → fix Y₂ = y so U = yY₁ is N(0, y²) → its MGF is et²y²/2 → average over Y₂ (tower rule) → one Gaussian integral gives MU(t) = (1 − t²)−1/2
  • M′(0) = 0 and M″(0) = 1 → E(U) = 0 and V(U) = 1, the same as a standard normal → the first two moments can't tell U from N(0, 1)
  • The MGF exists only for |t| < 1 (at t = 1 the et²y²/2 cancels the normal's e−y²/2) → exponential moments stop existing → U has heavier tails than a normal: E[U⁴] = 9 vs 3, P(|U| > 3) = 0.0196 vs 0.0027
  • detail that matters Independence makes every moment factor: E[U2k] = (E[Y2k])² → 1, 9, 225 → a quick check of the series without any MGF

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Now let Y₂ ~ N(0, 4) instead of N(0, 1) (Y₁ still standard normal). Before computing: what's the new domain of the MGF, and what's Var(U)?

Reveal

Given Y₂ = y the inner MGF is still et²y²/2, but averaging over N(0, 4) gives (1 − 4t²)−1/2, finite only for |t| < 1/2. Var(U) = E[Y₁²]E[Y₂²] = 1 × 4 = 4. A wider factor means heavier tails, so the domain shrinks.
