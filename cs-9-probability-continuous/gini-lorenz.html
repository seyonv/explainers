Lorenz curve and Gini index

Also called the Gini coefficient or Gini ratio; the Lorenz curve is also the concentration curve. The cheat-sheet's problem: a company's payroll has density f(x) = 3(1 − x)² over employees ranked from the best paid (x = 0) down, and G = 2∫|x − F(x)| dx. The source states the problem without an answer; it is G = 1/2.

In one breath

Line employees up from best paid to worst and plot, for each fraction x of them, the share F(x) of all pay they take home. If everyone were paid the same the curve would be the diagonal F(x) = x. The Gini index is twice the area between the curve and that diagonal: 0 means perfect equality, 1 means one person gets everything.

The words, two examples each

Think of a pizza shared along a queue ordered by appetite: the Lorenz curve is how much pizza is gone after each fraction of the queue, and the Gini index is how far that runs ahead of an even split.

The linear-pay company is illustrative, not from the source; its numbers are computed. In general f = (k + 1)(1 − x)k gives G = k/(k + 2): k = 0, 1, 2, 3 → 0, 1/3, 1/2, 3/5 (Simpson, matching).

In Python

def top_share(x):
    """F(x) = integral_0^x 3(1-t)^2 dt = 1 - (1-x)^3."""
    return 1 - (1 - x) ** 3

def gini_curve(F):
    """G = 2 * area between the curve and the diagonal."""
    return 2 * simpson(lambda x: abs(x - F(x)), 0, 1)

def gini_sample(pay):
    """Gini of a list of pay: mean |a - b| over pairs / (2 mean)."""
    w = sorted(pay)
    n = len(w)
    ranked = sum((2 * i - n + 1) * v for i, v in enumerate(w))
    return ranked / (n * sum(w))

def simulate(n=10**5, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    # employee at rank u from the top is paid in proportion to f(u)
    return gini_sample(3 * (1 - rng.random()) ** 2
                       for _ in range(n))

print(2 * (1 - Fraction(1, 4) - Fraction(1, 2)))  # exact
print(round(gini_curve(top_share), 6), round(simulate(), 4))
print(gini_sample([0] * 50 + [1] * 50))   # half paid nothing
# → 1/2

Time O(n log n) · Space O(n) for a sample of n salaries: sorting lets one pass weight each salary by its rank, replacing the O(n²) sum over all pairs. The curve version is O(m) for m Simpson steps.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Top-20% share

48.8% here. Easy to say, but it is one point on the curve and says nothing about how the other 80% split the rest.

✗ Standard deviation of pay

In currency units: give everyone a raise of 10% and it grows 10% with no change in fairness. Dividing by the mean (CV = √(4/5) ≈ 0.894 here) fixes scale but has no upper bound.

✗ Highest ÷ lowest pay

Uses two people only. Here the lowest-ranked employee earns f(1) = 0, so the ratio is infinite.

✓ Gini index

Uses the whole curve, ignores the currency and the headcount, and lands in [0, 1]. G = 1/2 also reads directly: the average pay gap between two random employees equals the average pay.

Is one number enough?

Not always. Different Lorenz curves can share a Gini. A company where half the staff earn nothing and the other half are paid equally also has G = 1/2 (the code's last line), yet its bottom half earn 0% against 12.5% here and its top 20% earn 40% against 48.8%: the two curves cross. When it matters where the inequality sits, report top and bottom shares too, or use the Theil index or the Palma ratio (top 10% ÷ bottom 40%).

Two conventions to watch. The source counts from the top, so its F sits above the diagonal; most texts rank from the bottom, L(p) = p³ here, and write G = 1 − 2∫L. Both give 1/2. And for a small sample of n people, the pairwise form above treats the sample as the whole population; some tools multiply by n/(n − 1) to estimate the population Gini (general practice, not from the source).
