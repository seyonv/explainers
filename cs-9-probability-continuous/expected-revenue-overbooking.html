Expected revenue: the overbooked bus

Also called overbooking, or the expected value of a piecewise payoff. The cheat-sheet's tour bus has 20 seats, sells 21 tickets at 50 each, and each tourist misses the trip with probability 0.02. What does the operator expect to earn, and how many tickets should he sell?

In one breath

Revenue is fixed money in (every fare, because tickets are non-refundable) minus a penalty that only bites when more people show up than there are seats. So the expected revenue is the fares minus the bump cost times the expected number of people left standing, and that expectation comes from the binomial count of who shows up.

E[R]= 50nfares kept − 100⋅E[(S−20)+]expected bump cost , S∼Binomial(n,0.98)

E[R] = 50*n - 100 * sum((k - 20) * C(n,k) * 0.98**k * 0.02**(n-k) for k in 21..n)

Filled in for n = 21: only k = 21 leaves someone standing, so E[(S − 20)⁺] = 0.98²¹ = 0.6543 and E[R] = 1050 − 65.43 = 984.57.

The source's answer, 21·0.98·50 − 0.98²¹·50 = 996, counts only the fares of tourists who show and charges 50 for a bump. But no-shows' fares are kept (non-refundable), and a bump costs 100. Corrected here: 984.57.

How many tickets should he sell?

Selling ticket n + 1 adds a sure 50 and costs 100 only if that tourist shows (0.98) and the bus is already full, P(Sn ≥ 20). So sell one more only while 50 > 98 · P(Sn ≥ 20). At n = 20 that is 98 · 0.98²⁰ = 65.43 > 50: stop. The bar axis starts at 750.

In Python

from math import comb

def expected_revenue(n, seats=20, fare=50, bump=100, p=0.98):
    """E[revenue] when n tickets are sold. S ~ Binomial(n, p)."""
    over = sum((k - seats) * comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k)
               for k in range(seats + 1, n + 1))  # E[(S - seats)+]
    return fare * n - bump * over

for n in range(20, 26):
    print(n, f"{expected_revenue(n):.2f}")
# → 20 1000.00
#   21 984.57
#   22 942.98

#   25 800.00

Time O(n) per ticket count · Space O(1). One binomial term per possible overflow k = 21..n; the full file also runs the 10⁵-trip simulation and the no-show sweep.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Count fares of those who show

The source's route: 21 · 0.98 · 50 = 1029. It drops the 21 fares already paid by no-shows, and it charges a bump 50 instead of 100. Result 996, off by 11.72.

✗ Plug in the average turnout

E[S] = 20.58, so "0.58 bumped" and 1050 − 58 = 992. Wrong: E[max(S − 20, 0)] ≠ max(E[S] − 20, 0) because the penalty is not linear. The true expected overflow is 0.6543.

✗ Sell seats ÷ show rate

20 / 0.98 = 20.4, round up to 21. That matches the expected turnout to the seats but ignores that a bump costs twice a fare: 984.57 < 1000 for selling 20.

✓ Expectation of the full payoff

Write revenue as a function of S, then average over the binomial. It gets 984.57 exactly, and its marginal form (50 vs 98 · P(full)) tells you when to stop selling.

Does overbooking always pay?

No. It depends on two numbers: how often people fail to show and how much a bump costs relative to a fare. Here the no-show rate (2%) is below the break-even 3.25%, so the source's 21st ticket loses 15.43 on average. At 10% no-shows the best is 22 tickets and 1056.23.

The model also assumes every tourist decides independently. Families and tour groups show up or stay home together, which fattens the tail of S and makes overbooking riskier than the binomial says. Real carriers also count goodwill and ask for volunteers before bumping anyone; that part is general practice, not from the source. With hundreds of seats, the normal approximation to the binomial replaces the exact sum.
