Tails, conditions and sums

The overview for course 9 of the cheat-sheet series. It ties together the 19 cards on the continuous half of the cheat-sheet's Univariate Random Variables page, its insurance and revenue applications, and the whole Multivariate page: one curve for tail questions, one move for anything with a hidden layer, and the normal for totals.

In one breath

Most continuous questions turn out to be about the tail, not the density. Write the survival function S(x) = P(X > x) first. Conditioning on "already past a" is then a ratio of two values of S, the minimum of independent values multiplies their S's, and a mean or an insurance payment is the area under S. When a problem has two layers (a parent and a child, a count and a total, a row and a column), fix the hidden one, solve the easy problem, and average. When it adds up many independent pieces, the total's mean and variance add, and the normal does the rest. Most of the traps in this course come from reaching for the normal or a bound when the exact tail was one line away.

The words people use

Per loss vs per payment

Whether you condition on a payment being made. Per loss counts the years with nothing paid; per payment divides them out by S(d). For the health policy that is 0.5956 against 0.7275, and the source picked the wrong one.

Light vs heavy tail

An exponential-type tail shrinks by the same factor over every fixed stretch; a power tail shrinks by the same factor each time x doubles. With α ≤ 2 the variance is infinite, and the CLT and Chebyshev both stop working.

Approximation vs bound

The normal is an estimate and can miss either way (11% low at 50 of 100). Chebyshev and Cantelli are guarantees for any shape, which makes them loose: 25× too big for the same tail. Use a bound only when you really don't know the shape.

✓ Survival function

S(x) = P(X > x), the chance of still being above x. Conditioning on a tail is S(b)/S(a), the min multiplies S's, and a mean or a policy layer is the area under S. Write it before the density.

When this advice doesn't apply

Every clean result here assumes independence: the orders at the counter, the tourists on the bus, the 20 jobs, the cars in the sample. Shared causes (one slow pharmacist, a family that travels together, one fleet depot) make values move together. Then F(a)ⁿ for the max is wrong, the bus's no-shows get a fatter tail than the binomial allows, and a sample carries less information than its size. The formulas still return numbers; check dependence on data before trusting them.

The named shapes are also the problems' assumptions, not facts: the pharmacy's "exponential" and the rat's Weibull were given, not fitted. The normal is weakest where questions usually ask, in the far tails; with a computer, sum the exact binomial or Poisson, or simulate and report the standard error. For heavy tails (a Pareto with α ≤ 2) the variance-based tools fail outright, so use medians, quantiles or the tail itself. The next course puts these tools to work on money: returns, prices and random walks.

Mixed distributionsAlso called mixed-type random variables, or a CDF with jumps. Part discrete, part continuous: the CDF climbs smoothly in places and leaps in others. The cheat-sheet has two such problems under…In one breathA CDF can rise in two ways. Where it jumps, the variable lands on that exact point with probability equal to the jump height (a point mass). Where it rises smoothly, its slope is an ordinary density. Split the CDF into those two parts, handle each the usual way (sum for the jumps, integrate for the slope) and add the results.
