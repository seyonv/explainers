Exponential distribution

Also called Exp(λ), the negative exponential, or the waiting time of a Poisson process. The source guide's Exponential heading is empty; its one exponential problem sits on the multivariate page: pharmacy orders take an exponential time with mean 10 minutes. This card uses that counter throughout.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Waiting times, time-to-next-request and decay are everywhere, and you need to answer "how long until the first of several finishes?" or "I've already waited 5 minutes, how much longer?" without integrals. Guess with intuition and you'll assume waiting brings the end closer, which for these processes it doesn't.

Where it fits: It follows the Pareto card's ratio S(b)/S(a), now with an exponential tail, and is the continuous twin of cs-8's geometric. The Weibull, order-statistics, deductible and normal-approximation cards all lean on it.

In one breath

If events arrive at a steady rate λ, completely at random, the time until the next one is exponential. The chance you are still waiting after x minutes is e−λx, so every extra minute cuts the survivors by the same factor. That is why the distribution is memoryless: having waited 5 minutes tells you nothing about the wait still ahead.

Memoryless, proven in one line

P(X>s+t∣X>s) = e−λ(s+t)e−λs = e−λt=P(X>t)s drops out

{X > s + t} sits inside {X > s}, so the conditional is a ratio of tails, and exponentials divide cleanly. It is the only continuous distribution with this property; the geometric is its discrete twin.

The minimum of exponentials is exponential

P(min(X1,…,Xn)>x) = ∏i=1ne−λixall still waiting = e−(λ1+⋯+λn)xExp(sum of rates)
two counters, each mean 10 (illustrative): rate 0.1 + 0.1 = 0.2 first order done: mean 1/0.2 = 5 min, P(min > 10) = e^(-2) = 0.1353 counter i finishes first with probability lambda_i / sum = 0.1 / 0.2 = 1/2

The minimum is over 10 only if every clock is, so the tails multiply and the rates add (independence needed). Simulated (10⁶ pairs, seed 1): mean 4.998. For 5 iid Exp(λ), P(min ≤ a) = 1 − e−5λa, the source's order-statistics problem.

In Python

import math
import random

def tail(lam, x):
    """P(X > x) for X ~ Exp(rate lam): no arrival in [0, x]."""
    return math.exp(-lam * x)

def cond_tail(lam, s, t):
    """P(X > s + t | X > s) as a ratio of tails."""
    return tail(lam, s + t) / tail(lam, s)

lam = 1 / 10                    # mean 10 min -> rate 0.1 per min
print(f"{tail(lam, 10):.4f}")
print([f"{cond_tail(lam, s, 10):.4f}" for s in (0, 5, 10, 30)])

rng = random.Random(0)
xs = [rng.expovariate(lam) for _ in range(10**6)]  # rate, not mean
print(f"{sum(x > 10 for x in xs) / len(xs):.4f}")

rng = random.Random(1)          # two counters: first to finish
mins = [min(rng.expovariate(lam), rng.expovariate(lam))
        for _ in range(10**6)]
print(f"{sum(mins) / len(mins):.3f} {tail(2 * lam, 10):.4f}")
# → 0.3679
# → ['0.3679', '0.3679', '0.3679', '0.3679']
# → 0.3674
# → 4.998 0.1353

Exact values come from math.exp; the simulations only check them. The full file also prints the median and the 50-of-100 binomial tail.

How it differs from related ideas

Four waiting-time models, all with mean 10 minutes. Compare the chance of a wait over 10 from scratch with the chance of 10 more after already waiting 10.

✗ Uniform(0, 20)

P(X > 10) = 0.5, but P(X > 20 | X > 10) = 0. The wait has a hard limit, so waiting brings the end closer.

✗ Gamma(2, 5)

Two exponential stages of mean 5. P(X > 10) = 3e−2 = 0.4060, then 5e−2/3 = 0.2256 given 10 already: it wears out, hazard rising.

✗ Weibull, shape ½

Scale 5. P(X > 10) = e−√2 = 0.2431, then 0.5567 given 10 already. Hazard falls: the longer it's been, the longer it will be.

✓ Exponential(0.1)

P(X > 10) = 0.3679 and P(X > 20 | X > 10) = 0.3679. Constant hazard; the only continuous memoryless law.

Is exponential the right model?

Use it for gaps between events that arrive independently at a constant rate: arrivals of a Poisson process, radioactive decay, or requests hitting a server over a short window. Then the minimum rule makes "which happens first" easy, which is why queueing models (M/M/1) and continuous-time Markov chains lean on it.

It is often wrong for durations of work. A service time with a setup step is closer to gamma, component lifetimes that wear out need a rising hazard (Weibull with shape > 1), and heavy-tailed jobs are closer to Pareto. The pharmacy's "exponential" is the problem's assumption, not a measured fact. A quick check on real data: plot log of the fraction still waiting against t; exponential data fall on a straight line with slope −λ (standard practice, not from the source).

Understand and remember

  • Events at a constant rate λ, independently → P(no event in [0, x]) = e−λx → the tail is one exponential, so every tail question is one exp call (e−1 = 0.3679 at the pharmacy)
  • {X > s + t} ⊂ {X > s} → P(X > s + t | X > s) = e−λ(s+t)/e−λs = e−λt → time already waited cancels → memoryless, constant hazard λ; the only continuous law with this property
  • min > x only if every clock is > x → with independence the tails multiply → e−(λ₁+…+λₙ)x → the first finisher is Exp(sum of rates) and clock i wins with probability λi/Σλ
  • detail that matters Rate vs scale parameterisation → random.expovariate takes λ, numpy's exponential takes 1/λ → passing the pharmacy's mean 10 where the rate belongs silently simulates waits of mean 0.1 min, 100× too short

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Geometric distribution: The geometric and the exponential are both memoryless. What's the continuous/discrete difference in the proof, and when would you use each?

Check
  • geometric: P(X > s + t | X > s) = (1 − p)t, trials counted in whole numbers
  • exponential: the same ratio with e−λt, time measured continuously
  • use the geometric for counts of trials, the exponential for clock time; the exponential is the limit of many tiny trials

vs Weibull distribution and the gamma function: Exponential vs Weibull: which parameter turns one into the other, what does it do to the hazard, and how does each fail on a part that wears out?

Check
  • the Weibull with shape k = 1 is the exponential
  • k > 1 makes the hazard rise with age, k < 1 makes it fall; the exponential's is flat
  • the exponential fails on wear-out by being memoryless; the Weibull fails on a bathtub-shaped hazard, since its hazard only moves one way

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Three counters with rates 0.1, 0.1 and 0.2 per minute are all busy. You've already waited 4 minutes and none has finished. What is the expected extra wait for the first one, and which counter is most likely to finish first?

Reveal

Memorylessness makes the 4 minutes irrelevant: the minimum is Exp(0.1 + 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.4), so the expected extra wait is 1/0.4 = 2.5 minutes. The rate-0.2 counter finishes first with probability 0.2/0.4 = 1/2; each of the others with 1/4.
