Deductibles and policy limits

Also called the payment function, ordinary deductible, policy cap, or coinsurance. The source guide has two exam-P insurance problems under Applications: an auto policy it leaves unsolved, and a health policy where its answer (0.596) is wrong. The correct answers are 328 and 0.7275.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Insurers, reinsurers and cloud credit programs don't pay the loss; they pay a function of it with a deductible, a cap and maybe a share. Questions about "the payment" answered with the loss's distribution come out wrong, and the source's own health answer (0.596) forgets to condition on a payment being made.

Where it fits: It combines three earlier ideas: the survival function (Pareto, exponential), the kinked payoff (overbooking) and the jumps of a mixed CDF (a payment is exactly 0 or exactly the cap with positive probability).

In one breath

The insurer doesn't pay the loss X. It pays a function of it, Y = g(X): nothing below the deductible, then the excess, sometimes only a share of it (coinsurance), and never more than the limit. Every question is about Y, so first draw g. Then, for a mean, add up the survival function over the band the policy covers. For a probability, run g backwards to find the matching loss, and divide by P(Y > 0) if the question says "given a payment is made".

The first holds because E[(X − d)+] = ∫d∞ S(x) dx, and the limit just cuts the integral off at u. A 50% coinsurance band would add 0.5 ∫ S(x) dx over that band.

The payment functions

Grey band: losses the insurer pays nothing on. Red dashed line: the cap. Because g only rises, "Y ≤ 115" is the same event as "X ≤ 150", which is all the health problem needs.

Worked example 1: the auto policy (expected claim payment)

A 15,000 car, deductible 1,000. Each year there's a 0.04 chance of partial damage X (in thousands) with density c·e−x/2 on (0, 15), and a 0.02 chance of a total loss.

The source prints the density as 0.5003ex/2. That must be e−x/2: only then does 0.5003 make it integrate to 1 (e+x/2 integrates to 3,614 on (0, 15)). Corrected here. The source gives no answer.

A simulation of 106 policy years gives 0.3282 thousand (seed 0, standard error 0.0020). The rare total loss supplies 280 of the 328.

Worked example 2: the health policy, G(115)

Costs X have F(x) = 1 − e−x/100. Deductible 20. The insurer pays 100% from 20 to 120 and 50% above 120. G is the CDF of the reimbursement given it is positive.

StepArithmeticValue
1 · invert g115 > 100, so the 50% bandx = 120 + (115 − 100) / 0.5150
2 · bandwhere the source stopsF(150) − F(20) = e−0.2 − e−1.5 = 0.8187 − 0.22310.5956
3 · conditionP(Y > 0)S(20) = e−0.20.8187
Answer0.5956 / 0.8187 = 1 − e−1.30.7275

Simulation: 0.7279 from 818,582 positive payments out of 106 costs (seed 0, standard error 0.0005). The exponent 1.3 is no accident: an exponential is memoryless, so given X > 20, the excess X − 20 is again Exp(mean 100), and Y ≤ 115 means that excess is ≤ 130.

The source's answer, 0.596, is the unconditional P(0 < Y ≤ 115). It never divides by P(Y > 0). Its first line also writes F((120 − 20) + 15/0.5), which is F(130), but then evaluates e−1.5, which is F(150). Corrected here.

In Python

import math
# simpson(f, a, b): Simpson's rule, 1,000 panels (see code/)

C = 1 / (2 * (1 - math.exp(-7.5)))   # auto density constant

def auto_sf(x):                        # P(X > x) on (0, 15)
    return C * 2 * (math.exp(-x / 2) - math.exp(-7.5))

def auto_expected(d=1, value=15):
    layer = simpson(auto_sf, d, value)  # E[(X - d)+]
    return 0.04 * layer + 0.02 * (value - d), layer

def health_cost_for(y, d=20, top=120):
    # g inverse: which cost pays exactly y?
    return d + y if y <= top - d else top + (y - (top - d)) / 0.5

def health_G(y, mean=100, d=20):
    F = lambda t: 1 - math.exp(-t / mean)
    return (F(health_cost_for(y)) - F(d)) / (1 - F(d))

pay, layer = auto_expected()
print(f"{pay:.4f} {layer:.4f}")
print(health_cost_for(115), round(health_G(115), 4))
# → 0.3282 1.2049
#   150.0 0.7275

Time O(n) for n Simpson panels, O(1) for G · Space O(1). The full file adds the simulations and prints G at 50, 100, 115 and 150 (0.3935, 0.6321, 0.7275, 0.8647).

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Treat the payment as the cost

F(115) = 1 − e−1.15 = 0.6834. That's the chance the cost is under 115, but a payment of 115 comes from a cost of 150.

✗ Skip the condition

F(150) − F(20) = 0.5956, the source's answer. It counts the 18% of cases with no payment in the denominator, but G is defined only over positive payments.

✗ Subtract the deductible from the mean

0.04 × (E[X] − 1) + 0.28 = 0.04 × 0.9917 + 0.28 = 0.3197, about 320. Damage under 1 would make a negative payment; (X − 1)+ floors it at 0, which lifts the mean from 0.9917 to 1.2049.

✓ Draw g, then invert or integrate

Probabilities: map y back to its loss and condition if the question says "given". Means: integrate S over the band the policy covers. Works for any deductible, cap or coinsurance mix.

Does this always work?

It works for one loss at a time with a payment function that only rises. Inverting fails where g is flat. Above a limit, every loss pays the same u − d, so G jumps there: the payment has a point mass at the cap. The auto payment has one at 14 (the total losses) and one at 0 (years with no claim). In that case, work with the probability mass directly, as the mixed-distributions card does.

Real policies often apply deductibles and out-of-pocket limits to a year's total across many claims. The payment is then a function of a random sum, and you'd model the number and size of claims together or simulate. That's our general framing, not something the source covers.

Understand and remember

  • The insurer pays Y = g(X), not X → g is 0 below d, slope 1 (or the coinsurance share) above, flat at the cap → E[(X − d)⁺] = ∫d∞ S(x) dx and a limit just stops the integral at u → the auto layer is ∫₁¹⁵ S = 1.2049
  • g only rises → "Y ≤ y" is the same event as "X ≤ g−1(y)" → a payment probability is a loss probability at the inverted point → Y ≤ 115 ⇔ X ≤ 150 in the 50% band
  • Per payment conditions on Y > 0 → divide by S(d) → G(115) = (F(150) − F(20))/S(20) = 1 − e−1.3 = 0.7275 → skipping the division gives the source's 0.5956
  • Flat stretches of g (below d, above the cap) put point masses in Y's law → Y is a mixed variable → invert only where g strictly rises and handle the jumps as mass
  • detail that matters An exponential loss is memoryless → given X > 20 the excess is again Exp(mean 100) → that's why the answer depends only on 1.3 = 130/100, and why G is so simple here

Reference only

  • the normalising constant c = 0.500277
  • the source's F(130)/e−1.5 slip
  • G at 50, 100, 150: 0.3935, 0.6321, 0.8647
  • the closed form 2c(2e−0.5 − 16e−7.5)
  • annual aggregate deductibles and out-of-pocket limits
