Sums and the central limit theorem

Also called the CLT, or the normal approximation for totals. The source guide's Linear Combinations section asks four questions about totals: A.J. and M.J.'s 20 jobs, a charity's 2025 gifts and the trees on 100 acres. It gives only the questions, with no answers. One recipe solves all of them; a fifth question there, the die-roll coupon collector, has its own card.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Totals are everywhere: time for 20 jobs, a year's donations, requests per hour, trees on 100 acres. You rarely know the shape of each piece, only its mean and sd, and the classic slip (adding sds instead of variances) makes a 1% event look like a 31% one.

Where it fits: It takes the normal card's Φ and applies it to sums of many independent pieces. The coupon-collector, normal-approximation and sample-size cards are all this recipe on a specific kind of total; the Weibull card's gamma distribution is the exact law it approximates for exponential jobs.

In one breath

Add up n independent values that share a mean μ and a standard deviation σ. The total's mean is nμ and its variance is nσ², so its spread grows like √n, not n. Once n is large, the total is close to normal, whatever shape the single values have. Standardise it and read the probability off Φ.

The four moves, two examples each

Think of each job as a noisy step. The steps' lengths add up in a straight line, but their errors partly cancel, so the total wanders only √n steps' worth of noise from where you'd expect it.

The middle of the distribution is right to about 0.003; the far left tail is off by about a fifth. Right-skewed job times (never negative, occasionally long) give a thinner left tail than the normal, and 20 terms aren't enough to wash that out. Other shapes with the same mean and sd would give other tails.

In Python

import math

def norm_cdf(z):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))

def norm_ppf(p, lo=-10.0, hi=10.0):   # bisection, no scipy
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if norm_cdf(mid) < p else (lo, mid)
    return (lo + hi) / 2

def sum_of(n, mu, sd):                  # mean and sd of a total
    return n * mu, sd * math.sqrt(n)

a_mu, a_sd = sum_of(20, 50, 10)          # A.J.
m_mu, m_sd = sum_of(20, 52, 15)          # M.J.
print(norm_cdf((900 - a_mu) / a_sd))
print(norm_cdf((m_mu - a_mu) / math.hypot(a_sd, m_sd)))
c_mu, c_sd = sum_of(2025, 3125, 250)     # charity
print(c_mu + norm_ppf(0.90) * c_sd)
# → 0.0126736593387341
#   0.6901018057529335
#   6342542.455112377

Time O(1) per question, plus 100 bisection steps for a percentile. math.hypot is √(a² + b²), the sd of a difference. The full file also does M.J., the trees (normal and exact Poisson) and the gamma simulation.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Adding the sds

The classic slip: sd 20 × 10 = 200 instead of 44.72. Then z = −0.5 and P(A.J. < 900) comes out 0.3085, about 24 times too big. Only variances add.

✗ A distribution-free bound

Cantelli's one-sided bound needs only the mean and sd: with k = 100 / 44.72 = 2.236, P ≤ 1 / (1 + k²) = 1/6 = 0.167. It's guaranteed, but 13 times looser than the CLT's 0.0127.

✗ Exact convolution

Convolve the single-job density 20 times. That's exact, but the source gives only a mean and sd, so there is no density to convolve. It works when the family is closed under sums, as with the Poisson trees.

✓ The CLT

It needs just n, μ and σ, gives a number in two lines, and lands within about 0.003 of the checks in the middle of the distribution (0.6455 vs the exact 0.6449).

Is the normal approximation always safe?

No. It is weakest in the far tails, as the gamma check above shows: at z = −2.2 it was about 20% high, and for rare events (z of 4 or more) the relative error can be much worse. Use the exact distribution when you have one (Poisson, binomial, gamma), or simulate. With a small n and a skewed value, the normal can even put probability on negative totals.

It fails outright when σ is infinite: a Pareto with shape ≤ 2 has no finite variance, and a few huge values dominate the sum. It also needs independence. Jobs that share a cause (the same slow machine, the same bad day) are positively correlated, which adds covariance terms, widens the total's spread and fattens both tails.

Understand and remember

  • Independent pieces → variances add (covariances are 0) → Var(Sn) = nσ² → sd = σ√n: noise partly cancels, so totals are relatively tighter as n grows (44.72 for 20 jobs, not 200)
  • Many independent pieces → the total is close to normal whatever each piece's shape → standardise with nμ and σ√n and read Φ → P(A.J. < 900) = Φ(−2.236) = 0.0127
