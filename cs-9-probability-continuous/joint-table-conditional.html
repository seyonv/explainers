Joint and conditional distributions from a table

Also called a contingency table, or a bivariate pmf with its marginals. The source guide gives basketball players' shoe size S and height H as a 2 × 3 table with one cell missing, then asks for the expected height and shoe size, E[S | H = 73], and the shoe size's coefficient of variation for a 5'8" (68-inch) player. It states the questions only; every answer below is worked here.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Two measured things (shoe size and height, region and latency, plan and churn) come as one table of joint chances. Answering "what's the average shoe size of 73-inch players?" by summing a column, or by ignoring the height, gives numbers that aren't even shoe sizes.

Where it fits: First multivariate card. It uses cs-8's Bayes table (rescale a column) on a two-variable joint pmf, and sets up the vocabulary (joint, marginal, conditional) that the hierarchical-models and conditioning-on-a-sum cards build on.

Builds on: Bayes' theorem as a table · Conditional independence

You'll meet: joint pmf: one cell marginal: a row or column sum conditional: a column ÷ its total conditional mean and CV the tower check Σ P(h)·E[S | h] = E[S]

By the end: From any two-way table, fill a missing cell, get both marginals, compute a conditional distribution with its mean, variance and CV, and test independence.

In one breath

A joint table gives the chance of every (shoe size, height) pair at once, so all its cells sum to 1. Add along a row or down a column and you get a marginal: the distribution of one variable when you ignore the other. Keep one column and divide it by its total and you get a conditional distribution: what S looks like once you know H.

The rule

pH(h)=∑sp(s,h)marginal: sum a column E[S∣H=h]=∑ss·p(s,h)pH(h)conditional mean: rescale the column to 1
p(s, h)joint: one cell of the tablep(12, 73) = 0.23 pH(h)marginal of H: the column totalpH(73) = 0.38 p(s, h) / pH(h)conditional pmf of S given H = h; sums to 1 over s15/38, 23/38 CVcoefficient of variation: SD ÷ mean, a unit-free spread0.1436 at 68
CV(S | H = h) = sqrt(Var(S | H = h)) / E[S | H = h] Var(S | H = h) = E[S² | H = h] − E[S | H = h]²

Filled in for the source: E[S | H = 73] = (8.5 × 0.15 + 12 × 0.23) / 0.38 = 4.035 / 0.38 = 807/76 ≈ 10.618.

The five words, two examples each

Think of the table as a crowd of 100 players sorted into six boxes. Joint is one box, marginal is a whole row or column of boxes, and conditional is "only look at the 38 players who are 73 inches".

In Python

import math
from fractions import Fraction as F

JOINT = {                                 # (s, h) -> P(S=s, H=h)
    (F(17, 2), 68): F(25, 100), (F(17, 2), 70): F(20, 100),
    (F(17, 2), 73): F(15, 100), (F(12), 68): F(5, 100),
    (F(12), 70): F(12, 100),
}
JOINT[(F(12), 73)] = 1 - sum(JOINT.values())  # the "?" cell

def mean(dist):
    return sum(x * p for x, p in dist.items())

def cond_s(joint, h):
    """P(S = s | H = h): one column, divided by its total."""
    col = {s: p for (s, hh), p in joint.items() if hh == h}
    tot = sum(col.values())
    return {s: p / tot for s, p in col.items()}

def cv(dist):
    m = mean(dist)
    var = sum((x - m) ** 2 * p for x, p in dist.items())
    return math.sqrt(var) / m, var

Time O(cells) · Space O(cells). Each marginal or conditional is one pass over the table; with r rows and c columns, all c conditional means together still cost one pass, O(rc).

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Forget to divide

8.5 × 0.15 + 12 × 0.23 = 4.035. That is E[S · 1{H = 73}], not a shoe size at all: the column holds only 38% of the probability.

✗ Ignore the height

Answer E[S] = 9.9 for everyone. It only works if S and H are independent, and they aren't: 0.25 ≠ 0.60 × 0.30.

✗ Average the two sizes

(8.5 + 12) / 2 = 10.25 treats both sizes as equally likely among 73-inch players, but the column says 0.15 vs 0.23.

✓ Column ÷ column total

15/38 and 23/38 sum to 1, so the column is a real distribution: its mean is 807/76 ≈ 10.618, and variance and CV come out of the same two numbers.

Is it always this easy?

Only for discrete tables. For continuous variables P(H = 73) is 0, so you divide the joint density by the marginal density instead: f(s | h) = f(s, h) / fH(h), a slice of the surface rather than a column. The idea is the same; the sums become integrals. The hierarchical-models card works the other direction, building the joint from a marginal times a conditional.

Two cautions. A CV only means something for positive, ratio-scale quantities like shoe size; for a variable centred near 0 it blows up. And a table like this is a model, usually estimated from counts: a column with few players (here, the 68-inch 12s at 0.05) gives a noisy conditional, so with real data report the counts too. That last point is general practice, not from the source.

Understand and remember

  • A joint table gives P(S = s, H = h) for every pair → all cells sum to 1 → the missing cell is 1 − 0.77 = 0.23 → row and column sums give the marginals
  • Condition on H = h → keep one column and divide by its total pH(h) → the column becomes a distribution summing to 1 → its mean is E[S | H = 73] = 4.035/0.38 = 807/76 ≈ 10.618
  • Independence would mean every cell = product of its marginals → 0.60 × 0.30 = 0.18 ≠ 0.25 → S and H are dependent → the conditional mean moves with h (9.083 at 68, 10.618 at 73)
  • Averaging the conditional means with the column weights recovers the overall mean → Σ P(h)·E[S | h] = 9.9 = E[S] → the tower rule as a free check
  • detail that matters A two-point conditional has variance p(1 − p)·gap² → Var(S | 68) = (5/6)(1/6)(3.5)² = 245/144 → CV in one line without summing squares

Reference only

  • the source's six cell values
  • E[H] = 70.54, E[S] = 9.9, Cov(S, H) = 1.309
  • the unconditional CV 0.1732
  • continuous version f(s | h) = f(s, h)/fH(h)
