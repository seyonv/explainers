Consensus with Raft

Also called replicated-log consensus, leader election with log replication, or a replicated state machine. The source guide's image-resize queue uses "Paxos consensus" for leader election and automatic failover, and it credits Raft for etcd and Consul, but it never explains how consensus works. This card teaches Raft from the Raft paper.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The image service's queue must fail over automatically: a human is too slow for 99.9%, and a backup that promotes itself on silence creates two leaders during a partition. You need a group of servers that agrees on one leader and one log, never loses a committed entry, and keeps going while a majority is up.

Where it fits: Builds on the service-discovery card's majority quorums and the quorums card's overlap argument; it's the "Paxos leader election" the architecture card relied on. This is the coordination the consistency part says to use only for the rules a merge can break.

In one breath

A small group of servers (usually 3 or 5) keeps identical copies of an ordered log. One elected leader appends every new entry and counts an entry as committed once a majority has stored it. If the leader dies, a follower whose timer runs out asks for votes, and it can only win if its log holds every committed entry. Any two majorities share at least one server, so nothing committed is ever lost.

Worked example: five queue nodes, the leader crashes

The image service's queue runs on five nodes, S1 to S5. S1 leads term 1 and has written 4 entries (enqueue operations). Entry 4 reached S1, S2 and S3 (3 of 5), so it is committed. S4 is one entry behind and S5 two. Then S1 crashes, right after its last heartbeat at t = 0.

The election, step by step

Each follower draws an election timeout uniformly from 150–300 ms (the Raft paper's example range). Whoever hears nothing for that long becomes a candidate: it bumps the term, votes for itself and sends RequestVote to everyone. A server grants at most one vote per term, and refuses any candidate whose log is less up to date than its own (compare the last entry's term, then the log length).

Then the new leader repairs the logs. S3 appends a no-op for term 4 at index 5 and sends AppendEntries with the entry just before it (prevLogIndex, prevLogTerm). A follower that lacks that entry says no, and the leader steps back one index and tries again:

S2 [1,1,1,1] prev=4 ok → append 5 S4 [1,1,1] prev=4 no, prev=3 ok → append 4, 5 S5 [1,1] prev=4 no, prev=3 no, prev=2 ok → append 3, 4, 5 index 5 (term 4) is on S2, S3, S4, S5: 4 ≥ 3 → commitIndex = 5

A leader counts replicas only for entries from its own term (Raft §5.4.2), which is why it writes the no-op: committing index 5 commits 1–4 with it.

Why S4 and S5 could never win: two majorities overlap

q=⌊n2⌋+1majority |A∩B|≥2q−n≥1any two majorities share a server
nservers in the group5 here qvotes to win an election, or copies to commit an entry3 Athe servers that stored entry 4S1, S2, S3 Bthe servers that vote for the winnerany 3 of 5

Filled in: 2 × 3 − 5 = 1. Every winning coalition includes S1, S2 or S3. S1 is dead, and S2 and S3 refuse anyone missing entry 4, so only a candidate holding entry 4 can collect 3 votes. S4's best was S4 + S5 = 2.

In Python: the vote rule and the commit rule

N, MAJORITY = 5, 5 // 2 + 1          # 5 nodes, 3 votes to win

def up_to_date(c_last_term, c_last_idx, my_log):
    my_term = my_log[-1] if my_log else 0
    if c_last_term != my_term:          # later last term wins
        return c_last_term > my_term
    return c_last_idx >= len(my_log)     # same term: longer wins

def on_request_vote(node, cand, term, last_term, last_idx):
    if term > node["term"]:              # newer term: follow it
        node.update(term=term, voted=None, state="follower")
    grant = (term == node["term"]
             and node["voted"] in (None, cand)   # one vote a term
             and up_to_date(last_term, last_idx, node["log"]))
    if grant:
        node["voted"] = cand
    return grant

def commit_index(match, leader_log, term):
    for i in range(len(leader_log), 0, -1):
        if sum(m >= i for m in match) >= MAJORITY \
                and leader_log[i - 1] == term:  # own term only
            return i
    return 0

# commitIndex = 5: entries 1..5 are committed

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Manual failover

Page a human to promote a replica. The source: "too slow to meet a 99.90% SLA". 99.9% allows 43.2 min down a month; one 15-minute failover (illustrative) uses 35% of it.

✗ Heartbeat failover, no quorum

The backup promotes itself when the primary goes quiet. In a partition both sides think the other died: two leaders take writes (split brain). Without a majority vote nothing stops it.

✗ Hand-rolled Paxos

Correct and equivalent in safety, but the source says "Paxos is very hard to implement", and the Raft paper was written because Paxos is hard to understand and needs big changes to become a real system.

✓ Raft

Automatic, majority-checked failover: here 274 ms, with committed entry 4 kept. Split into election, replication and safety so it can be understood, and shipped in etcd and Consul.

Should every service run consensus?

No. The source's advice is "avoid coordinating machines wherever possible". Consensus puts a majority round trip on every write (69 ms above for a cross-continent group) and stops accepting writes whenever a majority is unreachable: it picks consistency over availability. Use it for small, critical state such as who the leader is, cluster membership, configuration and locks, and usually through etcd, Consul or ZooKeeper rather than your own code. Bulk data goes elsewhere: the image service's usage records go to an eventually consistent store.

Two limits. Raft is always safe but only live when timing behaves: the paper asks for broadcast time ≪ election timeout ≪ mean time between failures, and FLP (in the source's networks page) says no algorithm can guarantee progress on a fully asynchronous network. And it assumes crash faults only: a server that lies (a Byzantine fault, in the source's Figure 3) needs a different family of protocols.

Understand and remember

  • Leader silent past a follower's randomised timeout → it becomes a candidate for a new term and asks for votes → one vote per server per term, granted only if the candidate's log is at least as up to date → a majority elects one leader per term
  • An entry is committed once a majority stores it → any two majorities overlap (2q − n ≥ 1) → every winning coalition includes someone holding every committed entry, who refuses candidates that lack it → the new leader always has every committed entry
  • The leader replicates with AppendEntries carrying the previous entry → a follower missing it says no → the leader steps back one index and retries → logs are repaired to match the leader's
  • A commit waits for the leader plus q − 1 acks → latency = the (q − 1)-th fastest follower RTT (69 ms for 5 nodes from East US) → and with no majority reachable, writes stop: consensus picks consistency over availability
  • detail that matters A leader counts replicas only for entries from its own term → so it appends a no-op on election → committing that no-op commits all earlier entries with it → without the rule, an old-term entry on a majority could still be overwritten

2 · Compress write it yourself first

From memory, write Raft as three chains: election (timeout → candidate → votes with the up-to-date check → leader), replication (append → majority → commit, own term only), safety (majorities overlap → committed entries survive). Put one number on each.

On paper or in your notes, in your own words, without scrolling up. Aim for the smallest thing that still explains it.

Then compare with this checklist
  • randomised timeouts avoid split votes (150–300 ms)
  • one vote per term, only for an up-to-date log
  • commit = stored on a majority; the leader counts only its own term's entries (hence the no-op)
  • AppendEntries repairs followers by stepping back to the last matching entry
  • two majorities share a server, so committed entries can't be lost
  • every write pays the majority's round trip; no majority → no writes

vs Quorums and quantifying 'eventual': Both use majorities, but a Dynamo-style store with w = r = 2 of 3 can still return an old value under LWW, and Raft can't. Which design choice makes the difference?

Check
  • Raft has one leader per term that orders every write in one log; there are no concurrent versions to pick between
  • the quorum store lets any replica accept writes, so it must choose a winner by timestamp, and clocks skew
  • the price: Raft needs a leader election on failure and refuses writes without a majority

vs Partial failure and CAP: In CAP terms, what does a Raft group do on each side of a partition that splits 5 nodes into 3 and 2, and why is that not a contradiction of "highly available"?

Check
  • the 3-node side elects a leader (if needed) and keeps committing
  • the 2-node side can't get a majority: it refuses writes
  • CAP's A requires every non-failing node to answer, so this is CP; operators call it highly available because the majority side stays up

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

The old leader S1 restarts after S3 has become leader of term 4. S1 still believes it leads term 1 and sends AppendEntries to S2. What happens to S1's message, and what happens to S1? Commit first.

Reveal

S2 is in term 4, so it rejects an AppendEntries from term 1 and replies with its current term. S1 sees a higher term (4 > 1), steps down to follower and adopts term 4. From then on it accepts S3's AppendEntries, and its log gets index 5 (the term-4 no-op) appended. Terms are what stop a returning old leader from causing split brain.
