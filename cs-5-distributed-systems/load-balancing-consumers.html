Spreading work: competing consumers vs partitions

Also called a work queue, a shared subscription (JMS) or a consumer group over a partitioned log (Kafka). The source guide files this under Load Balancers, but it's about message brokers splitting work among consumers, not the L4/L7 balancers in front of web servers.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Resize jobs pile up in a broker, and several workers must share them. If the broker hands out whole partitions, one huge image blocks every job behind it and idle workers can't help. If it hands out single jobs to whoever is free, the load evens out but the order is gone. Picking the wrong model shows up as idle workers next to a growing backlog.

Where it fits: Builds on the sizing card (10 conversions/s per core, 1 tick = 0.1 s) and the backpressure card's queues. It's the messaging half of "load balancing"; the partition idea returns in sharding and hot keys.

Builds on: Sizing a service: image resize · Backpressure, percentiles, yield and harvest

You'll meet: competing consumers (AMQP/JMS) partition assignment: consumers ≤ partitions range assignment: 6 over 4 → 2, 2, 1, 1 head-of-line blocking the greedy bound TP ≤ T1/P + T∞

By the end: Simulate both models on a backlog with one slow job, explain the finish times from the mechanisms, and choose the model from what the work needs: independence or per-key order.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why do jobs 10, 16 and 22 wait 9 ticks, and why does no job wait behind job 4 with competing consumers?
  2. Even with every job taking 1 tick, partitions finish at tick 8 but competing consumers at tick 6. Where do the 2 ticks go?
  3. What happens when you add a 7th and 8th consumer to a 6-partition topic?
  4. Why does the competing-consumers queue stay within the greedy bound while the partition schedule can break it?
  5. What do partitions give you that competing consumers throw away?

In one breath

A broker can share work two ways. Competing consumers: every message goes to exactly one consumer, whichever is free, so the load evens out by itself but order is lost. Partitions: the broker hands each consumer whole partitions of a log, and each consumer reads its partitions in order, so order per partition is kept but one slow message holds up everything behind it.

Worked example: 24 resize jobs, 6 partitions, 4 consumers

The image-resize service from this course: one core does 10 conversions/s, so a normal job takes 1 tick = 0.1 s. Job i lands in partition i mod 6. Job 4 is a huge image that takes 10 ticks (illustrative). All 24 are already waiting at tick 0.

In Python

import heapq
P, C, N, SLOW = 6, 4, 24, 4
cost = [10 if i == SLOW else 1 for i in range(N)]

def range_assign(parts, consumers):   # Kafka's RangeAssignor
    q, r = divmod(parts, consumers)
    out, start = [], 0
    for c in range(consumers):
        n = q + (c < r)             # first r get one extra
        out.append(list(range(start, start + n)))
        start += n
    return out

def competing():                  # next job to first idle consumer
    done, free = [0] * N, [(0, c) for c in range(C)]
    for i in range(N):
        t, c = heapq.heappop(free)
        done[i] = t + cost[i]
        heapq.heappush(free, (done[i], c))
    return done

def partitioned(assign):          # one job at a time, in order
    done = [0] * N
    for parts in assign:
        t = 0
        for i in range(N):
            if i % P in parts:
                t += cost[i]
                done[i] = t
    return done

# assignment [[0, 1], [2, 3], [4], [5]] [2, 2, 1, 1]
# competing  finish 11  mean of 23 normal 93 / 23 = 4.04  stuck 0
# partitions finish 13  mean of 23 normal 118 / 23 = 5.13  stuck 3
# 8 consumers, 6 partitions: [[0], [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [], []]

The source says consumers can't exceed partitions. More can join the group, but as the last line shows, the extras get no partition and sit idle.

Time O(N log C) · Space O(N + C). Competing pops and pushes a heap of C consumers once per job. Partitioned, as written, scans all N jobs per consumer (O(N·C)); a real consumer only reads its own partitions, so it's O(N) in total.

Why the shared queue won here: the greedy bound

The source's last paragraph is about online schedulers: they assign work without knowing what comes next. A competing-consumers queue is a greedy centralised scheduler: it never lets a consumer idle while a job waits. CLRS proves every such schedule finishes within:

TP≤ T1Pwork, shared out + T∞longest chain
T1total work: 23 × 1 + 1033 ticks T∞longest chain that must run in order; jobs are independent, so the slow job10 ticks Pconsumers4
bound: 33 / 4 + 10 = 18.25 ticks no schedule beats max(33/4, 10) = 10 got: competing 11 (inside the bound, 1 tick from optimal) all 1-tick jobs: bound 24/4 + 1 = 7 · competing 6 · partitions 8 > 7

The partition schedule breaks the bound because it isn't greedy: C3 idles while C0 still has jobs. Theorem 27.1 in CLRS 3rd ed.; applying it to a broker is our reading.

Picking one for the resize jobs

✗ One consumer

No sharing at all: 33 ticks for this backlog, 3× the 11 of four competing consumers.

✗ Partitions, 6 over 4

The 2, 2, 1, 1 split leaves two consumers idle, and job 4 blocks its partition: 13 ticks, 3 jobs stuck.

✗ Add consumers past 6

With partitions, consumers 7 and 8 get no partition. Scaling stops at the partition count.

✓ Competing consumers

Resize jobs are expensive and independent, the case the source names. The free consumer takes the next job: 11 ticks, nothing stuck.

So are partitions the worse design?

No. They're the right choice when order per key matters or when you need replay and fan-out: usage events for one account feeding billing, or a change log for one row. Competing consumers lose that order (partition 4's jobs finished 10, 16, 22, 4 above), and a queue deletes a message once it's acknowledged.

To soften the partition costs: use a partition count that's a multiple of the consumer count (12 over 4 gives 3 each), key by something fine-grained so one slow key doesn't share a partition with many others, and send expensive, order-free work to a separate queue. These fixes are common practice, not from the source.

Understand and remember

  • Competing consumers: each message goes to whichever consumer is free → no consumer idles while work waits (a greedy schedule) → load evens out and a slow job blocks only its own consumer → but messages finish out of order (10, 16, 22 before 4)
  • Partitions: each consumer owns whole partitions and reads them in order → per-partition order is kept, and the log allows replay and fan-out → but one slow message blocks everything behind it (head-of-line blocking: 3 jobs stuck)
  • Assignment is by whole partitions → 6 over 4 splits 2, 2, 1, 1 → two consumers idle while two still have work (8 ticks vs 6 even with no slow job) → and consumers past the partition count get nothing
  • A greedy scheduler never idles while work waits → finishes within T1/P + T∞ (33/4 + 10 = 18.25; got 11) → a non-greedy partition schedule can exceed it (8 > 7 with all 1-tick jobs)
  • detail that matters Choose the partition count as a multiple of the consumer count (12 over 4 → 3 each) and key by something fine-grained → evens the split and dilutes a slow key → softens both partition costs without losing per-key order

Reveal

range_assign gives 2, 2, 2: C0 p0,1 · C1 p2,3 · C2 p4,5. Competing consumers still finish at tick 11 (mean of the 23 normal jobs 5.78). Partitions finish at tick 17: C2 reads jobs 4, 5, 10, 11, 16, 17, 22, 23 one at a time, so all 7 of its normal jobs finish after job 4's 10 ticks (mean 7.39). An even split fixed the idle consumers but made the head-of-line blocking worse, because the slow partition now shares its consumer.
