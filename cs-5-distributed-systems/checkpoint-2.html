Checkpoint 2: silence, timeouts and finding servers

Ties together the network is unreliable, the end-to-end argument, delay, timeouts and congestion control, the path of a request, service discovery and competing consumers vs partitions: what a caller can and can't learn from the network, and how requests find a live server anyway.

How to use this page. Close the six cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure; it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you have to work out which idea applies before you can answer.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?A nightly job copies a 10 GB archive over TCP through ten routers, each of which checks a CRC on its own link. Every CRC passes, yet the copy on the far side sometimes differs from the original. Which idea explains it, and what is the cheapest reliable fix?
    Check
    • the end-to-end argument: each link check covers only its own wire; bytes damaged in a router's memory between two checks are covered by nothing below the ends
    • only a hash made by the sender's app and checked by the receiver's app can see every fault on the path
    • hash and resend per chunk, not per file: at 10−9 per byte per hop, a whole 10 GB file essentially never gets through clean, while 1 MB chunks cost 1.0% extra

    Missed it? Reopen the end-to-end argument, the worked example and the chunk table.

  2. A resize call times out at 110 ms. Of the six things that can go silent, in how many did the resize run anyway, and what does that force on your retry?
    Check
    • ran anyway in 4 of 6: request queued, worker paused, reply lost, reply delayed
    • never ran in 2: request lost, worker crashed
    • so a blind retry resizes (and bills) twice in the first group, and giving up loses work in the second: the retry must be idempotent

    Missed it? Reopen the network is unreliable, the six-case timeline and its tally.

  3. which concept?After the API servers move to new addresses, some clients keep connecting to the old ones for five minutes, and a few never notice a server that died. Which mechanism is responsible for each symptom, and what would you put in front of clients instead?
    Check
    • DNS answers are cached for the record's TTL (a 300 s TTL here), so a moved address reaches clients only when the cache expires
    • DNS never notices a crash: it says what could be at an address, not what is up
    • one DNS name → a load balancer that health-checks servers; inside the data centre, a registry with ephemeral nodes and watches

    Missed it? Reopen the path of a request, section 1, and service discovery, "Why it beats the alternatives".

  4. With a path that holds 16 packets and a drop whenever the window exceeds 16, AIMD settles into a cycle. Give the cycle, its average as a share of the path, and why the decrease is a halving rather than "subtract one".
    Check
    • drops at RTT 16, 26, 36: the window goes 17 → 8, then 8, 9, …, 17 again (10 RTTs per cycle)
    • mean (8 + 17) / 2 = 12.5 = 78% of 16
    • halving cuts the bigger flow by more, so two flows converge to fair shares (5.98 vs 6.02); subtracting one keeps them 3 vs 14 forever

    Missed it? Reopen delay, timeouts and congestion, section 4 and the AIAD cell.

  5. which concept?A resize server stops for a 25-second garbage-collection pause and disappears from the clients' server list, then reappears. The same week, a three-node registry stops accepting changes when one member crashes while another is paused. Which two mechanisms are at work, and what would you change?
    Check
    • ephemeral nodes tied to a session: 20 s of silence ends the session, the node is deleted and watchers are told; a paused process looks exactly like a dead one, and on waking it must re-register
    • majority quorum: 3 nodes need 2; crashed + paused leaves 1, so writes stop
    • changes: a 5-node registry (needs 3, survives 2); a longer session timeout if pauses are expected, at the cost of slower detection of real crashes

    Missed it? Reopen service discovery, the worked example and the quorum table.

  6. 24 resize jobs sit in 6 partitions read by 4 consumers, and job 4 takes 10 ticks instead of 1. Why do partitions finish at tick 13 while a shared queue with competing consumers finishes at tick 11?
    Check
    • head-of-line blocking: jobs 10, 16 and 22 sit behind job 4 in partition 4 (finishing at 11, 12, 13)
    • whole-partition assignment splits 6 over 4 as 2, 2, 1, 1, so two consumers idle while two still have work
    • competing consumers never idle while a job waits (a greedy schedule), so only job 4's own consumer is slow

    Missed it? Reopen competing consumers vs partitions, the worked example.

  7. One HTTPS fetch took 286.6 ms on a fresh connection and 83.6 ms on a reused one. Where did the difference go?
    Check
    • setup: DNS 3.2 ms (already cached), TCP 39.3 ms and TLS 41.4 ms, about one round trip each
    • transfer: 160.4 ms fresh vs 41.7 ms reused, because a new connection starts at a 10-segment window that doubles each round trip (4 extra round trips for 275 KB)
    • a reused connection skips both handshakes and already has a grown window

    Missed it? Reopen the path of a request, "Measured: one HTTPS request, to scale".

3 · Project: one bad minute for a resize client

What it reinforces: the six silent cases (unreliable network), choosing a timeout (delay and timeouts), crash signals and end-to-end checks (end-to-end), picking a live server (path of a request), session expiry and false deaths (service discovery), and why a queue changes the failure (consumers vs partitions).

Simulate one minute of the image service's clients in about 60 lines of Python, on a 1-second clock like simulate() in code/service-discovery.py. There are 40 servers at 250 requests/s each (10,000/s in total), and the registry's session timeout is 20 s. As on the service-discovery card, srv-2 sends its last heartbeat at t = 5 and crashes, and srv-3 is paused from t = 30 to t = 54 (last heartbeat at 29).

  1. Timeout. Set the client's timeout with 2d + r, taking d = 20 ms and r = 500 ms from the delay card. Is a timed-out request already over the 500 ms p99 budget before its retry starts?
  2. Misdirected requests. Clients send to every server on their current list. Count the requests sent to srv-2 while it is dead but still listed, and to srv-3 while it is paused but still listed. What share of the minute's 600,000 requests is that, and does the minute keep its p99?
  3. Crash signal. srv-2's process died but its machine didn't, so its OS refuses connections at once. Redo the count if clients treat a refused connection as an instant signal to pick another server. Does the minute keep its p99 now?
  4. The pause wakes up. srv-3 wakes at t = 55 holding the requests that were queued during its pause. Their callers timed out and retried elsewhere. What goes wrong if srv-3 processes them, and name two fixes: one at srv-3, one at the end that owns the effect (the usage records).
  5. End-to-end. Each request uploads a 256 KiB image across 10 hops. At 10−9 undetected damage per byte per hop, what share of uploads fail the end-to-end digest and need a resend?
  6. Queue instead. In one paragraph: if resize requests went through a competing-consumers queue instead of straight to servers, what would the crash and the pause each cost?
Done when: your program prints the per-server request counts for the minute, the misdirected share with and without the crash signal, and the number of duplicate resizes srv-3 would do on waking; and you can say, in one sentence each, which fix removed which failure. Then compare with the check.
Check: the numbers (arithmetic on the cards' inputs; the timeline is the service-discovery card's)
  • Timeout: 2 × 20 + 500 = 540 ms. Yes: a request that times out has already spent 540 ms, more than the whole 500 ms budget, so every misdirected request is a p99 miss.
  • Misdirected: srv-2 is dead but listed from 5 to 25: 20 × 250 = 5,000. srv-3 is paused and listed from 30 until its expiry at 49: 19 × 250 = 4,750. Total 9,750 of 600,000 = 1.625%, over 1%: this minute's p99 is blown.
  • With the crash signal, srv-2's 5,000 are refused at once and retried in milliseconds; only the pause's 4,750 = 0.79% still wait out a timeout. The p99 holds, just. A crash signal can't help a pause.
  • On waking, srv-3 would resize up to 4,750 images whose callers already retried elsewhere: up to 4,750 duplicate resizes, each billed twice. Fixes: srv-3 drops requests older than the caller's 540 ms deadline; the usage store dedupes by a client-chosen request id (the end-to-end argument: dedup where the effect lives).
  • λ = 262,144 × 10 × 10−9 = 0.00262, so eλ − 1 = 0.26% of uploads need a resend.
  • Queue: a dead or paused consumer stops taking jobs, so the work flows to the other consumers and nothing waits on the registry's timeout. Only the jobs it already held are delayed until the broker redelivers them after its own ack timeout. That redelivery is at-least-once, so the same dedup is still needed.
