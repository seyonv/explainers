Choosing where to coordinate

The overview for course 5 of the CS core reference series. It ties together the 20 cards on the source guide's distributed systems, networks, internet and consistency pages, all built around its image-resize service (10K requests/s per region, p99 < 500 ms, 99.9% available): what fails, what a replica may decide on its own, and what it costs when replicas have to agree.

In one breath

Machines fail in parts, the network answers some requests with silence, and replicas go down together far more often than the pN arithmetic says. So replicate for availability, and let each replica act alone wherever merging two replicas' work can't break a rule. Coordinate (one owner, a majority quorum, Raft) only where a merge can break one, because every coordinated write waits at least one round trip and has to refuse during a partition. Underneath all of it: a timeout is a guess, so a retry must be safe to repeat. Judge a service by its p99 and its yield, not its mean and its uptime.

The words people use

Linearizable vs eventually consistent

Linearizable (CAP's C): every operation looks as if it happened at one instant on one copy. Eventually consistent: replicas may disagree for a while and agree once writes stop. The first costs a round trip per write; the second costs broken rules if a merge can break them.

Partial failure, partition, timeout

Partial failure: some parts fail while the rest runs, and the survivors can't tell which. A partition is the network case. A timeout is the only detector, and it is a guess: a 25 s GC pause looks exactly like a crash.

At-least-once, exactly-once

Delivery over a lossy network is at-least-once: a retry may bring a second copy. The effect can still be exactly-once if every apply is idempotent, keyed by a request id the client chose once.

✓ Coordination-free

A write that commits using only its own replica's state. It's safe exactly when the rule is I-confluent, meaning any two valid replicas merge into a valid state. Deposits and foreign-key inserts pass. Withdrawals, chosen usernames and deletes fail, and only those need to pay the round trip.

When this advice doesn't apply

Both formulas are simple models. The p and c on these cards are illustrative, and c can't be derived; it comes from how the system is run, which is why the source says to ask vendors for real MTTF and MTTR distributions. The round-trip floor only bites across distance: inside one region zones are under 10 ms apart, so coordinating every write there often costs little, and the price shows up across regions and during partitions. Everything here assumes crash faults; a server that lies (a Byzantine fault) needs a different family of protocols.

If the work fits on one machine, most of this course doesn't apply. The image service needs it only because 1K cores won't fit in one box. Two accounts in one database want a local ACID transaction, not a log and dedup. A small, stable fleet is fine with DNS or a load balancer instead of ZooKeeper, and with plain hash mod N instead of a ring. And when you do need consensus, use etcd, Consul or ZooKeeper rather than your own Raft.
