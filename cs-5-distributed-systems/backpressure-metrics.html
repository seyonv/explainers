Backpressure, percentiles, yield and harvest

Also called load shedding or flow control; tail latency and p99; availability counted in requests; completeness of an answer. The source guide packs all four into the second half of Distributed Systems › Engineering (Figure 4 and the three-shard search example); here they're applied to its own image-resize service: 10 conversions/s per core, p99 < 500 ms, 99.9% availability.

In one breath

When a server can't keep up, it should say so quickly (backpressure) instead of letting a queue grow until every answer is late. Judge it by the slow end of its latencies (p99), not the average, and by the share of requests it answered (yield), not the share of time it was up. When part of the data is missing, choose on purpose between refusing (lower yield) and answering with less (lower harvest).

1 · Backpressure: 12 requests/s into a core that does 10

One core converts 10 images/s (the source's number). Offer it 12/s, a 20% surge (illustrative). Without a limit, the extra 2/s pile up. With a cap of 4 waiting requests (0.4 s of work), the extra 2/s get an immediate error and a hint to back off.

One-second tick model (queue() in the code). The unbounded core stays 100% busy, yet after 2 s every image it finishes is late. With the cap, the 2/s that can't be served learn it at once and can retry with exponential backoff.

2 · Percentiles: the mean looks fine, the tail doesn't

10,000 illustrative latencies, drawn from a lognormal (median 50 ms, σ = 1.0, seed 7). A long right tail is typical of real services; the exact shape here is made up.

Nearest-rank percentiles of the sample. 90 of the 10,000 (0.90%) took over 500 ms, so this server just meets its p99. The mean hides that: it is pulled up by the tail but lands nowhere near it.

Fan-out makes the tail everyone's problem. A search or page that waits for n servers in parallel is as slow as the slowest one:

P(request slow)=1− (1−p)nall n calls fast
pchance one server call is slow1% · 0.90% in the sample nservers the request waits for1 · 10 · 100
p = 1 %: n = 1 → 1.0 % n = 10 → 1 − 0.99^10 = 9.6 % n = 100 → 1 − 0.99^100 = 63.4 % sample: n = 100 → 1 − (1 − 0.0090)^100 = 59.5 % (simulated: 58.7 %) median of the max of 100 = p(100 × 0.5^(1/100)) = p99.31 = 542 ms

The 63% case is Dean and Barroso's example in The Tail at Scale: 1 in 100 calls slow, 100 servers per request. With fan-out 100, one server's p99 becomes the whole system's median.

3 · Yield vs uptime, and harvest

A 60-second outage costs the same uptime whenever it happens. Yield counts the requests lost, so it depends on the traffic at that moment. Illustrative day for the image service: 10,000/s at peak, 1,000/s at the trough, 5,500/s on average.

uptime = 1 − 60 / 86,400 = 99.931 % (either time) requests per day = 5,500 × 86,400 = 475,200,000 yield, peak = 1 − 60 × 10,000 / 475,200,000 = 99.874 % yield, trough = 1 − 60 × 1,000 / 475,200,000 = 99.987 %

The source's example. Every system that spans machines faces this choice when a partition hits: stop answering (lower yield) or give incomplete answers (lower harvest).

The code

def queue(arrive, serve, seconds, cap=None):
    backlog, rejected, rows = 0, 0, []
    for t in range(1, seconds + 1):
        room = arrive if cap is None else serve + cap - backlog
        taken = min(arrive, room)
        rejected += arrive - taken     # fast "503, back off"
        backlog = max(0, backlog + taken - serve)
        rows.append((t, backlog, backlog / serve, rejected))
    return rows

def pct(xs, p):                    # nearest rank, xs sorted
    return xs[max(0, math.ceil(p / 100 * len(xs)) - 1)]

def p_slow(fanout, p=0.01):        # at least one call slow
    return 1 - (1 - p) ** fanout

What to do when load exceeds capacity

✗ Queue everything

Nothing is lost, but the backlog grows 2/s: 0.6 s of waiting at 3 s, 12 s at 60 s. The core stays busy producing images nobody waits for.

✗ Time out and retry at once

Timeouts are needed, but an instant retry resends the same work. If every one of the 12/s times out and retries once, the core is offered 24/s. That is the cascade the source warns about.

✗ Just add servers

Helps, but late. Add a second core at 60 s (20/s against 12/s) and the 120-request backlog drains at 8/s, so latency stays over budget for another 15 s.

✓ Backpressure

Cap the queue at 4 and reject the rest with a fast error; callers back off exponentially. 10 of 12 per s (83.3%) finish within 0.5 s, and the refused 2/s learn it right away.

Always percentiles, always backpressure?

Not always. The mean is still the right number for capacity: total CPU time is mean × count, and the 1K-core sizing in this service is a mean-based estimate. And a p99 needs data: from 100 samples it is just the second-slowest request, while 10,000 samples put 100 requests above it.

A bounded queue with rejections suits interactive traffic. For batch work where throughput matters and latency doesn't, such as this service's usage logs shipped in batches to MapReduce, a large durable queue that absorbs bursts is the better fit (our reading; the source doesn't say). And lower harvest is the wrong choice when a partial answer is a wrong answer. A bill summed over 2 of 3 shards is wrong, so refuse and retry later (lower yield) instead.
