Checkpoint 1: failure, capacity and the C-or-A choice

Ties together why independent-failure math lies, sizing a service, partial failure and CAP, choosing consistency per subsystem and backpressure, percentiles, yield and harvest: what fails in the image-resize service, how much of it to buy, and what each part does when something breaks.

How to use this page. Close the five cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure; it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you have to work out which idea applies before you can answer.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?The resize fleet runs 40 servers spread over three racks. A pushed memory limit is set too low, and all 40 run out of memory within a minute of each other. Which idea explains why 40 servers didn't help, and what would have limited the damage?
    Check
    • correlated failure: one config change reaches every replica, so it's a shared event c that no replica count shrinks
    • P(all down) ≥ c whatever N is; spreading over racks attacks shared power, not a shared config
    • fix: stage the push, a canary to 1 of 40 first, so a bad change risks 1/40 = 2.5% of the fleet instead of 100%

    Missed it? Reopen correlated failure, "The four shared causes" and the ✓ cell.

  2. The fleet covers the average load exactly with 32 machines. Why does that fleet still miss the p99 < 500 ms target, and what does keeping 20% idle change?
    Check
    • 32 machines run at 97.7% busy: 84.9% of arrivals find every core busy
    • spare capacity per machine is only 320 − 312.5 = 7.5 requests/s, so the M/M/32 p99 queue wait is 592 ms, over budget before any resize
    • ÷ 0.8 → 40 machines at 78.1%: spare capacity 70/s, p99 wait 36 ms

    Missed it? Reopen sizing, "Why the headroom is not optional".

  3. which concept?A customer upgrades from free to pro. During a network split, another replica keeps telling the billing job the customer is on free, and the customer is billed at the wrong rate. Which property did that replica give up, and should this subsystem have made that choice?
    Check
    • it chose availability: it answered from its own copy, so the read wasn't linearizable (the write finished before the read began, yet the read missed it)
    • the plan gates billing, so a stale answer costs more than no answer
    • per-subsystem rule: favour consistency for plan changes (refuse, or read from the side that took the write); keep availability for data like the edge identity cache

    Missed it? Reopen partial failure and CAP, the two traces, and consistency per subsystem, "Is availability at the edge always safe?".

  4. The whole service promises 99.9%, yet the source sets the API tier at 99.99%. Why more nines for one part than for the whole, and how many minutes a month does that leave for everything behind it?
    Check
    • availabilities of parts in series multiply; the API tier is the front door, so its downtime is everyone's
    • 99.99% allows 4.32 min a month; the whole allows 43.2
    • 0.999 / 0.9999 = 99.91% for the rest: about 38.9 min a month

    Missed it? Reopen consistency per subsystem, decision 1 and the formula block.

  5. which concept?A dashboard shows mean latency 81 ms against a 500 ms target, so the team calls it healthy. The page that users complain about waits for 100 backend calls in parallel. Which idea explains the complaints, and what share of page loads are slow if each call is slow 1% of the time?
    Check
    • percentiles and fan-out: the mean hides the tail (p99 475 ms in the same sample)
    • a fan-out request is as slow as its slowest call: 1 − 0.99100 = 63.4% of page loads are slow
    • so one server's p99 has become the page's typical latency; measure p99, and cut or hedge the fan-out

    Missed it? Reopen backpressure and percentiles, section 2.

  6. Twelve requests a second arrive at a core that converts ten. Compare the unbounded queue with a cap of 4 waiting after 60 seconds: backlog, wait, and what the rejected requests do.
    Check
    • unbounded: backlog grows 2/s → 120 after 60 s, a 12.0 s wait; every image after t = 2 s finishes late
    • capped: backlog stays 4, wait 0.4 s, latency ≤ 0.5 s; 116 rejected by t = 60 (2 of every 12)
    • the rejected callers learn at once and back off exponentially instead of waiting for work nobody will use

    Missed it? Reopen backpressure, section 1.

  7. The queue's leader is cut off from the other two nodes, and a new job arrives at the old leader. Walk through what happens to that job, and say why that is the right behaviour for this subsystem.
    Check
    • the old leader reaches only itself (1 of 3), so it refuses the job
    • the other two, a majority, elect a new leader; the API retries the job there and it's stored after the earlier jobs
    • right for a FIFO job queue: refusing for about 2 s beats two leaders that duplicate, lose or reorder jobs

    Missed it? Reopen consistency per subsystem, the queue hand trace.

3 · Project: a design review for a surge day

What it reinforces: the sizing chain and the queueing tail (sizing), the front door's server count (consistency per subsystem), the shared-cause floor (correlated failure), per-subsystem C or A (CAP) and backpressure and yield (backpressure).

A marketing campaign will push the image service from 10,000 to 12,000 requests/s for a day (a 20% surge), and the platform team wants to roll out a new resize library the same morning. Write a one-page review with a number behind every recommendation. Use size() and p99_wait() from code/image-resize-sizing.py, and k_of_n() from code/image-resize-architecture.py.

  1. Workers. At 12,000 requests/s on today's 40 machines, what is the utilisation and the model's p99 queue wait? How many machines bring utilisation back under 80%, and what is the wait then?
  2. Front door. The API tier runs 3 servers at 5,000 requests/s each. Is it still true that losing one leaves enough? How many servers does the surge need? With the card's illustrative 99.5% per server, does "at least 3 of 4 up" still meet the 99.99% target, and what does a fifth server buy?
  3. The rollout. Using c + (1 − c)pN with p = 0.01 and N = 3, compare pushing the library to every server at once (c = 0.01) with a canary to 1 of 40 that stops a bad build there (c = 0.01 × 1/40). What share of the fleet does each put at risk?
  4. Past capacity. The whole fleet converts 40 × 32 × 10 images/s. If the surge overshoots to 14,000/s, what share do the per-core caps of 4 reject, and what latency do the answered requests get?
  5. C or A under stress. If the auth data plane fails during the surge, which subsystems keep working and which refuse? Say which one you'd let refuse and why.
  6. Yield. Against the backpressure card's illustrative day of 475,200,000 requests, what yield does a 60-second outage at 12,000/s leave, and does it meet 99.9%?
Done when: each of the six answers has its number and a one-line recommendation, the rollout plan names the canary, and you can say which single change buys the most protection for the day. Then compare with the check.
Check: the numbers (computed with the course's code files and plain arithmetic)
  • Workers: 12,000 × 0.1 / 1,280 = 93.75% busy; P(wait) 63.0%, p99 queue wait ≈ 207 ms, 41% of the budget before any resizing. 12,000 × 0.1 / (32 × 0.8) = 46.875 → 47 machines: 79.8%, p99 wait ≈ 42.5 ms (48 machines: 78.1%, 36 ms).
  • Front door: with 3 servers, losing one leaves 2 × 5,000 = 10,000 < 12,000. The surge needs ⌈12,000 / 5,000⌉ + 1 = 4. But k_of_n(0.995, 3, 4) = 99.985%, below 99.99% (today's 2 of 3 gives 99.9925%): needing 3 servers up out of 4 is harder than 2 of 3. Five servers, 3 of 5 up: 99.9999%. Independent failures assumed, as on the card.
  • Rollout: at once, P(all down) = 0.01000099 (2 nines) and 100% of the fleet at risk; canary, c = 0.00025 → 0.000251 (3.60 nines) and 2.5% at risk. Adding replicas changes neither floor.
  • Past capacity: 40 × 32 × 10 = 12,800/s; at 14,000/s the caps reject 1,200/s = 8.6%, so 91.4% are answered, each within 0.4 s of queueing + 0.1 s of work = 0.5 s.
  • C or A: the edge identity cache keeps authenticating (A; new users wait), the resize path keeps running, and usage keeps aggregating locally. Only account creation (the management plane) refuses, which is the right one to lose.
  • Yield: 1 − 60 × 12,000 / 475,200,000 = 99.848%, which misses 99.9% (a 60 s outage at 10,000/s gives 99.874%, also a miss). The single biggest win for the day is the canary: it moves the floor, while machines only move the tail.
