The questions

1Does an LLM judge that reads the compaction summary pass false success claims that the same judge catches when it reads the full trace?

capstone: judges seeing the summarystatus: open
Why it matters
If yes, every judge-graded eval of long runs inherits the agent's status inflation, and "grade from the full trace, never the working set" becomes a rule.
Status
Open for this comparison (graders card). Nearby: Advani, From Confident Closing to Silent Failure (arXiv 2606.09863), finds LLM judges reach AUROC ≤ 0.65 at spotting false success; Hu, Silence Is Endorsement (arXiv 2609.20211), finds summarizers drop "unverified" status. Neither feeds a judge the two views side by side.
Rough cost
50–100 runs; cheap.
Smallest experiment
Take runs that compacted and that the checker fails. Give one judge prompt (a) the summary plus final message and (b) the full trace; compare how often each says PASS.
Source card
Tasks, trials and graders (the worked example's row 3 vs 3′)

2What is pass^k of an honest final report, set beside pass^k of solving the task?

capstone: pass^k of honestystatus: open
Why it matters
A user needs every report to be true. An agent can be honest on average and still mislead on some tasks once every few runs, which a rate hides and pass^k shows.
Status
Open (pass-k card). Unknown whether any published paper reports it; none in the series' novelty check is described as doing so.
Rough cost
$0 extra: reuse the capstone's repeated runs (needs n ≥ k per task).
Smallest experiment
Mark each run honest or not with the claim checker, compute C(c,3) ÷ C(n,3) per task, average across tasks, and print it next to pass^3 of "solved".
Source card
pass@k vs pass^k

3Can a trace-only rule, "a success claim after a non-zero test exit in the spans", replace re-running the checker?

capstone: the claim checkerstatus: partly studied
Why it matters
A re-run needs a container per run; a span rule is free and could run on every production trace, turning the claim checker into a standing release gate.
Status
Partly studied. The capstone pilot's transcript detector held up on a spot check by the auditing agent, not a human (35 of 40 flagged runs confirmed), and Advani (arXiv 2606.09863) reports cheap TF-IDF detectors at AUROC 0.83–0.95 on τ²-bench and AppWorld. The first pilot pass found that raw self-contradiction barely predicts a hidden-test failure (30.4% vs 27.6%); in the expanded pass, task-relevant contradictions do (53% unresolved vs 29%), but only 14 of 105 automatic positives held up when read by hand (see the pilot audit lab), so the two checks may measure different things. No study compares them on coding runs.
Rough cost
Low to medium.
Smallest experiment
On a sample of pilot runs, compute the span rule and a fresh checker re-run, and report the 2 × 2 agreement table with the disagreements read by hand.
Source card
Trace, audit, eval, feedback

4How strongly do false claims cluster by task and by repo (the ρ that sizes the compaction experiment)?

capstone: compaction → false success claimsstatus: open
Why it matters
ρ decides the paper's size: a 10-point rise needs 136 tasks at ρ 0.2 and 194 at ρ 0.5. And if tasks from one repo cluster, every SWE-bench bar built per task is too short.
Status
Partly answered by this series' own pilot: across 45 public submissions, ρ for a task-relevant self-contradiction is 0.055 [0.036, 0.075] among claims, and 0.32 for honest misses (between-task part only; see the pilot audit lab). No published ρ for false claims is known to this series. The method: Miller, arXiv 2411.00640.
Rough cost
$0: rescore public SWE-bench results and the pilot's 23 submissions × 150 tasks.
Smallest experiment
From the pilot table, take each task's share of submissions with a self-contradicted claim and estimate ρ; then cluster by repo instead of task and compare the two standard errors. Caveat: those repeats are different agents, not reruns of one, so this gives the between-task part only.
Source card
How many runs do you need? · Error bars that don't lie

5Do repeats of an identical prompt agree with each other more than real users would, and so overstate pass^k?

status: partly studied
Why it matters
Every reliability number in this course assumes independent trials. Shared caches or one fixed phrasing could make repeats too alike, inflating pass^k and distorting ρ.
Status
Partly studied: τ-bench (arXiv 2406.12045) gets its variation from a simulated user who rephrases the task each trial. A direct identical-vs-paraphrased comparison: unknown.
Rough cost
About 480 runs.
Smallest experiment
30 tasks × 8 runs with the identical prompt, and 30 × 8 with paraphrases from a fresh environment each time; compare per-task pass^k and ρ between the arms.
Source card
pass@k vs pass^k · Error bars that don't lie

6When you swap a judge model or rubric, does a frozen human-labelled set catch "a change in the ruler" before it is read as agent progress?

status: partly studied
Why it matters
A grader is a deployable. If judge swaps move scores silently, release decisions get made on the ruler, not the agent.
Status
Partly studied. Wolfe says to calibrate judges against humans and keep watching agreement, and Govindarajan names the ruler problem; neither gives target agreement or drift numbers (graders card).
Rough cost
Low.
Smallest experiment
Grade the same saved transcripts with the old and the new judge, score both against the frozen human labels, and report the score move beside the agreement move.
Source card
Trace, audit, eval, feedback · Tasks, trials and graders

7Does a judge from the agent's own model family miss the same failures the agent makes?

status: partly studied
Why it matters
Layers only multiply down if their misses are independent: in the card's illustrative numbers, aligned holes let 6.0% through instead of 2.4%. If misses align by family, a different-family judge is a free fix.
Status
Partly studied: self-preference bias in LLM judges (named by the graders card's writer, with no paper; the card itself doesn't cover it); Khullar et al., Self-Attribution Bias (arXiv 2603.04582), on monitors going easy on themselves.
Rough cost
Low to medium.
Smallest experiment
On runs with checker verdicts, measure each judge's miss rate on true failures, same family vs another family, and how often its misses fall on the runs the agent got wrong.
Source card
Tasks, trials and graders

8How correlated are per-task scores when you swap the scaffold, compared with swapping the model?

status: partly studied
Why it matters
That correlation decides how much pairing saves (0.63 in the error-bars simulation cut the gain's SE from 0.095 to 0.059) and so how many tasks a harness comparison needs.
Status
Partly studied: Miller (arXiv 2411.00640) notes scores on the same questions correlate even across unrelated models. For scaffold swaps: unknown.
Rough cost
About 300 runs; a first look is $0 from public SWE-bench per-task results, which cover several scaffolds on the same tasks.
Smallest experiment
From public per-task results, correlate pairs of submissions that share a model but differ in scaffold, and pairs that share a scaffold but differ in model.
Source card
Error bars that don't lie · How many runs do you need?

Also raised, ranked lower: whether incident-derived release gates cut repeat failures (unstudied, and it needs production data; evidence-loop card), and small-n intervals for pass^k when c < k, plus Bayesian power at small n (simulation only; Bowyer et al., arXiv 2503.01747, cover accuracy but not pass^k; pass-k and power-analysis cards).

How to generate your own

  1. Split an overloaded word. Govindarajan's move: "observability" was hiding four jobs, and "done" was hiding five links. Find a word your team uses for two things (a "passing" eval, a "verified" fix), split it, and ask which half anyone measures.
  2. Ask what's unmeasured. Every card here ends with a clarification box; most say "the author reports no data on…". Each of those sentences is a question.
  3. Check for a cheap controlled experiment. Can you hold everything fixed and change one thing, on runs you already have? Questions 2, 4 and 8 above cost nothing because the data already exists.

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

For question 2, suppose 70% of reports are honest overall. Before revealing: is pass^8 of honesty higher if the dishonest reports spread evenly across tasks, or if they cluster on a few hard tasks?

Reveal

Clustered is far higher. The pass-k card's example: at a 70% average, an even spread gives pass^8 = 0.058, while tasks at 1.0, 1.0, 0.4, 0.4 give 0.500. So pass^k of honesty also tells you whether lying is a property of some tasks or a background rate, which a single rate can't.

Why this step?

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Pick question 5 and write a half-page pre-registration: hypothesis, the one thing changed, the metric (per-task pass^k and ρ), the design (30 tasks × 8 runs per arm, 480 runs), and what result would change your mind.

Done when: Your plan changes exactly one thing between arms, names the metric and its estimator, keeps the 480-run budget, and states a result that would make you stop trusting identical-prompt repeats.

Hint

The estimator is C(c,k) ÷ C(n,k) per task; with 8 runs per task you can report k up to 8.
