Finding the learning rate, then decaying it

Also called: a learning-rate sweep, LR range test; step decay (a learning-rate schedule). Lecture 3 · finding a good initial learning rate · 0:45:40

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Every update is p.data += -lr * p.grad, and the whole run depends on that one number. Guess too small and 1000 steps barely move the loss; guess too big and it bounces or explodes. Guessing costs a full run per try, and a fixed lr also leaves the model jittering above the bottom at the end.

Where it fits: It builds on the minibatch loop from the previous card: the same noisy 32-row steps, now with the step size chosen on purpose. The noise that made minibatches fast is also what the decay at the end is there to calm down.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why does the sweep space the exponent evenly rather than lr itself, and what fraction of steps would test lr below 0.1 otherwise?
  2. The sweep is one continuous training run. Which part of its U-shaped curve is caused by lr, and which part by the net simply not having trained yet?
  3. Why pick 0.1 when the lowest point of the smoothed curve is at 0.157?
  4. Why does dropping lr 10× buy more in 10,000 steps than keeping 0.1 for the same 10,000 steps?
  5. If the loss is falling at lr 0.1, why not decay much earlier to settle sooner?

In one breath: the learning rate (lr) is how far each training step moves the parameters along the gradient. Too small and the loss barely falls; too big and it bounces or blows up. Rather than guess, run one short training in which lr climbs from 0.001 to 1 on a log scale, watch where the loss falls fastest, train there, and near the end shrink lr 10× so the noisy minibatch steps can settle.

Predict first: the sweep tries 1000 values of lr between 0.001 and 1, one per step. If you spaced them evenly (torch.linspace(0.001, 1, 1000)), how many of the 1000 steps would test an lr below 0.1?

Only 99. Even spacing spends 90% of the run on lr between 0.1 and 1, the region where training is already unstable. Spacing the exponent evenly (10**torch.linspace(-3, 0, 1000)) gives each factor of 10 the same 333 steps, so 666 steps test lr below 0.1 (both counted with a script).

Why the smaller lr helps late but not early: each minibatch gradient is a noisy estimate from 32 rows, so every step carries some random error, and lr scales that error. Early on, the real downhill direction dominates and big steps win. Near the bottom, the noise dominates and the parameters jitter around the low point by an amount that grows with lr. Cutting lr 10× shrinks the jitter so they settle lower: the decay block drops 0.0774, more than four times the 0.0169 that the same 10,000 steps at 0.1 would have bought.

▶ Build itruns on your M3 · about 7 s

When: pause the video at 0:45:40 and write the sweep before Karpathy does. Start from the minibatch loop, with X, Y built from all the words and C, W1, b1, W2, b2 made from g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(2147483647) in that order, plus import matplotlib.pyplot as plt.

lre = torch.linspace(-3, 0, 1000)
lrs = 10**lre                     # 0.001 ... 1, log-spaced
lossi = []
for i in range(1000):
    ix = torch.randint(0, X.shape[0], (32,), generator=g)
    h = torch.tanh(C[X[ix]].view(-1, 6) @ W1 + b1)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(h @ W2 + b2, Y[ix])
    for p in parameters: p.grad = None
    loss.backward()
    for p in parameters: p.data += -lrs[i] * p.grad
    lossi.append(loss.item())
plt.plot(lre, lossi)              # x = exponent, not lr
for k in range(10):
    print(f'{lre[100*k]:+.2f} {sum(lossi[100*k:100*k+100])/100:.3f}')

Then re-create the parameters from a fresh seed-2147483647 generator, run the same loop for 40,000 steps with the update changed as below, and compute F.cross_entropy on all of X, Y at the end:

    lr = 0.1 if i < 30000 else 0.01   # step decay
    for p in parameters: p.data += -lr * p.grad

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Guess one lr

lr 0.001 leaves the loss at 10.74 after 1000 steps; lr 10 sends it to 70.31. You find out only after paying for a run, and one run per guess adds up.

✗ Sweep lr evenly

A linear sweep from 0.001 to 1 spends 901 of its 1000 steps above 0.1, so the useful range from 0.001 to 0.1 gets a sliver of the curve.

✗ Keep lr fixed forever

At 0.1 the minibatch noise keeps the parameters jittering: 40,000 steps end at 2.4006, while the same steps with a decay at 30,000 reach 2.3401.

✓ Log sweep, then step decay

One 1000-step run covers three factors of ten evenly and shows the valley. Big steps then do the early work, and the 10× drop lets the model settle into the low point.

Is the valley "the" learning rate?

No, it's a good starting point. The sweep is one continuous training run, so its left side is high for two reasons at once: lr is small and the net hasn't learned anything yet (its first minibatch loss is 21.81 here). That pushes the dip a little to the right, which is one reason to pick a value on the low side of the valley, as Karpathy's 0.1 does. The best lr also moves when you change the batch size, the model size or the initialisation, so rerun the sweep when those change.

The step decay here is the simplest schedule. Later in the series, GPT-2 is trained with the AdamW optimizer, a warmup that raises lr over the first steps and a cosine curve that lowers it smoothly (optimizer recipe). The idea is the same: large steps first, small steps to settle.

Understand and remember

  • each update moves parameters by lr × gradient → too small barely moves the loss (0.001: 10.74 after 1000 steps), too big overshoots the low point (1.0: 4.64) or diverges (10: 70.31) → lr has to be found, not guessed
  • lr's effect spans factors of ten → sweep the exponent evenly (10**linspace(-3, 0, 1000)) → each decade gets 333 steps → one 1000-step run traces loss vs lr and the valley (about 10−1) shows where learning is fastest and still stable
  • minibatch gradient = true direction + noise, and lr scales both → early the direction dominates, so big steps win; late the noise dominates and parameters jitter around the bottom → cut lr 10× → jitter shrinks → loss settles lower (2.4175 → 2.3401, vs 2.4006 without the decay)
  • the sweep trains while it measures → early high losses mix "lr too small" with "net still untrained" → the dip is shifted right and flat → choose a value on the low side of the valley and rerun the sweep when batch size, model or init change
  • detail that matters plot loss against lre (the exponent), not lrs → on a linear lr axis the whole useful range 0.001–0.1 is squashed into the left 10% → the valley is unreadable

Reference only

  • lre = torch.linspace(-3, 0, 1000); lrs = 10**lre and the lab's 10 bins of 100 steps
  • the exact bin losses (18.166 … 3.221 … 5.925) and the smoothed minimum at step 731, lr 0.157
  • Karpathy's final schedule: lr = 0.1 if i < 100000 else 0.01 over 200,000 steps
  • the name "LR range test" for this kind of sweep

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Minibatches: noisy but fast: Minibatches made each step noisy on purpose to get more steps per second. How does that choice create the need for a learning-rate decay, and how would a full-batch loop (all 228,146 rows per step) change the picture?

Check
  • minibatch gradient = true gradient + sampling noise; lr scales the noise into parameter jitter
  • decay trades speed for less jitter once the true gradient is small
  • full-batch gradients have no sampling noise, so a fixed lr can settle on its own, but each step costs about 1000× more time (126 ms vs 0.119 ms measured)

vs The GPT-3 recipe: AdamW, clipping, warmup and cosine decay: This card uses plain SGD with a one-time 10× drop. GPT-2's recipe uses AdamW with warmup and a cosine decay. What problem does each part of that recipe solve that the step decay doesn't, and what idea do both share?

Check
  • shared idea: big steps early, small steps to settle at the end
  • warmup starts lr small for the first steps, when a freshly initialised big model is fragile
  • cosine lowers lr smoothly instead of one sudden jump
  • AdamW scales each parameter's step by its own gradient history, so one global lr matters less
