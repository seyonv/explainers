Checkpoint 2: shortest paths and spanning trees

Ties together BFS in layers, Dijkstra's algorithm, A* search, Bellman–Ford and Floyd–Warshall and minimum spanning trees: one relax step applied in different orders, and the one greedy tree that isn't a route at all.

How to use this page. Close the five cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure; it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you have to work out which idea applies before you can answer.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?A travel site wants the cheapest fare with at most 2 layovers. A teammate proposes plain Dijkstra on cities. What goes wrong, and what do you run instead?
    Check
    • Dijkstra keeps one cost per city, so it can't keep a pricier route that uses fewer flights; it returns the cheapest route regardless of stops
    • Bellman–Ford for k + 1 = 3 rounds, each reading only last round's costs (the snapshot)
    • or Dijkstra on (city, flights used) states

    Missed it? Reopen Bellman–Ford, worked example 1 and "Why Bellman–Ford beats the alternatives".

  2. On the course's graph, Dijkstra from A pops (4, B) at pop 4 and skips it. Where did that entry come from, and why is skipping it safe?
    Check
    • pop 1 relaxed A→B to 4 and pushed (4, B)
    • pop 2 found A→C→B = 3, pushed (3, B); heapq can't lower the old key
    • B was finalised at 3 on pop 3, and with weights ≥ 0 nothing can beat a popped distance

    Missed it? Reopen Dijkstra, the hand trace and "Stale entry".

  3. which concept?Six offices must be joined by fibre at the lowest total cable length, and a colleague runs Dijkstra from the head office and keeps its tree. Is that optimal? What should you run?
    Check
    • no: a shortest-path tree minimises each distance from one source, not the total
    • on the card's 6-vertex graph, Dijkstra's tree from A weighs 19 (it uses C–F 9); the MST weighs 13
    • run Kruskal or Prim: the cut property makes the greedy choice safe

    Missed it? Reopen minimum spanning trees, "Why it beats the alternatives".

  4. Take A→B 2, A→C 3, C→B −2, B→D 1. Trace Dijkstra from A to D. Where does it go wrong, and what does Bellman–Ford do differently?
    Check
    • B pops first at 2 and is finalised; D is set to 3
    • C pops at 3 and finds B = 1, but B is already done, so the (1, B) entry is skipped and D stays 3
    • Bellman–Ford never finalises: it retries every edge each round, so D becomes 2

    Missed it? Reopen Dijkstra, "When does it give the wrong answer?".

  5. which concept?On an 8-way grid, someone uses Manhattan distance as A*'s heuristic because it's familiar. What property does it break, and what do you see in the output?
    Check
    • Manhattan overestimates with diagonal moves (it says 2 for one diagonal step): not admissible
    • A* can then pop the target before a cheaper route is explored
    • on a 5 × 5 grid it returned 7 cells where 6 is shortest; Chebyshev is the right lower bound

    Missed it? Reopen A*, "The three words".

  6. Why does reweighting every edge by w′ = w + h(v) − h(u) leave the shortest path unchanged, and what condition on h keeps Dijkstra valid on the new weights?
    Check
    • along any s–t path the h terms cancel in pairs: w′(path) = w(path) + h(t) − h(s), the same shift for every path
    • consistency h(u) ≤ w(u, v) + h(v) makes every w′ ≥ 0
    • Dijkstra then pops in order of g + h − h(s): A*'s order

    Missed it? Reopen A*, "Why the source's reweighted Dijkstra is A*".

  7. which concept?You need the distance between every pair of 150 warehouses (dense road table, no negative weights), once a night. What do you run, and roughly how many relax checks does it take?
    Check
    • Floyd–Warshall: one 150 × 150 table and three nested loops
    • 150³ = 3,375,000 checks: trivial for a nightly job
    • for a large sparse graph, Dijkstra from every node would win instead

    Missed it? Reopen Floyd–Warshall, "Which one, and when not to use them".

3 · Project: five answers from one network

What it reinforces: hops vs cost (BFS vs Dijkstra), edge-limited costs and the snapshot (Bellman–Ford), all pairs (Floyd–Warshall), and why a spanning tree isn't a route (MSTs). It uses the MST card's six-vertex graph, first with the edges directed as listed.

A→B 4 A→C 2 C→B 1 B→D 5 C→D 8 C→E 10 D→E 2 C→F 9 E→F 3

Reuse the card snippets. About 50 lines in all:

  1. Hops. BFS from A: how many hops to each node? Which route does BFS find to F, and what does it cost?
  2. Cost. Dijkstra from A: all distances, the parent of each node, the number of heap pops (stale ones included) and the total weight of the shortest-path tree's edges.
  3. Edge limits. Bellman–Ford from A with the snapshot, printing costs after rounds 1 to 5. After which round does E reach its final value, and why does round 5 change nothing?
  4. All pairs. Floyd–Warshall on the six nodes. Count the checks, the checks that improve a value, the ordered pairs (i ≠ j) that are reachable at all, and the largest finite distance.
  5. Wiring. Make every edge undirected. Build the MST with Kruskal and compare its weight with the weight of Dijkstra's tree from A on the undirected graph.
Done when: all five parts print, and you can explain the difference between the three trees you built (BFS's, Dijkstra's and the MST) in one sentence each. Then compare with the check.
Check: the numbers (computed with a Python version of exactly these steps)
  • Hops: B, C at 1; D, E, F at 2. BFS reaches F by A-C-F, which happens to cost 2 + 9 = 11.
  • Dijkstra: A 0, B 3, C 2, D 8, E 10, F 11. Parents B ← C, C ← A, D ← B, E ← D, F ← C. 9 pops for 6 nodes; the tree weighs 2 + 1 + 5 + 2 + 9 = 19. F stays at 11 because E→F gives 10 + 3 = 13.
  • Bellman–Ford: E is ∞, 12, 11, 10 after rounds 1–4 (F is ∞, 11, 11, 11). Round 5 changes nothing: the longest shortest path, A-C-B-D-E, has 4 edges, and with no negative cycle V − 1 = 5 rounds are always enough.
  • Floyd–Warshall: 6³ = 216 checks, 11 improvements, 15 reachable ordered pairs (nothing reaches A, and F reaches nothing), largest finite distance 11 (A → F).
  • Wiring: the MST weighs 13 (C–B 1, A–C 2, D–E 2, E–F 3, B–D 5). Dijkstra's undirected tree from A still weighs 19, because it reaches F through C–F 9 to keep F's own distance at 11 (13 along the MST).
