Bidirectional BFS

Also called two-ended or meet-in-the-middle BFS. Run one breadth-first search from the start and one from the goal, and stop when they touch. The source guide solves Word Ladder I and II this way, under Graphs › Breadth-first Search.

In one breath

A single BFS to a goal d steps away grows a ball of radius d. Two searches that each grow a ball of radius d/2 cover the same distance, and since balls grow exponentially with radius, two small balls are far cheaper than one big one. Grow one layer at a time, always on the side with the smaller frontier, and the first word that lands on the other frontier closes the shortest path.

Why it pays: bd vs 2·bd/2

bdone search, radius d vs 2·bd/2two searches, radius d/2 each
bbranching factor: new neighbours per node10 in the example dlength of the shortest path, in edges6 in the example
b = 10, d = 6: 10^6 = 1,000,000 vs 2 × 10^3 = 2,000 → 500× fewer nodes

A model, not a measurement: it assumes every node has exactly b new neighbours. Real graphs are lumpier, so the counts below are computed on real word lists.

Worked example: hit → cog, words = [hot, dot, dog, lot, log, cog] → 5

edges = radius from hit + radius from cog + the meeting edge = 2 + 1 + 1 = 4 words = edges + 1 = 5 hit → hot → dot → dog → cog

Two words are joined when they differ in exactly one letter. Green = the ladder found; grey = reached but not on it.

In Python

def ladder_length(begin, end, word_list):
    words = set(word_list)           # never mutate the caller's list
    if end not in words:
        return 0
    front, back = {begin}, {end}
    seen = {begin, end}
    length = 1                       # words on the ladder so far
    while front and back:
        if len(front) > len(back):   # always grow the smaller side
            front, back = back, front
        length += 1
        nxt = set()
        for w in sorted(front):
            for v in neighbours(w, words):  # 26 × len(w) tries
                if v in back:        # the two searches touch
                    return length
                if v not in seen:
                    seen.add(v)
                    nxt.add(v)
        front = nxt
    return 0                         # one side ran dry

wl = ["hot", "dot", "dog", "lot", "log", "cog"]
print(ladder_length("hit", "cog", wl))       # → 5
print(ladder_length("hit", "cog", wl[:-1]))  # → 0

The source's version is correct but slower: it strictly alternates sides instead of growing the smaller one, builds its graph by comparing every pair of words (O(n²·m)), and appends beginWord to the caller's list. Its Minimum Genetic Mutation calls a word_ladder that is never defined and tests for infinity; the fix is this search over the letters ACGT, returning words − 1 (mutations), or −1 when it returns 0.

Time O(n · 26m · m) · Space O(n) for n words of length m. Each word joins a frontier at most once (the shared seen set), and expanding it builds 26m candidate strings of m letters each. That's the worst case; meeting in the middle is what keeps the typical case tiny.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ One-way BFS

Correct and simple, but it expands everything closer than the goal: 1,606 words for cold → warm, where two-way needs 29.

✗ DFS or backtracking

Finds a ladder, not the shortest. To prove it's shortest you must try every path, which is exponential in the length.

✗ Strict alternation

The source's version: correct, but when one side sits next to a hub word its frontier balloons, and it still gets grown every other step.

✓ Two-way, smaller side first

Two balls of radius d/2 instead of one of radius d, and each step pays for the cheaper frontier. Same shortest answer.

Is it always worth it?

No. You need one known goal and the ability to walk edges backwards. Word Ladder has both (one-letter changes are symmetric). A maze with "any exit" as the goal, or a directed graph without a reverse adjacency list, doesn't. For small graphs, plain BFS is simpler and the saving is noise (6 vs 4 above).

With edge weights the meet test above is wrong: the first meeting is not always the cheapest, so bidirectional Dijkstra needs a different stopping rule, and A* is often used instead. For Word Ladder II (all shortest ladders), finish the whole layer where the searches meet and keep every parent of each word, as the source's second Tree class does with child_parents; stopping at the first meet would lose ladders. These two points are standard practice, not from the source.
