One operation does almost all the work

Dot product, matrix multiply, shapes, transpose · Raschka A.2, A.2.3, §3.3.1

The problem: Every later card is a page of equations. Without one move, multiplying rows against columns and reading the shapes, each equation looks new. With it, most of them turn out to be the same equation with different matrices.

A dot product is large and positive when two vectors point the same way, near 0 when they are unrelated, and negative when they point opposite ways. That is the whole idea behind attention scores, which card 8 builds.

A matrix multiply is a grid of dot products

A matrix is a stack of rows. Multiplying matrix AAA by matrix BBB means: for every row of AAA and every column of BBB, take their dot product. Each one fills one cell of the answer.

Rows are tokens, and a layer is xWxWxW

This course keeps one convention on every card. Each row of an activation matrix is one token, and a layer multiplies on the right: xWxWxW, with WWW shaped (in,out)(\text{in}, \text{out})(in,out). So W1W_1W1​ is 4×84 \times 84×8 because it takes 4 numbers per token in and gives 8 out, and Wout\WoutWout​ is 4×54 \times 54×5 because it turns 4 numbers into 5 vocabulary scores. Three tokens go in, three rows come out, every time.

PyTorch's nn.Linear(4, 8) does the same maths but stores its weight the other way round, as W⊤W^\topW⊤, shape (out,in)=8×4(\text{out}, \text{in}) = 8 \times 4(out,in)=8×4, and computes xW⊤xW^\topxW⊤. That is why tiny.py copies W.T into PyTorch when it checks itself. The transpose W⊤W^\topW⊤ just swaps rows and columns: entry [i,j][i, j][i,j] moves to [j,i][j, i][j,i]. So KKK (3×43 \times 43×4, one row per token) becomes K⊤K^\topK⊤ (4×34 \times 34×3, one column per token), and QK⊤QK^\topQK⊤ is (3×4)⋅(4×3)=3×3(3 \times 4) \cdot (4 \times 3) = 3 \times 3(3×4)⋅(4×3)=3×3, one score per pair of tokens.

Get the transpose wrong and a non-square shape stops you with an error. A square one doesn't. Wq\WqWq​ is 4×44 \times 44×4, so using Wq⊤\Wq^\topWq⊤​ by mistake runs fine and quietly changes the loss from 2.2067 to 2.1561. Shapes catch most mistakes, but not that one.

That is why GPUs are built around fast matrix multiply, and why "how many multiply-adds" is the usual cost of a model. A handy rule falls out: Qwen does 494,262,272 matmul multiply-adds per token, almost exactly its 494,032,768 parameters. Each weight is used about once per token.

Principle

Learn one move and its shape rule, and most of the model is readable

A dot product measures how much two vectors agree. A matrix multiply does that for every row against every column, and the shapes (m×n)⋅(n×p)→(m×p)(m \times n) \cdot (n \times p) \to (m \times p)(m×n)⋅(n×p)→(m×p) tell you what comes out before you compute anything. Projections, scores, mixing, feed-forward and logits are all this one operation.

Buys you: a way to check any equation in the course by its shapes, and a cost model: count the multiply-adds.

Raschka A.2 and A.2.3 (tensors, shapes, matrix multiply), §3.3.1 (dot products as similarity)

Where this simplification breaks
  • Counting operations is not counting time. Matmuls are the most arithmetic, but GPUs do them so fast that small element-wise steps can take a real share of the time, because they spend it reading and writing memory. "99.7% of the arithmetic" is not "99.7% of the seconds".
  • The non-matmul count is rough. How many operations GELU or softmax "is" depends on how you count exp and tanh. The trend with width doesn't depend on it.
  • Batches and heads add dimensions. Real code multiplies batch×heads×tokens×size\text{batch} \times \text{heads} \times \text{tokens} \times \text{size}batch×heads×tokens×size tensors. The rule is the same: the last two dimensions multiply as matrices, and the others line up (see broadcasting below).
  • Long prompts change the mix. The scores QK⊤QK^\topQK⊤ grow with tokens×tokens\text{tokens} \times \text{tokens}tokens×tokens. On a 7-token prompt they are tiny; at tens of thousands of tokens they become a big share.

3. Why does the matmul share rise from 44.6% to 99.4% as ddd goes from 4 to 896?

Show the answer

A matmul on a 3×d3 \times d3×d input with a d×dd \times dd×d weight costs 3×d×d3 \times d \times d3×d×d, which grows with the square of ddd. Norms, GELU and adds cost a few operations per number, 3×d3 \times d3×d, which grows only with ddd. At large ddd the square term swamps the rest.

  • without d\sqrt{d}d​ the same dot products grow with width and saturate softmax

vs A token becomes a row of a table: An embedding lookup can be written as onehot⁡⋅E\operatorname{onehot} \cdot Eonehot⋅E, a matmul. Why does the operation count here treat it as 0 multiply-adds, and what would it cost if you really multiplied?

Check
  • a one-hot row has a single 1, so the product just copies one row of EEE
  • real code indexes the row instead of multiplying, so it costs no arithmetic
  • multiplying would cost tokens×vocab×d\text{tokens} \times \text{vocab} \times dtokens×vocab×d: 3×5×4=603 \times 5 \times 4 = 603×5×4=60 in the tiny model, 7×151,936×8967 \times 151{,}936 \times 8967×151,936×896 in Qwen
  • the matmul view is useful for gradients, the lookup view for speed
