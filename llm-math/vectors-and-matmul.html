One operation does almost all the work

Dot product, matrix multiply, shapes, transpose · Raschka A.2, A.2.3, §3.3.1

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Every later card is a page of equations. Without one move, multiplying rows against columns and reading the shapes, each equation looks new. With it, most of them turn out to be the same equation with different matrices.

Where it fits: The first card of the course. It sets the convention every card uses (rows are tokens, a layer is x · W) and the dot product that attention scores are built from.

You'll meet: the dot product as agreement between two vectors matrix multiply as a grid of dot products the shape rule (m × n) · (n × p) → (m × p) transpose, and why PyTorch stores W.T why matmul is nearly all the arithmetic at real width

By the end: Compute any entry of a matrix product in the tiny model by hand, predict any product's shape before computing it, and explain with measured numbers why wide models are almost all matmul.

  2. In (m × n) · (n × p), which size disappears, and what happened to it inside each cell?

  4. At d = 4 matmuls are 44.6% of the work and at d = 896 they are 99.4%. Which term grows faster, and by what power of d?

Every card after this one is full of equations, and most of them turn out to be the same equation: rows of numbers multiplied against columns and added up. If you can do that one move by hand and read its shapes, you can follow the whole model. A dot product compares two vectors; a matrix multiply is a grid of dot products; and in a real LLM that grid is 99.7% of the arithmetic.

Try this first

Guess first: one forward pass of the tiny model runs embeddings, three LayerNorms, attention, a GELU, two residual adds and a softmax over the vocabulary. Count every arithmetic operation. What share of them are the multiply-adds inside matrix multiplies: about 10%, 45% or 95%?

Show the answer

About 45% (44.6%: 516 multiply-adds against 642 other operations). At d = 4 the model is so narrow that LayerNorm and GELU cost as much as the matrices. Make the same model 896 wide and the share is 99.4%; real Qwen2.5-0.5B on a 7-token prompt is 99.7%. The section "Wider models are almost all matmul" shows why.

A dot product says how much two vectors agree

A vector here is just a row of numbers, like one token's 4 numbers. The dot product of two vectors of the same length multiplies them position by position and adds the results into one number.

a·b=∑i=0n−1aibi

plain text: a @ b = sum(a[i] * b[i] for i in range(n))

a, b
two vectors, each of length n
n
how many numbers each vector holds, 4 in the tiny model
i
the position, 0 to n − 1
a · b
one number (a scalar)
Filled in, the attention score of sat (query row 2) against cat (key row 1):
Q row 2, 1 × 4
-1.7157-1.89831.5939-1.3733
·
K row 1, 1 × 4
-0.8817-0.3163-0.3271-1.7547
= 1.5126 + 0.6004 − 0.5214 + 2.4097 = 4.0013, the same as S_raw[2,1] in tiny.out.

Three of the four products are positive, because the two rows have the same sign in those positions. A dot product is large and positive when two vectors point the same way, near 0 when they are unrelated, and negative when they point opposite ways. Divide by both lengths and you get the cosine of the angle between them: here 4.0013 / (3.3126 × 2.0157) = 0.5992, a fair match. In sat's row of scores, [0.9141, 4.0013, −4.2507], cat is the most aligned key, so sat will attend to cat most. That is the whole idea behind attention scores, which card 8 builds.

A matrix multiply is a grid of dot products

A matrix is a stack of rows. Multiplying matrix A by matrix B means: for every row of A and every column of B, take their dot product. Each one fills one cell of the answer.

Cij=∑k=0n−1AikBkj,(m×n)·(n×p)→(m×p)

plain text: C = A @ B, C[i, j] = A[i, :] @ B[:, j]

A
left matrix, m × n (here a, 3 × 4)
B
right matrix, n × p (here Wq, 4 × 4)
C
the answer, m × p (here Q, 3 × 4)
i, j
row of A and column of B, which pick the cell of C
k
the shared position that gets summed away
m, n, p
sizes: n must match on both sides; m and p survive
Filled in, Q[0,0] = a row 0 · Wq column 0 = (−0.1255)(−1.0) + (0.3765)(−0.3) + (1.2549)(−0.5) + (−1.5059)(0.3) = 0.12550 − 0.11295 − 0.62745 − 0.45177 = −1.06667 ≈ −1.0667, matching tiny.out. (A 4-decimal number times a 1-decimal weight is exact at 5 decimals, so keep 5 until the end.)

Row 0 of a meets column 0 of Wq to fill one cell of Q. Read the shape rule first: the inner sizes must match and vanish, the outer sizes give the answer's shape. Q has 3 × 4 = 12 cells, so this one matmul is 12 dot products of length 4, or 48 multiply-adds.

Rows are tokens, and a layer is x · W

This course keeps one convention on every card. Each row of an activation matrix is one token, and a layer multiplies on the right: x · W, with W shaped (in, out). So W1 is 4 × 8 because it takes 4 numbers per token in and gives 8 out, and Wout is 4 × 5 because it turns 4 numbers into 5 vocabulary scores. Three tokens go in, three rows come out, every time.

PyTorch's nn.Linear(4, 8) does the same maths but stores its weight the other way round, as Wᵀ, shape (out, in) = 8 × 4, and computes x · Wᵀ. That is why tiny.py copies W.T into PyTorch when it checks itself. The transpose Wᵀ just swaps rows and columns: entry [i, j] moves to [j, i]. So K (3 × 4, one row per token) becomes Kᵀ (4 × 3, one column per token), and Q · Kᵀ is (3 × 4) · (4 × 3) = 3 × 3, one score per pair of tokens.

Get the transpose wrong and a non-square shape stops you with an error. A square one doesn't. Wq is 4 × 4, so using Wqᵀ by mistake runs fine and quietly changes the loss from 2.2067 to 2.1561. Shapes catch most mistakes, but not that one.

Wider models are almost all matmul

Count every operation in one forward pass of the tiny model (matmul multiply-adds counted exactly; LayerNorm, softmax, GELU and adds counted generously, about 1 to 9 operations per number they produce):

StepMatmulOther
Q, K, V: three (3×4)·(4×4)1440
Q·Kᵀ and A·V; scale, mask, softmax7263
output projection ·Wo480
feed-forward ·W1, ·W2; GELU192216

So at d = 4 matmuls are only 44.6% of the work. Now look at how each kind grows. A (3 × d) · (d × d) matmul costs 3 × d × d. A LayerNorm on the same 3 × d input costs a few operations per number, so about 3 × d. Double the width and matmul work goes up four times while everything else only doubles.

← scroll the sketch →

Measured in labs/vectors-and-matmul.py. The same one-block model, widened: 44.6% at d = 4, 92.4% at d = 64, 99.4% at d = 896. Real Qwen2.5-0.5B on a 7-token prompt: 3,459,835,904 multiply-adds against 11,872,560 other operations, 99.7%.

That is why GPUs are built around fast matrix multiply, and why "how many multiply-adds" is the usual cost of a model. A handy rule falls out: Qwen does 494,262,272 matmul multiply-adds per token, almost exactly its 494,032,768 parameters. Each weight is used about once per token.

Predict first: Q and K are both 3 × 4. Does it run? If it fails, which two sizes will the error complain about?

Show the measured result

It stops at that line: ValueError: matmul: Input operand 1 has a mismatch in its core dimension 0 … (size 3 is different from 4). (3 × 4) · (3 × 4) has inner sizes 4 and 3, which don't match. With the transpose it is (3 × 4) · (4 × 3), and the 4s cancel.

At real scale. Qwen2.5-0.5B: the prompt "What is the capital of India?" is 7 tokens, so the input is 7 × 896, one row per token. One query projection is (7 × 896) · (896 × 896) → 7 × 896: 7 × 896 = 6,272 cells, each a dot product of length 896, so 5,619,712 multiply-adds. GPT-2 124M: the input is T × 768 (T up to 1,024), and its query projection is (T × 768) · (768 × 768) → T × 768. Same move, same shape rule.

Principle

Learn one move and its shape rule, and most of the model is readable

A dot product measures how much two vectors agree. A matrix multiply does that for every row against every column, and the shapes (m × n) · (n × p) → (m × p) tell you what comes out before you compute anything. Projections, scores, mixing, feed-forward and logits are all this one operation.

Buys you: a way to check any equation in the course by its shapes, and a cost model: count the multiply-adds.

Raschka A.2 and A.2.3 (tensors, shapes, matrix multiply), §3.3.1 (dot products as similarity)

Where this simplification breaks
  • Counting operations is not counting time. Matmuls are the most arithmetic, but GPUs do them so fast that small element-wise steps can take a real share of the time, because they spend it reading and writing memory. "99.7% of the arithmetic" is not "99.7% of the seconds".
  • The non-matmul count is rough. How many operations GELU or softmax "is" depends on how you count exp and tanh. The trend with width doesn't depend on it.
  • Batches and heads add dimensions. Real code multiplies batch × heads × tokens × size tensors. The rule is the same: the last two dimensions multiply as matrices, and the others line up (see broadcasting below).
  • Long prompts change the mix. The scores Q · Kᵀ grow with tokens × tokens. On a 7-token prompt they are tiny; at tens of thousands of tokens they become a big share.

Check yourself

1. W1 is 4 × 8 and the input to it is 3 × 4. What shape comes out, and how many multiply-adds does it take?

Show the answer

(3 × 4) · (4 × 8) → 3 × 8. That is 24 cells, each a dot product of length 4: 3 × 4 × 8 = 96 multiply-adds, as in the table.

2. Q row 2 · K row 1 = 4.0013 and Q row 2 · K row 2 = −4.2507. What does the sign tell you?

Show the answer

Positive means sat's query and cat's key point roughly the same way, a match. Negative means sat's query and its own key point roughly opposite ways, so sat will give itself very little attention.

3. Why does the matmul share rise from 44.6% to 99.4% as d goes from 4 to 896?

Show the answer

A matmul on a 3 × d input with a d × d weight costs 3 × d × d, which grows with the square of d. Norms, GELU and adds cost a few operations per number, 3 × d, which grows only with d. At large d the square term swamps the rest.

Understand and remember

  • multiply two vectors position by position and add → products are positive where the signs agree → big positive sum = vectors point the same way, negative = opposite → Q row 2 · K row 1 = 4.0013 is sat's best match, so attention scores are dot products
  • matrix product = one dot product per (row of A, column of B) → the shared inner size n is summed away in every cell → (m × n) · (n × p) → (m × p) → reading shapes predicts the output and catches mismatched inner sizes
  • rows are tokens and a layer is x · W with W (in, out) → T tokens in, T rows out → PyTorch's nn.Linear stores Wᵀ (out, in) → a non-square wrong transpose errors, but a square one (Wq, 4 × 4) runs silently and changes the loss 2.2067 → 2.1561
  • matmul on 3 × d with a d × d weight costs 3·d·d; norms, GELU and adds cost a few ops per number, about 3·d → matmul share rises with width: 44.6% at d = 4, 92.4% at d = 64, 99.4% at d = 896, 99.7% in Qwen2.5-0.5B → cost of a model ≈ its multiply-adds
  • detail that matters About one multiply-add per weight per token: Qwen does 494,262,272 matmul multiply-adds per token against 494,032,768 parameters, so you can estimate a model's compute from its size

Reference only

  • tiny model: Q[0,0] = −1.0667, Q[0,1] = −0.0502, cosine of Q row 2 and K row 1 = 0.5992
  • tiny forward pass: 516 matmul multiply-adds, 642 other operations (rough count)
  • A @ B and A.matmul(B) are the same; K.T swaps rows and columns
  • one Qwen query projection (7 × 896) · (896 × 896) = 5,619,712 multiply-adds

vs Every token scores every earlier token: Both this card and the attention-scores card compute 4.0013. What does each card treat as the main idea, and what does attention add on top of the plain dot product?

Check
  • here 4.0013 is a dot product: a measure of how aligned two vectors are
  • attention computes all such pairs at once as Q · Kᵀ, a 3 × 3 matmul
  • it then divides by √d so the spread stays near 1, and masks the future

Check
  • a one-hot row has a single 1, so the product just copies one row of E
  • real code indexes the row instead of multiplying, so it costs no arithmetic
  • multiplying would cost tokens × vocab × d: 3 × 5 × 4 = 60 in the tiny model, 7 × 151,936 × 896 in Qwen
  • the matmul view is useful for gradients, the lookup view for speed

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Make the tiny model's feed-forward hidden size 4 times wider instead of 2 (H = 16 instead of 8) and keep d = 4. Does the matmul share of the forward pass go up or down from 44.6%? Commit first.

Reveal

Up only a little, from 44.6% to 45.2% (708 multiply-adds against 858 other operations). The two FFN matmuls double (96 → 192 each) but so does the GELU count (216 → 432), because GELU runs once per hidden number.
