Every token scores every earlier token

Attention scores, queries and keys, scaled dot-product, causal mask · Raschka §3.4.1, §3.5.1

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Each token's vector only describes that token. Before it can use the words around it, it needs a number for every other token saying how relevant that token is. And in training, a token that can see the next word will just copy its answer.

Where it fits: Builds on the dot product from One operation does almost all the work and on softmax from card 7. This card makes the scores; the next card turns them into weights with softmax and mixes the values.

A token's own vector says nothing about the words around it. To use its context, each token needs a number for every other token: how useful is that one to me? Attention gets those numbers from one matrix product, Q·Kᵀ, scaled by √d, with the future blanked out.

One score is one dot product: sat asks, cat answers

Take sat (row 2) asking about cat (row 1). Multiply its query and cat's key number by number, and add. A dot product is big when two vectors point the same way, so a high score means "this key matches what I'm looking for".

← scroll the sketch →

S_raw[2,1] = Q row 2 · K row 1 = 1.5126 + 0.6004 − 0.5214 + 2.4097 = 4.0013. Divided by √4 = 2, that's 2.0006. It is the top score in sat's row, beating 0.4570 for the and −2.1253 for sat itself.

Why two matrices and not one? If a token's query and key were the same vector, the grid would be symmetric: the would score cat exactly as cat scores the, and "what I want" would always equal "what I am". Separate Wq and Wk let a word go looking for something different from itself, like a verb looking for its subject. Training sets both.

Divide by √d, or one key takes everything

A dot product adds up d products. Add more terms and the total swings wider. For random query and key vectors with spread 1 per number, the measured spread (standard deviation) of q·k is 2.007 at d = 4, 8.005 at d = 64 and 29.898 at d = 896. That tracks √d: 2, 8, 29.93. Dividing by √d brings the spread back to about 1.00 at every size.

← scroll the sketch →

Measured in labs/why.py. Wide scores make softmax pick one winner. With d = 64 and 10 keys, the top key gets 0.868 of the weight on average unscaled, and 0.324 scaled.

Why is a near-certain winner bad? Softmax's slope is A(1 − A), where A is a weight. It is largest at A = 0.5 and shrinks to 0 near 0 or 1. In one measured unscaled row the top weight was 0.9519, and the slope was at most 0.0458 anywhere in the row. A small slope means a small gradient, so Wq and Wk barely learn. The softmax card has the slope; card 16 pushes gradients through it.

Mask the future, because training grades every position at once

One forward pass predicts all three next tokens together: the → cat, cat → sat, sat → on. So the target for position t is the input at position t + 1. If token t could see t + 1, it would be reading its own answer.

Measured in labs/attention-scores.py: without the mask, the would put 0.2416 of its attention on cat, the very word it must predict. Training would reward copying, and at generation time there is no next word to copy. The mask makes training match generation: each token only ever uses what is already written.

Why −inf and not 0? Softmax takes e to the power of each score. e⁰ = 1, which would still give a later token a share. e^(−∞) = 0, so the masked cells get exactly 0 weight and the rest of the row still sums to 1.

Next: Attention is a weighted average of values applies softmax to each row of S_masked to get A, shape 3 × 3. Row 2 comes out [0.1737, 0.8132, 0.0131].

At real scale. GPT-2 124M: a is T × 768 (T up to 1,024). Wq and Wk are 768 × 768, split into 12 heads of 64, so each head's S is T × T and is divided by √64 = 8. Qwen2.5-0.5B on the 7-token prompt: a is 7 × 896, Wq is 896 × 896 (14 query heads of 64), and Wk is 896 × 128 (2 key heads of 64, shared). Each head's S is 7 × 7, divided by 8. Qwen also rotates Q and K by position (RoPE) before the dot product.

Principle

A score is a dot product, kept at a sensible size, and only for the past

Q·Kᵀ scores every pair of tokens in one matrix multiply. Dividing by √d keeps those scores at spread about 1, so softmax stays soft enough to learn from. The mask keeps each position from seeing its own answer.

Buys you: all positions trained in parallel, with the same rule the model will follow when it writes one token at a time.

Raschka §3.4.1 (scaled scores), §3.5.1 (the causal mask)

Where this simplification breaks
  • One head. The tiny model has one head with d = 4, so d_model and the head size are the same. Real models split into many heads, and √d means √(head size), 64 in both GPT-2 and Qwen, not √768 or √896.
  • The √d argument assumes spread 1. It holds for unit-variance q and k. Trained weights drift from that, which is one reason some newer models also normalise Q and K.
  • −inf is an idea. Code uses −inf or a very large negative number, and fused attention kernels never build the full T × T grid in memory. The result is the same.
  • Generation is different. When the model writes one new token, its single query only meets earlier keys (cached), so there is nothing to mask. The mask matters most in training and in reading a long prompt.
  • Positions. Here position is already added into a. Qwen instead rotates Q and K (RoPE), so the score depends on distance; see card 6.

Check yourself

1. Why is S 3 × 3 when Q and K are both 3 × 4?

Show the answer

Q·Kᵀ is (3 × 4)·(4 × 3) = 3 × 3. The 4 numbers per token get summed away in each dot product, leaving one score per (query token, key token) pair.

2. At d = 896, unscaled q·k has spread about 29.9. What happens to softmax, and to learning?

Show the answer

Softmax gives nearly all the weight to one key, so the weights sit near 0 or 1, where the slope A(1 − A) is tiny (at most 0.0458 in the measured d = 64 row). Small slope, small gradient, and Wq and Wk stop learning. Dividing by √896 ≈ 29.93 brings the spread back to about 1.

3. Which cells of S are masked for a 3-token input, and what would go wrong without them?

Show the answer

The three cells above the diagonal: [0,1], [0,2] and [1,2]. Without them, a position could read the next token, which is its own training target. The measured unmasked weight of the on cat is 0.2416.

1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • row of a × Wq → query (what I'm looking for); × Wk → key (what I offer) → score = query · key → big when the two point the same way → Q·Kᵀ gives every pair at once, 3 × 4 times 4 × 3 = 3 × 3
  • q·k sums d products → its spread grows like √d (2.007, 8.005, 29.898 at d = 4, 64, 896) → softmax of wide scores puts almost all weight on one key (0.868 vs 0.324 scaled) → slope A(1−A) near 0 (at most 0.0458) → gradients to Wq and Wk vanish → dividing by √d keeps the spread near 1
  • one pass predicts every next token at once → position t's target is the input at t + 1 → without the mask t could read its own answer → set later cells to −inf → softmax gives them exactly 0 → training matches generation, where the future doesn't exist yet
  • detail that matters The mask is −inf, not 0: softmax exponentiates, e⁰ = 1 would still give the future a share, while e^(−∞) = 0 removes it and the row still sums to 1

Reference only

  • tiny model: S_raw[2,1] = 4.0013, S_scaled[2,1] = 2.0006; row 2 of A = [0.1737, 0.8132, 0.0131]
  • np.triu(ones, k=1) marks the cells above the diagonal; PyTorch uses masked_fill
  • GPT-2 and Qwen2.5 both use head size 64, so they divide by 8
  • Qwen's keys are 896 × 128 (2 shared key heads), its queries 896 × 896 (14 heads)

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Softmax turns scores into shares: Temperature divides logits by T; attention divides scores by √d. What do the two divisions have in common, what sets each value, and what goes wrong when each is too small?

Check
  • both shrink the inputs to softmax and so flatten its output
  • T is a choice at sampling time; √d is fixed by the head size to undo the growth of q·k
  • too small a divisor (T = 0.5, or no ÷ √d) makes the output nearly one-hot
  • for sampling that just means less variety; for attention it also kills the gradients

vs Masked softmax: −∞ means no looking ahead: Karpathy builds the mask from a lower-triangular matrix of ones; tiny.py marks the upper triangle with np.triu(k=1). Are these the same mask, and what would break if you used k=0?

Check
  • yes: keep on and below the diagonal, hide above it
  • k=0 would also hide the diagonal, so a token could not attend to itself
  • row 0 would then be all −inf, and softmax of all −inf is nan

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Double every number in Wq and leave the ÷ 2 alone. What happens to row 2 of A (now [0.1737, 0.8132, 0.0131])? Commit to a direction and a rough value.

Reveal

Every query doubles, so every score doubles, which exactly cancels the ÷ 2. Row 2 becomes [0.0436, 0.9561, 0.0002], the same as deleting the scale. That is why the √d rule is about the spread of the scores at the start, not a fixed property: a model can learn its way back into the saturated region by growing Wq and Wk.
