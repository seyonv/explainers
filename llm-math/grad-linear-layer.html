Let the shapes write the gradient

Linear layer backward, matmul backward, weight gradient, dW = xᵀ·dy, dx = dy·Wᵀ · Raschka A.4, A.5

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The previous card ends with dz, a push on every logit. Without a rule for carrying that push through y = x · W, no weight in the model can learn and nothing below the output head ever gets a gradient.

Where it fits: The second backward stop. It takes dz = (p − y)/3 from the softmax + cross-entropy card and turns it into dWout for the update and dh for the final LayerNorm. It reuses the matmul from card 2 and the chain rule from card 3.

  2. Why do the three positions' pushes on one weight add instead of being averaged, given that the loss is a mean?
  3. dW sums over positions while dx sums over output columns. What decides which size disappears?
  4. When W is square, why can't the shapes alone tell xᵀ · dy from dyᵀ · x?
  5. If a weight matrix is all zeros, which gradients still flow and which are blocked?

The previous card gave us the push on every logit, dz. Now that push has to reach the weights. Almost every weight in a transformer sits inside a layer of the form y = x · W. For that layer, one rule gives every gradient, and the shapes alone tell you how to write it.

Try this first

Guess first: the weight Wout[1,3] is used at all 3 positions of the cat sat. Each position gives it a push. Is its gradient the average of the three pushes, the largest one, or their sum?

Show the answer

The sum: −0.0193 + (−0.0569) + (−0.5340) = −0.6103. The loss is a mean over 3 positions, but that 1/3 is already inside dz. The weight itself just adds up every push it gets.

Where this sits: the first weights the gradient reaches

Forward, the output head was h (3 × 4) times Wout (4 × 5) → z (3 × 5). Backward runs the other way: dz (3 × 5, from the previous card) comes in, and two things go out. dWout (4 × 5) is kept for the update. dh (3 × 4) is passed down to the final LayerNorm, on the next card.

A gradient is written dX. It means ∂L/∂X, the slope of the loss with respect to X: how much L moves per unit nudge of each entry. dX always has the same shape as X, because there is one slope per entry. That one fact does most of the work on this card.

The equations: two products and a sum

y=xW+b∂L∂Wi,j=∑t=0T−1xt,idyt,j dW=x⊤dydx=dyW⊤db=∑tdyt

plain text: dW = x.T @ dy; dx = dy @ W.T; db = dy.sum(0)

x
the layer's input, T × in: one row per token. On the output head it is h, 3 × 4
W
the weights, in × out, learned. On the output head it is Wout, 4 × 5
b
the bias, one number per output column, added to every row (the output head has none)
y
the output, T × out. On the output head it is z, 3 × 5
dy
the gradient arriving from above, T × out, the shape of y. Here dz
dW
the gradient for the weights, in × out, the shape of W
dx
the gradient passed down to the layer below, T × in, the shape of x
db
the gradient for the bias, one number per output column
t
the position (row), 0 to T − 1; T = 3 here
i, j
an input column and an output column of W
⊤
transpose: swap rows and columns, so a T × in matrix becomes in × T

The whole output head, with the real arrays. dWout must be 4 × 5. From h (3 × 4) and dz (3 × 5), the only product that fits and comes out 4 × 5 is hᵀ · dz. Its entry [1,3] is row 1 of hᵀ times column 3 of dz: the sum above. Two other orders don't fit at all, and dzᵀ · h gives the same numbers flipped, 5 × 4.

Derive one entry, and the matrix formula follows

Start from what the forward pass did. Each logit is a dot product: z[t,j] = h[t,0]·W[0,j] + h[t,1]·W[1,j] + h[t,2]·W[2,j] + h[t,3]·W[3,j]. So where does W[1,3] appear? In z[t,3], for every position t, each time multiplied by h[t,1]. Nudge W[1,3] by a tiny amount and z[t,3] moves by h[t,1] times that nudge.

The chain rule says: the loss's slope with respect to W[1,3] is the sum, over every place W[1,3] was used, of (how much z[t,3] moves) × (how much L moves per unit of z[t,3]). That is Σt h[t,1] · dz[t,3]. Do the same for every i and j and you get a grid of sums over t. A grid of "row of one matrix times column of another" sums is a matrix product, and here it is hᵀ · dz.

← scroll the sketch →

The three pushes on one weight. Position 2 should have said on, so its dz[2,3] = −0.3109 is negative, and its h[2,1] = 1.7177 is large. Its product, −0.5340, is most of the total. The nudge test agrees: adding 0.001 to Wout[1,3] changes the loss by −0.000610, a ratio of −0.6102.

Passing it down: dx = dy · Wᵀ

The layer below needs dh, so the same reasoning runs the other way. The number h[t,i] fed all 5 logits of its own row, z[t,0] to z[t,4], each through W[i,j]. Add up its 5 pushes: dh[t,i] = Σj dz[t,j] · W[i,j]. That is row t of dz times row i of W, which is (dz · Woutᵀ)[t,i]. Check the shapes: (3 × 5) · (5 × 4) = 3 × 4, the shape of h.

← scroll the sketch →

Row 2 of dz against row 0 of Wout (column 0 of Woutᵀ) gives dh[2,0] = 0.2976. The biggest term is (−0.3109)(−0.8) = 0.2487: the push for the right word, on, through the weight that links h's column 0 to it.

So the two rules are mirror images. dW sums over the positions (the T rows); dx sums over the outputs (the out columns). In both, the size that disappears is the one that appears in both factors.

Why a sum over positions: every position is a training example

One weight matrix is shared by all 3 positions. Forward, each position ran its own copy of h[t] · Wout. Backward, each copy hands the shared weights its own gradient, h[t]ᵀ · dz[t], and the shared weights collect all of them. Adding is what the chain rule does when one quantity is used in several places.

There is no extra division: the loss already took the mean, so every row of dz carries its 1/3. Think of the 3 positions as 3 training examples in one batch. Their gradients add, exactly as if you trained on each one and summed.

The same sum runs down the rows for a bias. A bias is added to every row, so its gradient is dy summed over rows. For the FFN's output bias c2 it is df.sum(0) = [0.2764, 0.1680, 0.0066, −0.4510], the same as tiny.py's dc2.

One rule, every linear layer

Every matrix in the tiny block is used as x · W, so every one gets dW = xᵀ · dy. Here is that formula, written once, against PyTorch's autograd on all seven:

PyTorch stores the transpose

This course writes a layer as x · W with W shaped (in, out). PyTorch's nn.Linear stores weight shaped (out, in), which is Wᵀ, and computes x @ weight.T. So its weight.grad is dWᵀ: for the output head it is 5 × 4, and its entry [3,1] is our −0.6103. That is the dzᵀ · h from the sketch. It is why tiny.py's torch_check transposes every weight going in and every gradient coming out.

2. dh[0,0] is row 0 of dz times row 0 of Wout. Row 0 of dz is [0.1463, −0.2854, 0.0506, 0.0729, 0.0156] and row 0 of Wout is [−0.6, 0.8, 0.7, −0.8, 0.2]. What is dh[0,0]?

Change it

In llm-math/labs/tiny.py, set the output head to all zeros:

"Wout": np.zeros((D, V)),

Predict first: using dW = xᵀ · dy and dx = dy · Wᵀ, what is dh? Does dWout become zero too? What happens to the gradients of every weight below the head?

Show what happened

dh is all 0.0000, because dx = dz · Woutᵀ and Wout is zero. So every gradient below the head is 0: all of W2, W1, Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, E, P and the norms. PyTorch agrees (largest difference 2.5e-16).

But dWout is not zero. dW = hᵀ · dz needs only the input h, which is unchanged, and dz is now (0.2 − y)/3. Its entry [1,3] is −0.4945. A zero weight learns, but it blocks the gradient to everything beneath it until the first update makes it nonzero.

At real scale. GPT-2 124M, one sequence of 1,024 tokens: the output head's dWout = hᵀ · dz is (768 × 1,024) · (1,024 × 50,257) = 768 × 50,257, a sum over 1,024 positions for each of 38,597,376 entries. Each attention projection is 768 × 768 by the same rule. Qwen2.5-0.5B, the 7-token prompt: dWout is (896 × 7) · (7 × 151,936), a sum over 7 positions. Its head is tied to the embedding table E, so E's gradient adds this one to the one from the lookup. With a batch of sequences, the sum just runs over every row of every sequence.

Principle

A gradient has the shape of what it belongs to, and that pins down the product

For y = x · W, the weight gradient dW = xᵀ · dy sums over the positions that shared the weight. The input gradient dx = dy · Wᵀ sums over the outputs each input fed. Write the shapes down and only one arrangement fits.

Buys you: the backward pass of every Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo, W1, W2 and Wout from one rule, so the later cards only have to find dy.

Raschka A.4, A.5

Where this simplification breaks
  • Shapes can't settle it when W is square. For Wq, x and dy are both 3 × 4, so xᵀ · dy and dyᵀ · x are both 4 × 4. Only one is right (the other is its transpose). Derive one entry, as above, to check.
  • The rule is only for x · W. Elementwise steps like GELU, and steps that mix a row, like softmax and LayerNorm, need their own backward rules. Those are the next two cards.
  • Attention has products of two activations, Q · Kᵀ and A · V, with no weight in them. The same shape logic still works (dA = dC · Vᵀ, dV = Aᵀ · dC), but both sides get a gradient.
  • Real code doesn't loop over positions. It flattens batch × positions into one tall x and does one big matmul, which is why this rule is fast on a GPU.

Check yourself

1. W1 is 4 × 8, its input is 3 × 4 and the gradient at its output, du, is 3 × 8. Write dW1 and dx using only the shapes.

Show the answer

dW1 must be 4 × 8: (input)ᵀ · du = (4 × 3) · (3 × 8). The gradient passed down must be 3 × 4, like the input: du · W1ᵀ = (3 × 8) · (8 × 4).

2. Why is dWout[1,3] the sum of three pushes and not their mean, when the loss itself is a mean?

Show the answer

The 1/3 of the mean is already inside dz = (p − y)/3. Beyond that, one weight used at 3 positions collects a push from each use, and the chain rule adds them: −0.0193 − 0.0569 − 0.5340 = −0.6103.

3. You copy head.weight.grad from PyTorch and it is 5 × 4, but your Wout is 4 × 5. Which is wrong?

Show the answer

Neither. nn.Linear stores Wᵀ, shaped (out, in), so its gradient is dWᵀ. Transpose it: its entry [3,1] = −0.6103 is your dWout[1,3].

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose the tiny model saw a 6-token sequence instead of 3, with the loss still the mean over positions. What happens to the shapes of dWout and dh, and to how many terms each entry of dWout sums?

Reveal

dWout stays 4 × 5, because it has Wout's shape, whatever the length. dh grows to 6 × 4, one row per position. Each entry of dWout now sums 6 terms, and dz carries 1/6 instead of 1/3, so the gradient doesn't grow just because the text got longer.
