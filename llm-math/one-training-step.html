One training step, by hand

Forward pass, loss, backpropagation, SGD and Adam update, gradient check · Raschka §5.2, A.7

The problem: Each earlier card taught one stop on its own. Training needs every stop in order, with the right shapes, in both directions, and one missed array or swapped transpose breaks the whole step.

Where it fits: The capstone. It strings together the forward cards (embedding to loss), the four backward rules from Part 2, and the SGD and Adam updates from the card before, on the same tiny model, so you can do the full step on paper and match PyTorch.

The loss: one number, 2.2067

Each row of ppp gives the right next word a share: 0.1438 for cat, 0.1377 for sat, 0.0673 for on. The loss at each position is −ln⁡-\ln−ln of that share, [1.9390, 1.9825, 2.6985][1.9390,\ 1.9825,\ 2.6985][1.9390, 1.9825, 2.6985], and LLL is their mean:

L=−1T∑tln⁡p[t,yt]=1.9390+1.9825+2.69853=2.2067 L = -\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t \ln p[t, y_t] = \frac{1.9390 + 1.9825 + 2.6985}{3} = \hg{2.2067} L=−T1​t∑​lnp[t,yt​]=31.9390+1.9825+2.6985​=2.2067

That is higher than ln⁡5=1.6094\ln 5 = 1.6094ln5=1.6094, the loss of guessing 0.2 for every word. The random model is confidently wrong: it puts 0.7404 on mat at position 2. That one wrong bet is the biggest single source of loss, and it is where the biggest gradients start.

Backward: the same stops in reverse, with four rules

The backward pass looks long, but it uses only four rules. Each one is a card in Part 2.

  • The top: softmax and cross-entropy together give dz=13(p−y)dz = \tfrac{1}{3}(p - y)dz=31​(p−y), where yyy is 1 at the target word and 0 elsewhere, and the 3 comes from the mean. (The gradient at the top is p − y)
  • A matrix multiply Y=XWY = XWY=XW sends back dW=X⊤dYdW = X^\top dYdW=X⊤dY and dX=dY W⊤dX = dY\, W^\topdX=dYW⊤. You never memorise these: they are the only products whose shapes fit. (Let the shapes write the gradient)
  • A +++ copies the gradient unchanged to both inputs. Where two paths meet on one array, their gradients add. (The residual is a gradient highway)
  • A function applied in place (GELU, softmax, LayerNorm) multiplies the gradient by its own local slope. Softmax and LayerNorm mix a row, so their slope uses the whole row. (The residual is a gradient highway and Backprop through attention, three tokens)

What the gradients tell you, with numbers

The highway carries most of the signal. At row 2, dx2[2,0]=0.4440dx_2[2,0] = 0.4440dx2​[2,0]=0.4440 arrives straight through the +++, and the trip through the FFN and LayerNorm 2 adds 0.2543, giving dx1[2,0]=0.6984dx_1[2,0] = 0.6984dx1​[2,0]=0.6984 (the last digit is rounding). Further down, the attention branch adds only 0.0198 to make dx0[2,0]=0.7182dx_0[2,0] = 0.7182dx0​[2,0]=0.7182. The +++ stops make sure the embeddings get a strong gradient even when a branch passes back very little.

Attention's query weights barely learn on this step. The largest ∣dWq∣|dW_q|∣dWq​∣ is 0.2320, while dWoutdW_{\text{out}}dWout​ reaches 0.6103 and dW2dW_2dW2​ 0.6837. Row 0 of AAA is fixed at 1 (the first token can only see itself), so its scores get a gradient of exactly 0. Row 1 is nearly 50/50, so its gradients are small too. Only row 2's dSdSdS is sizeable, and even it is [−0.1103, 0.1109, −0.0006][-0.1103,\ 0.1109,\ -0.0006][−0.1103, 0.1109, −0.0006].

Words that never appeared get nothing. dEdEdE rows 3 and 4 (on, mat) are exactly 0, because those rows were never looked up. A gradient only reaches the weights that took part in the forward pass.

The update: the same 220 gradients, two ways to step

SGD (stochastic gradient descent) moves each weight by the learning rate times its gradient. Adam divides each gradient by its own running size first, so on step 1 every weight moves by exactly η=0.1\eta = 0.1η=0.1, in the downhill direction.

Do it on paper in about 2 hours

Doing all 3 rows of every array by hand would take a day. What changes at real scale: the size, not the equations

Every equation on this card runs unchanged in a real model. Three things grow.

  • More blocks. Stops 2–11 are one transformer block. GPT-2 124M, the book's model, stacks 12 of them, so the forward list is stops 1, then 2–11 twelve times, then 12–14. The backward pass undoes them in reverse, and the residual +++ in each block keeps the gradient strong all the way down.
  • Wider arrays. Each row is 768 numbers instead of 4, with 12 heads instead of 1. Wout\WoutWout​ is 768×50,257768 \times 50{,}257768×50,257, one column per word in the vocabulary.
  • Batches of long sequences. Instead of one 3-token sequence, each step reads a batch of sequences of up to 1,024 tokens. The loss is the mean over every position of every sequence: the same p−yp - yp−y divided by a bigger count.

The update stays the same too. The book trains with AdamW, which is Adam plus weight decay, at η=0.0004\eta = 0.0004η=0.0004 with weight decay 0.1 (Raschka §5.2), and repeats the step many times instead of once.

At real scale. GPT-2 124M: 12 blocks, hidden 768, 12 heads, vocab 50,257, context 1,024, learned positions, LayerNorm and GELU, so it has the same 14 kinds of stop as the tiny model. Qwen2.5-0.5B: 24 blocks, hidden 896, 14 query heads sharing 2 key/value heads, FFN 4,864 with SwiGLU, vocab 151,936, RoPE instead of PPP, RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm, and Wout\WoutWout​ tied to EEE. That is 494,032,768 weights, about 2.2 million times the tiny model's 220. The 7-token prompt "What is the capital of India?" starts as a 7×8967 \times 8967×896 x0x_0x0​.

Principle

Training is one loop: forward, loss, backward in reverse, step

Every stop on the way down has a matching rule on the way up. The gradient of each array has that array's shape, and four rules cover them all: p−yp - yp−y, the two matrix-multiply products, +++ copies, and multiply by the local slope. The update then uses those 220 numbers once and throws them away.

Buys you: a step you can check by hand on 220 weights, and the same code runs a model with 494 million.

Raschka §5.2, A.7

Where this simplification breaks
  • One sequence of 3 tokens is not a batch. Real gradients are an average over many sequences, so one odd example can't swing a step the way it does here.
  • The check runs in float64. Real training runs in float32 or lower precision, where hand-written and autograd gradients agree to far fewer places.
  • η=1\eta = 1η=1 for SGD is chosen to make the step visible, and it already overshoots on position 2. After 30 steps at η=1\eta = 1η=1 the loss bounces back up to 1.2475 (why.out).
  • There is no dropout, no weight decay, no learning-rate schedule and no gradient clipping. Real training loops add all four. None of them changes the forward or backward equations.
  • The tiny model uses learned positions and LayerNorm, like GPT-2. Qwen's RoPE, RMSNorm and SwiGLU change stops 1, 2, 4, 9, 10 and 12 a little, and their backward rules with them.

1. Why does the backward pass need the forward pass's arrays, not just the weights?

Show the answer

Every rule uses them. dWout=h⊤dzdW_{\text{out}} = h^\top dzdWout​=h⊤dz needs hhh. GELU's slope needs uuu. Softmax backward needs AAA, and LayerNorm backward needs its σ\sigmaσ and normalised rows. That is why training stores the activations, and why it uses much more memory than generating text.

2. dEdEdE rows 3 and 4 are exactly 0. Is that a bug? What happens to those rows under SGD, and under Adam?

Show the answer

Not a bug: on and mat were never looked up, so changing their rows can't change the loss. SGD moves them by 0. Adam moves them by 0 too: with g=0g = 0g=0, m^\hat{m}m^ is 0, so the step is 0/(0+ϵ)=00 / (0 + \epsilon) = 00/(0+ϵ)=0. After 30 Adam steps they are still exactly their starting values.
