Step downhill, at the right size

Gradient descent, SGD, learning rate, momentum, Adam, AdamW, weight decay, warmup, cosine decay, gradient clipping · Raschka §5.2, A.7, appendix D

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The backward pass hands you a gradient for every weight, but a gradient is only a local slope: it says which way is downhill, not how far to go. Step too short and training crawls; step too long and the loss bounces or blows up to inf. And one step size can't suit weights whose gradients differ by 160 times.

Where it fits: The first Learning card. It takes the gradients from the backward-pass cards (dWout, dWq, …) and turns them into new weights, which is the last move of the training step that the tiny-network card did with plain w ← w − η·g.

By the end: Take one SGD step and one Adam step on the tiny model by hand, explain why lr 1.0 bounces while 0.1 settles, and explain why Adam's first step moves every weight by exactly lr.

The backward pass gave each of the 220 weights a gradient. A gradient says which way is uphill, but not how far to step. The optimizer is the rule that turns a gradient into a step: plain gradient descent steps the learning rate times the gradient, and Adam gives each weight a step of about the learning rate, sized by that weight's own recent gradients.

Plain gradient descent: step against the gradient, scaled by the learning rate

any one weight, for example Wout[1,3]. The rule runs on all 220, each with its own gradient.
g

Filled in, Wout[1,3] at learning rate 1.0:

−0.4 − 1.0 × (−0.6103) = −0.4 + 0.6103 = 0.2103

The gradient is negative, which means "raising this weight lowers the loss", so the weight goes up. Do the same to all 220 weights and run forward again: one step takes the loss from 2.2067 to 1.3544.

This is SGD, stochastic gradient descent. "Stochastic" means each step normally uses a random batch of examples. Our tiny model has just one batch (the three tokens), so here it's plain gradient descent. Raschka's training loop in A.7 calls exactly this rule through torch.optim.SGD.

Too small crawls, too big bounces, much too big blows up

Run the same rule 30 times at five learning rates (why.out):

"Blows up" is literal. At lr 10, by step 2 the logits reach 1805.8, two of the three right words get probability 0, and −ln 0 is inf. After that every number is broken.

Why does this happen? The gradient is the slope where you stand, and it says nothing about how far the ground keeps going down. A small step trusts the slope only a little way, so it's always safe but slow. A long step bets that the slope holds a long way, and past the bottom of the valley it doesn't: you land on the far wall. Do that every step and the loss zigzags, as lr 1.0 does at steps 15 to 16 and again at step 29.

Momentum: average the recent gradients, so the zigzags cancel

One fix for zigzagging is to stop trusting each gradient on its own. Keep a running average of the recent gradients, called momentum, and step along that instead. Directions that keep coming back add up; directions that flip from step to step cancel out.

m←β1m+(1−β1)gw←w−η·m

plain text: m = beta1 * m + (1 - beta1) * g; w = w - lr * m

m
the momentum, one number per weight (so the same shape as each weight array). It starts at 0.
β1
beta 1, how much of the old average to keep: 0.9, so 90% old and 10% new
g
this step's gradient for the weight
η
the learning rate
w
the weight
Filled in, step 1 for Wout[1,3]: m starts at 0, so

m = 0.9 × 0 + 0.1 × (−0.6103) = −0.0610

The first step is only a tenth of the gradient. At learning rate 1.0, momentum's first step is exactly SGD at lr 0.1, and the loss after it is the same: 1.2269.

Measured over 30 steps (labs/gradient-descent-and-adam.py): momentum at lr 1.0 reaches 0.0001 and never bounces, while SGD at lr 1.0 ends at 1.2475. Same learning rate, but the averaged step doesn't flip direction each time the gradient does.

Notice the cost, though: because m starts at 0, the first few steps are too short. Momentum at lr 0.1 is slower than plain SGD at lr 0.1 (0.1591 against 0.0871 after 30 steps). Adam fixes that start-up shrinkage, which is what "bias correction" means below.

Adam: divide each weight's step by the size of its own gradients

Momentum still uses one learning rate for every weight. But the gradients in this model differ in size by a lot. dWout[1,3] is −0.6103; dWq[1,0] is −0.0038. The gradients on Wq are smaller than the head's (the largest is 0.232, and all of row 1 is below 0.011), because attention row 0 is fixed at 1 and row 1 is nearly 50/50, so nudging a query barely changes anything yet. With one shared learning rate, a step big enough for Wq is far too big for Wout.

Adam keeps two running averages per weight: the momentum m, and the average of the squared gradient v, which tracks how big that weight's gradients usually are. Then it divides one by the square root of the other.

m←β1m+(1−β1)gv←β2v+(1−β2)g2 m^=m1−β1tv^=v1−β2tw←w−η·m^v^+ε

plain text: m = b1*m + (1-b1)*g; v = b2*v + (1-b2)*g**2; m_hat = m/(1-b1**t); v_hat = v/(1-b2**t); w = w - lr * m_hat/(sqrt(v_hat) + eps)

g
this step's gradient for the weight, one number
m
running average of g, one per weight, starts at 0
v
running average of g², one per weight, starts at 0. It is never negative.
β1, β2
how much old average to keep: 0.9 for m, 0.999 for v (PyTorch's defaults)
t
the step number, starting at 1
m̂, v̂
"m hat" and "v hat": the averages with bias correction. Dividing by 1 − β^t undoes the shrinkage from starting at 0; after a few thousand steps both divide by almost 1.
√v̂
the typical size of this weight's gradients
ε
epsilon, 1e-8: stops a divide by zero when a gradient is exactly 0
η
the learning rate: 0.1 here
Filled in, step 1 (t = 1) for Wout[1,3], g = −0.61026:

m = 0.1 × (−0.61026) = −0.061026, and m̂ = −0.061026 / (1 − 0.9) = −0.61026. That is g again.
v = 0.001 × 0.61026² = 0.0003724, and v̂ = 0.0003724 / (1 − 0.999) = 0.3724, so √v̂ = 0.61026. That is |g| again.
step = 0.1 × (−0.61026 / 0.61026) = 0.1 × (−1), so the weight moves +0.1: −0.4 → −0.3.

The same for Wq[1,0], g = −0.00385: m̂ = −0.00385, √v̂ = 0.00385, the ratio is −1 again, and it also moves +0.1.

← scroll the sketch →

Two weights whose gradients differ by about 160 times. SGD at lr 1 moves them by +0.61026 and +0.00385. Adam's first step at lr 0.1 moves both by +0.1, because on step 1 the ratio m̂ / √v̂ is g / |g|: just the sign.

On step 1 that is exact: Adam's first step moves every weight by lr, in the downhill direction, however big or small its gradient. After that the ratio is no longer ±1. On step 2, Wout[1,3]'s gradient flips to +0.01362, but m still remembers the big negative one, so the weight keeps going up by 0.0653. Wq[1,0]'s gradients stay small and consistent, so it moves 0.0958, nearly the full lr.

That's the point of Adam. Each weight gets a step measured in "how many of its usual gradient sizes", not in raw gradient. Weights with small gradients still learn at a useful speed. At lr 0.1, Adam moves Wq[1,0] 260 times further than SGD at lr 0.1 would on step 1. Over 30 steps at lr 0.1, Adam reaches 0.0002 and SGD 0.0871.

A caveat on that win: the "data" is three tokens, and 220 weights can memorise them. A loss of 0.0002 means the model has learned to repeat cat sat on after the cat sat, not that it has learned language. Comparing optimizers properly needs held-out text, which Raschka's §5.2 loop tracks as the validation loss.

AdamW, and the three things real training adds

Weight decay pulls every weight a little toward 0 on every step, so weights grow large only if the loss really needs them to. AdamW does this separately from the gradient, which is what "decoupled" means:

w←w−η·m^v^+ε−η·λ·w

plain text: w = w - lr * m_hat/(sqrt(v_hat) + eps) - lr * weight_decay * w

λ
lambda, the weight decay: 0.1 in Raschka's recipe
η · λ · w
the decay: the same fraction η·λ of every weight, taken off every step
m̂, v̂, ε, η
as in Adam above
Filled in, Raschka's recipe (§5.2): AdamW with lr 0.0004 and weight decay 0.1.

The decay takes 0.0004 × 0.1 = 0.00004 of each weight off per step. The Adam part moves each weight by about 0.0004 on step 1.

Why separate it? The older way adds λ·w to the gradient (called L2 regularisation). Inside Adam, that extra term then gets divided by √v̂ along with the rest, so weights with big gradients barely decay and weights with small ones decay a lot. Decoupling gives every weight the same pull. On the tiny model, 30 steps at lr 0.1 (labs/gradient-descent-and-adam.py): the size of all 220 weights together grows from 8.4303 to 12.8295 with plain Adam and to 10.1397 with weight decay 0.1. The loss is 0.0002 against 0.0021: decay costs a little fit on the training tokens, in exchange for smaller weights.

Appendix D of the book adds three more pieces to the training loop. Each one scales the step; none changes which way the gradient points:

  • Warmup. Start the learning rate near 0 and raise it over the first steps. At the start m and v know nothing, and the weights are random, so the gradients can be large and erratic; small steps are safer until the averages fill in.
  • Cosine decay. After warmup, lower the learning rate along a cosine curve toward a small value. Near the bottom of the valley long steps overshoot (the lr 1.0 row above), so the steps shrink as training goes.
  • Gradient clipping. Measure the size of the whole gradient, the square root of the sum of all 220 squared gradients. On the tiny model's first step that is 4.3868. If it exceeds a chosen maximum, scale every gradient down by the same factor so the size equals the maximum. The direction stays the same; only a freak, oversized gradient gets cut down before it can throw the weights far off.

Show the answer

At real scale

GPT-2 124M. The same rule runs on each of about 124 million weights. Adam keeps an m and a v the same shape as every weight matrix (for example 50,257 × 768 for the token embedding table), so it stores about 248 million extra numbers. Raschka trains it with AdamW, lr 0.0004, weight decay 0.1 (§5.2).

Qwen2.5-0.5B. 494,032,768 weights, so Adam's m and v add 988,065,536 numbers. At 4 bytes each, that's 3,952,262,144 bytes, about 3.95 GB, before counting the weights or their gradients.

Principle

The gradient chooses the direction; the optimizer chooses the distance

A gradient is only the local slope, so the step has to be short enough that the slope still holds where you land. One shared step size fits some weights and not others, so Adam measures each weight's step in units of that weight's own gradient size, and momentum averages away the zigzags.

Buys you: one learning rate that works across weights whose gradients differ by 160 times, and training that settles instead of bouncing.

Raschka §5.2, A.7, appendix D; measured in labs/why.py and labs/gradient-descent-and-adam.py

Where this simplification breaks
  • Three tokens, memorised. All the "Adam wins" numbers here are about fitting 3 training tokens. They say nothing about text the model hasn't seen; that needs a validation loss.
  • One batch, no noise. Real SGD uses a different random batch each step, so each gradient is a noisy estimate. Part of momentum's job in real training is averaging out that noise, which this card can't show.
  • Our learning rates are huge. lr 0.1 for Adam works on 220 weights and 3 tokens. Real LLMs use around 0.0004 (the book) or less, plus warmup and decay.
  • PyTorch's SGD momentum is written differently. torch.optim.SGD(momentum=0.9) uses m ← 0.9·m + g, without the (1 − β1). Its steps come out about 10 times longer than this card's form at the same lr, so the two learning rates aren't comparable.
  • ε matters when a gradient is exactly 0. Rows 3 and 4 of E (on, mat) were never looked up, so their gradient is 0 and Adam's step is 0 / (0 + 1e-8) = 0. With weight decay, AdamW still shrinks them.
  • Adam isn't the last word. Its per-weight scaling costs two extra numbers per weight. Other optimizers trade that memory or use different scaling; this course stays with AdamW because the book does.

Check yourself

1. SGD at lr 1.0 lowers the loss after one step (to 1.3544) but ends higher after 30 steps (1.2475) than lr 0.1 does (0.0871). What is going on?

Show the answer

Each step trusts the local slope for a long distance. Far from the bottom that's fine, so it starts fast. Near the bottom, a long step lands on the far wall of the valley, and the next gradient sends it back. The loss zigzags (4.2009 at step 29) instead of settling.

2. On step 1, why does Adam move Wout[1,3] (gradient −0.61026) and Wq[1,0] (gradient −0.00385) by the same 0.1?

Show the answer

With bias correction, step 1 gives m̂ = g and v̂ = g², so √v̂ = |g| and the step is lr × g / |g| = lr × (±1). The size of the gradient cancels; only its sign is left. From step 2 on, m and v hold history and the ratio is no longer exactly ±1.

3. Why does AdamW apply weight decay outside the m̂ / √v̂ ratio instead of adding λ·w to the gradient?

Show the answer

If λ·w goes into the gradient, Adam divides it by √v̂ too, so weights with large gradient histories hardly decay and weights with small ones decay a lot. Applying η·λ·w separately takes the same fraction off every weight, which is what weight decay is meant to do.

Adam's first step moves every weight by exactly lr. So what happens to the tiny model's loss if you run Adam at lr 1.0 instead of 0.1, for 30 steps?

Reveal

It gets worse first. Every weight jumps by 1.0 on step 1, far too much for weights that start between −1 and 1, so the loss rises from 2.2067 to 3.3532, peaks at 11.7361 at step 5, and only falls back to 0.2804 by step 30 (scratch run of labs/gradient-descent-and-adam.py's run). Adam fixes uneven gradient sizes, not a bad learning rate.
