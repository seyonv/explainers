The loss is surprise at the right answer

Logits, output head, cross-entropy, negative log-likelihood (NLL), perplexity · Raschka §4.6, §5.1.2

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: After the block, each token is 4 numbers. Training needs one number that says how wrong the model's next-word guesses were, or there is nothing to push down.

Where it fits: The last forward stop. It reuses softmax from card 7 to turn the logits into shares, and it hands the backward pass its first gradient, dz = (p − y)/3, on the next card.

Needs first: Softmax turns scores into shares

  2. Why does −ln give a strong push on a confident mistake while 1 − p gives almost none?
  3. Why can a model with random weights score worse than a model that guesses 0.2 everywhere?

After the block, each token is still just 4 numbers. To learn anything, the model needs one number that says how wrong its guesses were. That number is the loss: for each position, take the probability the model gave the word that really came next, and compute −ln of it.

Try this first

Guess first: a model that spreads its bet evenly over the 5 words (0.2 each) scores a loss of 1.6094. Our tiny model has random weights. Will its loss be lower than 1.6094, about the same, or higher?

Show the answer

Higher: 2.2067. Random weights don't spread the bet evenly. They make the model sure of the wrong word. At the last position it puts 0.7404 on mat and only 0.0673 on the right word, on. Being confidently wrong costs more than shrugging.

Where this sits: the last forward stop

x2 (3 × 4, out of the block) → final LayerNorm → h (3 × 4) → times Wout (4 × 5) → z (3 × 5, the logits) → softmax → p (3 × 5) → pick the right word's share in each row → L (one number, 2.2067).

The inputs are the cat sat. The targets are the same text shifted by one: cat sat on. Logits are raw scores, one per vocabulary word. They can be any size and any sign.

The equations: four numbers in, five scores out, one loss

plain text: h = LNf(x2); z = h @ Wout; p = softmax(z, rows); nll[t] = -log(p[t, target[t]]); L = nll.mean()

x2
the block's output, 3 × 4: one row per token
LNf
the final LayerNorm (scale 1, shift 0 at the start), applied to each row
h
the normalised rows, 3 × 4
Wout
the output head, 4 × 5: one column per vocabulary word, learned
z
the logits, 3 × 5: row t holds a score for every possible next word after position t
p
the probabilities, 3 × 5: each row is positive and adds to 1
t
the position, 0, 1 or 2
j, k
vocabulary indices, 0 to 4 (the cat sat on mat)
yt
the target id at position t: [1, 2, 3], that is cat, sat, on
Lt
the loss at position t, one number each: [1.9390, 1.9825, 2.6985]
L
the loss, one number: the mean of the three, 2.2067
ln
the natural log (base e), so the loss is measured in nats

Softmax of that row: the five e^z add to 15.2629, and e^0.0269 = 1.0273, so p[2, on] = 1.0273 / 15.2629 = 0.0673. The loss there is −ln 0.0673 = 2.6985.

Three tokens in, three predictions out

Causal attention means row t has only seen tokens 0 to t. So each row of p is an honest guess at the next word, and one 3-token sequence gives three training examples at once.

Why −ln, and not something simpler

You could score a guess by how far it missed, 1 − p. Here is what −ln does that 1 − p doesn't, each with a measured number.

1. It is 0 when sure and right, and it has no ceiling

p[target]−ln p1 − p
0.990.01010.0100
0.50.69310.5000
0.12.30260.9000
0.014.60520.9900

1 − p barely tells 0.01 from 0.001. −ln keeps climbing. That shows up in the push on the right word's logit at position 2. With −ln, the slope is p − 1 = −0.9327. With 1 − p it is −p(1 − p) = −0.0628, about 15 times weaker. 1 − p goes quiet exactly when the model is most wrong.

2. Products of probabilities become sums

The chance the model gives the whole text cat sat on is the product 0.1438 × 0.1377 × 0.0673 = 0.001333. Its −ln is 6.6200, exactly the sum of the three losses. Dividing by 3 gives the mean, 2.2067. Sums also survive length. At p = 0.1 per token, 1,024 tokens multiply to 0.000e+00 in float64 (it underflows), while the sum of −ln p is a plain 2357.8.

3. It is the surprise, in nats

−ln p is how surprised you should be to see something you gave probability p. A sure thing surprises you not at all. At position 2, 1 / 0.0673 = 14.8579: seeing on was as surprising as one pick out of about 15 equally likely words. The log is base e, so the unit is the nat (base 2 would give bits).

2.2067 is worse than guessing

A model that knows nothing should spread its bet: 0.2 on each of 5 words. Its loss is −ln 0.2 = ln 5 = 1.6094 at every position. That is the number a freshly built model should score. The tiny model scores 2.2067, above ln 5, because its random weights make it confidently wrong. Its top picks are the, on and mat, and none of them is the target.

Perplexity turns the loss back into a count of words: e^L = e^2.2067 = 9.0855, about 9.1. The model is as unsure as if it were choosing among about 9 words. Guessing gives e^1.6094 = 5, the vocabulary size. A perfect model gives 1.

Predict first: what are the logits, what is p, and what is the loss?

Show what happened

Every logit is 0.0000, so every p is 0.2000 and every position loses 1.6094. The loss is exactly ln 5, 1.609438 in both numpy and PyTorch. With zero scores the model can only guess, and that beats the random head's 2.2067. The gradient at the top becomes 0.0667 for each wrong word and −0.2667 for the target, which is (0.2 − y)/3.

At real scale. GPT-2 124M: h is up to 1,024 × 768, Wout is 768 × 50,257 (38,597,376 weights), z is 1,024 × 50,257, and guessing scores ln 50,257 = 10.8249. Qwen2.5-0.5B: the 7-token prompt gives h of 7 × 896, Wout is 896 × 151,936 (136,134,656 weights) and is tied to the embedding table E (the same numbers, transposed), z is 7 × 151,936, and guessing scores ln 151,936 = 11.9312. Raschka's model keeps a separate head and discusses tying in §4.6.

Principle

Score a prediction by the probability it gave to what really happened, on a log scale

Only the right word's share enters the loss, but softmax shares add to 1. So raising that share means taking probability from the others, including the confident wrong one. The log makes confident mistakes cost the most and lets the losses of many tokens add up.

Buys you: one number to push down, with a ready-made baseline (ln V) that tells you at step 0 whether the model is set up sanely.

Raschka §5.1.2

Where this simplification breaks
  • Real code never forms p and then takes its log. cross_entropy takes the logits and computes logsumexp(z) − z[target]. For row 2 that is 2.7254 − 0.0269 = 2.6985, the same number, with no tiny p to underflow.
  • Real training takes the mean over a batch of many sequences, not 3 positions of one. The formula is the same.
  • A low loss on 3 tokens just means memorising. Adam at lr 0.1 drives this loss to 0.0002 in 30 steps. A real loss is only meaningful on text the model didn't train on.
  • Losses are comparable only with the same vocabulary. ln 5 and ln 151,936 are different floors, so compare a loss with its own ln V.

Check yourself

1. The loss reads only the right word's probability. How can it still make the model less sure of mat?

Show the answer

Each row of p adds to 1, and mat's logit is in the denominator of every share. Raising p[on] means the other shares must shrink. The next card shows the exact push on every logit: p − y.

2. Why is ln 5 = 1.6094 the loss to expect from an untrained 5-word model, and why is 2.2067 a warning sign?

Show the answer

Knowing nothing means p = 0.2 everywhere, and −ln 0.2 = ln 5. Scoring above it means the model is confidently wrong (0.7404 on mat), usually because the starting weights make the logits too large.

3. Another model's loss on the same 5 words is 2.3026. What is its perplexity, and is it better than guessing?

Show the answer

e^2.3026 = 10, since −ln 0.1 = 2.3026. That is worse than guessing, whose perplexity is 5.

Understand and remember

  • z = h · Wout gives one score per vocabulary word → softmax turns each row into shares that add to 1 → the loss at position t is −ln of the target's share → position 2: p[on] = 0.0673 → 2.6985; mean of [1.9390, 1.9825, 2.6985] = 2.2067
  • −ln p is 0 at p = 1 and has no ceiling as p → 0 → a confident mistake costs the most → its slope on the right logit is p − 1 = −0.9327, while 1 − p gives −p(1 − p) = −0.0628 → −ln keeps the learning signal exactly where the model is most wrong
  • Knowing nothing means p = 1/V for every word → loss ln V (ln 5 = 1.6094) → the tiny model scores 2.2067, above it → random weights make it confidently wrong (0.7404 on mat) → perplexity e^L ≈ 9.1 vs 5 for guessing
  • The probability of a whole text is a product of per-token shares → −ln turns the product into a sum (0.001333 → 6.6200 = 3 × 2.2067) → long texts don't underflow (1,024 tokens at 0.1: product 0, sum 2357.8) and every position adds its own training signal
  • detail that matters Causal masking means row t has seen only tokens 0..t → every row is an honest next-word guess → 3 tokens give 3 predictions, and the mean over positions is the loss PyTorch's cross_entropy returns (2.206682)
  • detail that matters Real code computes logsumexp(z) − z[target] straight from the logits (row 2: 2.7254 − 0.0269 = 2.6985) → same number, but no tiny p ever has to be formed and logged

Reference only

  • Shapes in the tiny model: h 3 × 4, Wout 4 × 5, z and p 3 × 5, L one number
  • Targets are the inputs shifted by one: ids [1, 2, 3] = cat sat on
  • GPT-2 124M: Wout 768 × 50,257 (38,597,376 weights), ln 50,257 = 10.8249; Qwen2.5-0.5B: 896 × 151,936 (136,134,656), tied to E, ln 151,936 = 11.9312

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Softmax turns scores into shares: The softmax card used softmax for attention weights; here it produces next-word probabilities. What is the same, what is different about where the scores come from and what happens to the output?

Check
  • same formula: e^z over the row's sum, shares add to 1
  • attention's scores are QKᵀ/√d over earlier tokens; here they are h · Wout over the vocabulary
  • attention's shares weight a mix of values; here one share is read by −ln to give the loss
  • both are hurt by huge scores: saturated attention, or a confident wrong guess

vs The gradient at the top is p − y: The loss reads one probability, yet its gradient on the logits touches all five. How does the next card's p − y follow from this card's equations?

Check

  • differentiating −ln softmax gives p for each word and p − 1 for the target

vs The loss you should see at step 0: That card uses ln V as the loss a well-initialised model should start at. Our tiny model starts at 2.2067, above ln 5. What does each card say causes the gap, and how would you fix it?

Check
