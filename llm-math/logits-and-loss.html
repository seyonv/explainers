The loss is surprise at the right answer

Logits, output head, cross-entropy, negative log-likelihood (NLL), perplexity · Raschka §4.6, §5.1.2

The problem: After the block, each token is 4 numbers. Training needs one number that says how wrong the model's next-word guesses were, or there is nothing to push down.

After the block, each token is still just 4 numbers. To learn anything, the model needs one number that says how wrong its guesses were. That number is the loss: for each position, take the probability the model gave the word that really came next, and compute −ln⁡-\ln−ln of it.

Try this first

Guess first: a model that spreads its bet evenly over the 5 words (0.2 each) scores a loss of 1.6094. Our tiny model has random weights. Will its loss be lower than 1.6094, about the same, or higher?

Show the answer

Higher: 2.2067. Random weights don't spread the bet evenly. They make the model sure of the wrong word. At the last position it puts 0.7404 on mat and only 0.0673 on the right word, on. Being confidently wrong costs more than shrugging.

Where this sits: the last forward stop

x2x_2x2​ (3×43 \times 43×4, out of the block) → final LayerNorm → hhh (3×43 \times 43×4) → times Wout\WoutWout​ (4×54 \times 54×5) → zzz (3×53 \times 53×5, the logits) → softmax → ppp (3×53 \times 53×5) → pick the right word's share in each row → LLL (one number, 2.2067).

The inputs are the cat sat. The targets are the same text shifted by one: cat sat on. Logits are raw scores, one per vocabulary word. They can be any size and any sign.

Why −ln⁡-\ln−ln, and not something simpler

You could score a guess by how far it missed, 1−p1 - p1−p. Here is what −ln⁡-\ln−ln does that 1−p1 - p1−p doesn't, each with a measured number.

1. It is 0 when sure and right, and it has no ceiling

1−p1 - p1−p barely tells 0.01 from 0.001. −ln⁡-\ln−ln keeps climbing. That shows up in the push on the right word's logit at position 2. With −ln⁡-\ln−ln, the slope is p−1=p - 1 =p−1= −0.9327-0.9327−0.9327. With 1−p1 - p1−p it is −p(1−p)=-p(1 - p) =−p(1−p)= −0.0628-0.0628−0.0628, about 15 times weaker. 1−p1 - p1−p goes quiet exactly when the model is most wrong.

2. Products of probabilities become sums

The chance the model gives the whole text cat sat on is the product 0.1438×0.1377×0.0673=0.0013330.1438 \times 0.1377 \times 0.0673 = 0.0013330.1438×0.1377×0.0673=0.001333. Its −ln⁡-\ln−ln is 6.6200, exactly the sum of the three losses. Dividing by 3 gives the mean, 2.2067. Sums also survive length. At p=0.1p = 0.1p=0.1 per token, 1,024 tokens multiply to 0.000e+00 in float64 (it underflows), while the sum of −ln⁡p-\ln p−lnp is a plain 2357.8.

3. It is the surprise, in nats

−ln⁡p-\ln p−lnp is how surprised you should be to see something you gave probability ppp. A sure thing surprises you not at all. At position 2, 1/0.0673=14.85791 / 0.0673 = 14.85791/0.0673=14.8579: seeing on was as surprising as one pick out of about 15 equally likely words. The log is base eee, so the unit is the nat (base 2 would give bits).

2.2067 is worse than guessing

A model that knows nothing should spread its bet: 0.2 on each of 5 words. Its loss is −ln⁡0.2=ln⁡5=1.6094-\ln 0.2 = \ln 5 = 1.6094−ln0.2=ln5=1.6094 at every position. That is the number a freshly built model should score. The tiny model scores 2.2067, above ln⁡5\ln 5ln5, because its random weights make it confidently wrong. Its top picks are the, on and mat, and none of them is the target.

Perplexity turns the loss back into a count of words: eL=e2.2067=9.0855e^{L} = e^{2.2067} = 9.0855eL=e2.2067=9.0855, about 9.1. The model is as unsure as if it were choosing among about 9 words. Guessing gives e1.6094=5e^{1.6094} = 5e1.6094=5, the vocabulary size. A perfect model gives 1.

Principle

Score a prediction by the probability it gave to what really happened, on a log scale

Only the right word's share enters the loss, but softmax shares add to 1. So raising that share means taking probability from the others, including the confident wrong one. The log makes confident mistakes cost the most and lets the losses of many tokens add up.

Buys you: one number to push down, with a ready-made baseline (ln⁡V\ln VlnV) that tells you at step 0 whether the model is set up sanely.

Raschka §5.1.2

Where this simplification breaks
  • Real code never forms ppp and then takes its log. cross_entropy takes the logits and computes the log-sum-exp form, Lt=ln⁡∑kez[t,k]−z[t,yt]L_t = \ln \sum_{k} e^{z[t,k]} - z[t, y_t]Lt​=ln∑k​ez[t,k]−z[t,yt​]. For row 2 that is ln⁡∑kez[2,k]−z[2,3]=2.7254−0.0269=2.6985\ln \sum_k e^{z[2,k]} - z[2,3] = 2.7254 - 0.0269 = 2.6985ln∑k​ez[2,k]−z[2,3]=2.7254−0.0269=2.6985, the same number, with no tiny ppp to underflow.
  • Real training takes the mean over a batch of many sequences, not 3 positions of one. The formula is the same.
  • A low loss on 3 tokens just means memorising. Adam at η=0.1\eta = 0.1η=0.1 drives this loss to 0.0002 in 30 steps. A real loss is only meaningful on text the model didn't train on.
  • Losses are comparable only with the same vocabulary. ln⁡5\ln 5ln5 and ln⁡151,936\ln 151{,}936ln151,936 are different floors, so compare a loss with its own ln⁡V\ln VlnV.
