Softmax turns scores into shares

softmax, normalised exponential, softmax with temperature · Raschka §3.3.1, §5.3.1

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The model's layers end in raw scores that can be negative or huge. You can't average with them or sample a word from them until they're positive and add to 1, and the obvious fix (divide by the sum) gives negative "probabilities".

Where it fits: First stop that turns vectors into weights. It takes the rows of numbers that the embedding and matrix-multiply cards produce, and it's used twice: on attention scores (next card) and on the logits at the top (the loss card).

  3. Why does mat's share relate only to the gap between its score and the others, not to the scores' size?

The model's layers end in plain scores: any real number, negative or large. But to choose a next word, or to decide how much one token listens to another, it needs shares that are positive and add up to 1. Softmax makes them: raise e to each score, then divide by the total.

Try this first

Guess first: three scores are 1000, 999 and 998. Only the gaps matter, so what share should the first one get? Then guess what a computer gets if it works out exp(1000) directly.

Show the answer

The shares are 0.6652, 0.2447, 0.0900, the same as for the scores 0, −1 and −2. The direct route breaks: exp(1000) is too big for a float, so it comes out as inf, and inf ÷ inf gives nan ("not a number") for every share. Fixing this takes one subtraction, shown below.

Where this sits: the model uses it twice

Inside attention (the next card): the scaled scores S, 3 × 3, one row per token, go in. Softmax runs on each row, and the attention weights A, 3 × 3, come out. Each row says how much a token listens to each earlier one.

At the very top: the logits z, 3 × 5 (one score per vocabulary word, for each position), go in. Softmax runs on each row, and the next-token probabilities p, 3 × 5, come out. The loss reads them on the card after.

Same function both times. It always works along one row, and every row it returns adds up to 1.

Exponentiate, then divide by the total

softmax(z)i=exp(zi)∑j=1nexp(zj)

plain text: p = exp(z) / exp(z).sum() (one row at a time)

z
one row of scores, length n: 1 × 5 for a row of logits, 1 × 3 for a row of attention scores
n
how many scores are in the row: 5 (the vocabulary) or 3 (the tokens so far)
zi
one score in that row, a single number
exp
e (2.7183…) raised to that power. Always positive, and a bigger input always gives a bigger output
∑j
add up exp(zj) over all n entries in the same row: one number, the total
softmax(z)i
entry i's share of the total, between 0 and 1. The output row is 1 × n, like the input
Filled in, last logits row, entry mat: exp(2.4248) = 11.3000, the row's total is 15.2629, so the share is 11.3000 ÷ 15.2629 = 0.7404.

Worked through on the tiny model's last row

This is the row of logits for position 3, where the model has read the cat sat and has to guess the next word. Five scores, one per word in the vocabulary.

z, last row (1 × 5)
0.4963−0.5355−0.34580.02692.4248

Step 1, exponentiate each score. Negative scores become numbers between 0 and 1. Positive ones grow, and the biggest grows fastest.

exp(z) (1 × 5), total 15.2629
1.64260.58540.70771.027311.3000

Step 2, divide each one by the total, 15.2629. For the: 1.6426 ÷ 15.2629 = 0.1076.

p = softmax(z) (1 × 5), sums to 1
0.10760.03840.04640.06730.7404

These match tiny.py's printed p exactly. One thing to notice: mat's share is 6.8792 times the's. That is exp(2.4248 − 0.4963) = exp(1.9285) = 6.8792. Softmax turns a gap between two scores into a ratio between two shares. The scores' actual size never matters, only how far apart they are.

Why exp, and not just dividing by the sum

The simplest way to make numbers add to 1 is to divide each one by their sum. Try it on the same row. The sum of the five scores is 2.0667, and dividing gives:

z / sum(z): adds to 1, but these aren't shares
0.2401−0.2591−0.16730.01301.1732

They add up to 1, and they're useless: −0.2591 is a "probability" below zero, and 1.1732 is one above 100%. If the scores happened to sum to 0, you'd divide by zero.

exp fixes all of it at once:

  • Always positive. exp of any number is above 0, so every share is above 0 and the total can't be 0.
  • Keeps the order. A bigger score always gives a bigger exp, so the top score is still the top share.
  • Gaps become ratios. exp(a) ÷ exp(b) = exp(a − b), which is why a shift of the whole row changes nothing (next section).

exp is the simplest function that is always positive, keeps the order, and turns differences into ratios. It also undoes the log that the loss takes on the card after, which is why that pair simplifies so neatly.

Subtract the row's largest score first: same answer, no overflow

Pick any number c and subtract it from every score. The e−c comes out of the top and the bottom, and cancels:

exp(zi−c)∑jexp(zj−c)=e−cexp(zi)e−c∑jexp(zj)=softmax(z)i

plain text: z = z - z.max(); p = exp(z) / exp(z).sum() # same p, nothing overflows

c
any single number subtracted from the whole row. Code uses the row's largest score, z.max()
e−c
a constant factor, the same for every entry, so it cancels in the fraction
zi, ∑j, exp
as above: one score, the sum over the row, e to the power
Filled in, last logits row, c = 2.4248: z − c = [−1.9285, −2.9603, −2.7706, −2.3979, 0], exp = [0.1454, 0.0518, 0.0626, 0.0909, 1.0000], total 1.3507, so p = [0.1076, 0.0384, 0.0464, 0.0673, 0.7404]. The same p. Every exp shrank by the same factor, exp(2.4248) = 11.3000.

Why bother, if the answer is the same? Because a computer's numbers have a ceiling. In 32-bit floats (float32, what most training uses), exp of anything above 88.72 is too big and becomes inf. Back to the guess at the top:

  • Directly: [1000, 999, 998] → exp(1000) = inf → the shares come out [nan, nan, nan].
  • Max first: subtract 1000 → [0, −1, −2] → exp = [1, 0.3679, 0.1353], total 1.5032 → [0.6652, 0.2447, 0.0900].

After subtracting the max, the biggest exp is exactly 1, so nothing can overflow, and the total is at least 1, so you never divide by zero. That's why tiny.py's softmax starts with this subtraction. It's also why Raschka, in §3.3.1, moves from a hand-written softmax to PyTorch's built-in torch.softmax, which takes care of it for you.

A masked score of −inf becomes a share of exactly 0

← scroll the sketch →

The tiny model's real attention scores and weights. A token mustn't look at words that come after it, so those cells are set to −inf before softmax. exp(−inf) is exactly 0, so they get 0 weight, and the rest of each row still adds to 1.

That's the whole trick behind the causal mask, which the next card builds. You don't remove the future tokens from the row; you give them a score whose exp is 0. The first row has only one real score left, so it gets all of the weight, 1.0000, whatever that score is.

Temperature: divide the scores before softmax

pi=softmax(z/T)i=exp(zi/T)∑jexp(zj/T)

plain text: p = softmax(z / T)

T
the temperature, one positive number chosen when you generate text. T = 1 is plain softmax
z, p
one row of logits (1 × 5) and the probabilities that come out (1 × 5)
Filled in, last logits row, share for mat: T 0.5 → 0.9652, T 1 → 0.7404, T 2 → 0.4628.

← scroll the sketch →

Dividing by T stretches or squeezes the gaps between scores. Gaps become ratios (see above), so wider gaps make the top word take more, and narrower ones spread the 1.0 out.

Raschka uses this in §5.3.1 to control how adventurous text generation is. It's only a dial for sampling: training always uses T = 1. Temperature changes how sharp the shares are, never their order.

Do it by hand (about 3 minutes, with a calculator)

The last row of the tiny model's scaled attention scores is [0.4570, 2.0006, −2.1253]. Nothing in this row is masked, because sat may look at all three tokens. Subtract the max, exponentiate, add up and divide. What are the three attention weights?

Show the answer

The max is 2.0006, so the shifted row is [−1.5436, 0, −4.1259]. The exps are [0.2136, 1, 0.0161], and the total is 1.2298. Dividing gives [0.1737, 0.8132, 0.0131], which is row 3 of A in tiny.out. sat puts most of its attention on cat.

Change it

In a copy of llm-math/labs/tiny.py, delete the max-subtraction line inside softmax:

z = z - z.max(-1, keepdims=True)

Predict first: does the loss change from 2.2067? Does anything break?

Show what happened

Nothing changes. The whole printout was identical, line for line: loss 2.2067, and the largest change in any probability was 2.2e-16 (rounding noise). The tiny model's biggest logit is 2.4248, far below the 88.72 ceiling, so the max trick has nothing to rescue.

Then push it: in the same copy, multiply Wout by 1000. The biggest logit becomes 2424.7995, and mat's probability comes out as nan. With the subtraction put back, it comes out as 1.0000. The trick only matters once scores get big, which they do in real models.

At real scale. The tiny model runs softmax on 3 attention rows (length 1 to 3) and 3 logits rows (length 5). GPT-2 124M: attention rows up to 1,024 long, in 12 heads × 12 layers, and each logits row has 50,257 entries, one per vocabulary word. Qwen2.5-0.5B: 14 query heads × 24 layers, so on the 7-token prompt "What is the capital of India?" that's 24 × 14 × 7 = 2,352 attention rows of length 1 to 7, and each logits row has 151,936 entries. The equation is the same at every size.

Principle

exp makes every score positive, and dividing by the total makes them shares

Only the gaps between scores matter. That one fact explains three things on this card: shifting the row by its max changes nothing, temperature rescales the gaps, and a gap of −inf gives exactly 0.

Buys you: one function that turns any row of scores into weights you can average with (attention) or sample from (the next word), safely, at any size.

Raschka §3.3.1 (normalising attention scores with softmax), §5.3.1 (temperature scaling)

Where this simplification breaks
  • The max trick stops overflow, not underflow. In the Wout × 1000 run above, the right word's probability rounds to 0, so −log p is inf. That's why real code computes the log of softmax in one step (log-softmax) instead of taking a log afterwards. The loss card comes back to this.
  • Lower precision has a much lower ceiling. In 16-bit floats (float16), exp overflows above 11.09 instead of 88.72 (measured). bfloat16 keeps float32's range, 88.72, which is one reason training uses it.
  • No share is ever exactly 0 unless you mask it. Over 151,936 words, thousands of tiny shares still add up to real probability, so samplers cut the tail off (top-k, top-p). The decoding card covers those.
  • Big models don't compute the row in one go. Fast attention kernels process a long row in blocks and keep a running max as they go. The maths is this same subtraction trick, done piece by piece.

Check yourself

1. Add 100 to every logit in the last row. What happens to p?

Show the answer

Nothing. p is still [0.1076, 0.0384, 0.0464, 0.0673, 0.7404] (measured). A constant added to every score comes out as a factor of e100 on the top and the bottom, and cancels. It's the same algebra as the max trick, with c = −100.

2. Why does a masked cell get a weight of exactly 0, and not just a very small one?

Show the answer

Its score is −inf, and exp(−inf) is exactly 0, so it adds nothing to the top or the bottom of the fraction. Any finite score, however negative, would leave a tiny positive weight.

3. At T = 2, which word gets the biggest share? Could any temperature make on the top word?

Show the answer

mat, with 0.4628. No temperature can change the top word: dividing every score by the same positive T keeps their order, and exp keeps it too. Only training (changing the weights) can make on win.

Understand and remember

  • exp of any number is positive and grows with its input → every share is above 0 and the order of the scores is kept → dividing by the total makes the row add to 1 (last logits row: total 15.2629, mat 11.3000 → 0.7404)
  • exp(a) ÷ exp(b) = exp(a − b) → only the gaps between scores matter → subtracting any constant c from the whole row cancels as e−c on the top and bottom → subtracting the max gives the same p, and the largest exp becomes exactly 1
  • float32 exp overflows above 88.72 → exp(1000) = inf and inf ÷ inf = nan → shifting [1000, 999, 998] to [0, −1, −2] gives [0.6652, 0.2447, 0.0900] safely
  • Dividing by T > 0 scales every gap by the same factor and keeps the order → small T widens the gaps (mat 0.9652 at T 0.5), big T narrows them (0.4628 at T 2) → a sampling dial that can't change the top word
  • detail that matters exp(−inf) = 0 exactly → a masked score contributes nothing to the top or bottom of the fraction → the causal mask is just −inf written into the score grid before softmax
  • detail that matters The max trick prevents overflow but not underflow → with very large logits the right word's p rounds to 0 and −log p is inf → real code computes log-softmax in one step

Reference only

  • Softmax always runs along the last axis, one row at a time: exp(z) / exp(z).sum(-1, keepdims=True)
  • exp overflow ceilings: float32 and bfloat16 88.72, float16 11.09
  • PyTorch: torch.softmax(x, dim=-1) does the max subtraction internally

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Attention row 1 has one real score and two −inf cells. Suppose instead that every cell in a row were −inf (a token allowed to see nothing). What would softmax return, and why?

Reveal

nan for every entry. Every exp is 0, so the total is 0, and 0 ÷ 0 is undefined. Even the max trick can't help, because the max is −inf and −inf − (−inf) is nan. That's why the causal mask always leaves the diagonal open: each token can at least see itself.
