The residual is a gradient highway

Backprop through the residual add, GELU and LayerNorm · skip connection, shortcut connection, GELU′, LayerNorm backward · Raschka §4.4

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The first layers of a deep model learn only from the gradient that reaches them. Through a plain stack of 24 tanh layers that gradient arrives at size 3.52e-16, so the bottom layers barely train. The backward pass needs a path that doesn't shrink, and it needs correct rules for the GELU and LayerNorm it passes through.

Where it fits: Comes after the output head's gradient (cards 13 and 14), which delivers dx2. It runs the forward pass of the feed-forward half of the block in reverse: back through x2 = x1 + f, GELU and LayerNorm 2, reusing the linear-layer rule for W2 and W1.

The first layers of a deep model learn only from the gradient that reaches them, and a gradient that passes through many layers usually shrinks along the way. At every + in the block, the incoming gradient is copied back unchanged, so there's always one path that doesn't shrink. This card follows the gradient for the token sat backward through the feed-forward half of the block, using three rules: through the +, through GELU, and through LayerNorm.

Try this first

Guess first: the gradient arriving at the block's output for sat is dx2 row 2 = [0.4440, 0.0408, 0.0180, −0.5029]. The block computed x2 = x1 + f. When the gradient reaches x1, will it be smaller than dx2, the same, or bigger?

Show the answer

Bigger. dx1 row 2 = [0.6984, −0.2800, 0.2120, −0.6303]. Its size (the square root of the sum of squares) is 1.0042, against 0.6724 for dx2. The + passes all of dx2 straight through, and then the gradient coming back through the feed-forward branch is added on top.

Where this sits

A gradient dX means ∂L/∂X: how much the loss L changes per small nudge of each entry of X. It always has the same shape as X. The backward pass runs the forward pass in reverse, one step at a time.

Comes in: dx2, 3 × 4. It came down from the loss through the output head (cards 13 and 14) and the final LayerNorm, whose backward is rule 3 below.

This step: back through x2 = x1 + f, where f = GELU(u) W2 + c2, u = bn W1 + c1 and bn = LN2(x1). On the way it gives the weight gradients dW2 (8 × 4) and dW1 (4 × 8).

Goes out: dx1, 3 × 4. It goes on into the attention half (card 16), where rules 1 and 3 apply again at x1 = x0 + o and LayerNorm 1.

Rule 1: a + copies the gradient to both of its inputs

In x2 = x1 + f, nudge one entry of x1 by 0.001 and that entry of x2 moves by exactly 0.001. The slope is 1. The same is true for f. The chain rule multiplies the incoming gradient by that slope, so both inputs get an exact copy of dx2.

But x1 is used twice in the forward pass: once on the stream, and once as the input to LayerNorm 2 at the start of the branch. When a value feeds two places, its gradient is the sum of what comes back from each.

Rule 2: GELU scales each number's gradient by its own slope

First the copy goes back through W2. That's the linear-layer rule from card 14: dgu = df @ W2.T, a (3 × 4)(4 × 8) product, 3 × 8. Then it meets GELU. GELU works on each of the 24 numbers on its own, so its backward does too. Each gradient is multiplied by GELU's slope at that number's own u.

The slopes match what why.py measured on the GELU curve: 1.0830 at u = 1 and −0.0830 at u = −1. Where u is positive, the gradient passes at full strength or a little more. Where u is negative, it's cut hard: to 0.2241 of its size at u = −0.3611, and to about a tenth or less further left. A negative slope also flips the sign (−0.0871 became +0.0106). GELU's backward is a filter: each hidden unit passes back gradient in proportion to how open it was on the way forward.

Then the weight gradient. W1's gradient uses the linear-layer rule again: the input, turned on its side, times the output's gradient. dW1 = bn.T @ du is (4 × 3)(3 × 8) = 4 × 8. Each entry adds up one product per token. Entry [0, 0] is the biggest gradient in the FFN:

dW1[0,0] = (−1.0647)(−0.0021) + (−1.1288)(0.0660) + (1.0700)(−0.3531) = 0.0023 − 0.0745 − 0.3778 = −0.4500

Almost all of it comes from sat, the token with the big gradient. This is the same −0.4500 that the nudge test confirmed in why.out: nudging W1[0,0] by 0.001 changed the loss by −0.000450.

Rule 3: LayerNorm's backward takes out the mean and the spread

Next, dbn = du @ W1.T (3 × 8 times 8 × 4) carries the gradient back to LayerNorm 2's output. At the start γ is 1, so the gradient on the normalised row x̂ is the same thing: dx̂ = dbn.

You might expect LayerNorm's backward to just divide by σ, because the forward divided by σ. It doesn't, because every number in the row also moves the row's mean μ and its spread σ, and those feed back into all four outputs.

← scroll the sketch →

The four numbers of dx̂ for row 2 go through the formula one step at a time. Subtracting the mean (dashed line) makes the row add to zero. Subtracting x̂ · (−0.2827) removes the part that would stretch the row. Dividing by σ = 0.7813 rescales the result, because the forward pass divided by it.

The first subtraction: LayerNorm erased the mean. Add 0.5 to every number of x1 and LayerNorm 2's output changes by at most 1.1e-16, which is rounding noise. If shifting the whole row does nothing to the output, it does nothing to the loss. So the gradient can't point in the "shift everything" direction, and its four numbers must add to zero. Subtracting mean(dx̂) does exactly that. All three rows of the LN2 backward output add to 0.0000. Before the subtractions, dx̂'s rows added to −0.0558, −0.0628 and 1.3760.

The second subtraction: LayerNorm erased the spread. Make the row 1.5 times wider and the output barely moves (at most 6.0e-06, only because of ε). So the gradient also can't point in the "stretch the row" direction. That direction is x̂ itself: a wider row is the same row plus more of x̂. Subtracting x̂ · mean(dx̂ ⊙ x̂) removes it. Check: multiply the result by x̂ entry by entry and add, and every row gives 0.0000.

The gradient can't change what LayerNorm throws away, so LayerNorm's backward throws away the same two things: the mean and the spread.

What a naive backward gets wrong. Dividing by σ alone gives row 2 = [0.3075, −0.2043, 0.8993, 0.7586], which adds to 1.7611. It tells x1 to shift up, a move that can't change the loss, and it is wrong on every entry.

Why the highway matters: 0.371 instead of 3.52e-16

In the tiny model, the highway carried 0.6724 of dx1's size of 1.0042 for row 2, and the branch added a part of size 0.4706. Both parts matter in one block. Stack many blocks and the difference becomes the whole story.

Without the add, the gradient reaching the first layer is the product of every layer's slope above it. Here's what why.py measured at the input of a stack of tanh layers, width 64:

DepthWithout residualWith residual
41.79e-050.125
243.52e-160.371

With residuals, each layer's backward is "copy, plus the branch's part", so the copy reaches the bottom at full size however deep the stack is. The first layer of a 24-layer stack gets a gradient about 1015 times bigger with residuals than without. Without them it effectively doesn't train.

Raschka §4.4 shows the same effect his own way: he builds a small network of linear layers with GELU, runs it with and without shortcut connections (the book's name for residual adds), and prints the gradient at each layer. Without the shortcuts, the early layers' gradients are tiny.

Show what happened

Only the weights below x1 change. Wout, W2, W1 and LayerNorm 2's γ and β are exactly as before, because their gradients were finished before the gradient reached the +. Everything below gets less: the gradient on Wq shrinks from size 0.3341 to 0.0535, and on E from 1.0609 to 0.5332. The PyTorch check now reports a largest difference of 6.5e-01 instead of 5.6e-16. dx1 row 2 is now just the branch's part, [0.2543, −0.3208, 0.1939, −0.1274], which adds to zero. (Don't read anything into the "one update" line dropping to 0.8068: lr 1.0 overshoots with the true gradient, and a wrong, smaller gradient happens to overshoot less. Card 17 covers learning rates.)

At real scale

Tiny model: dx2 3 × 4 → du 3 × 8 → dW1 = bnᵀ du, (4 × 3)(3 × 8) = 4 × 8 → LayerNorm backward on rows of 4 → dx1 3 × 4. 1 block, 2 residual adds.

GPT-2 124M: for a 1,024-token sequence, dx2 is 1,024 × 768 and du is 1,024 × 3,072. dW1 = bnᵀ du is (768 × 1,024)(1,024 × 3,072) = 768 × 3,072: every token's product added into each entry. LayerNorm's backward takes its two means over 768 numbers per row. 12 blocks give 24 residual adds, each one a copy on the way back.

Qwen2.5-0.5B: for the 7-token prompt, dx2 is 7 × 896 and the hidden gradient is 7 × 4,864. RMSNorm never subtracted the mean, so its backward has only the second subtraction (the x̂ term). SwiGLU has two up-matrices, so the gradient splits into two hidden paths before it adds back. 24 layers give 48 residual adds.

Principle

Each operation passes back exactly what it let through on the way forward

A + let both inputs through at full strength, so it copies the gradient to both. GELU let each hidden unit through by its slope, so it scales each gradient by that slope. LayerNorm threw away the mean and the spread, so it removes them from the gradient too. Put a + around every block and the gradient always has a full-strength path to the bottom.

Buys you: a 24-layer stack whose first layer still gets a gradient of 0.371 instead of 3.52e-16, and a LayerNorm whose gradient never wastes effort on a shift or a stretch it would erase.

Measured in labs/why.py (depth table), labs/tiny.py (every row on this card) and labs/grad-ffn-norm-residual.py (the LayerNorm steps, row sums, shift test and naive backward)

Where this simplification breaks
  • The highway isn't the only path. The branch's part is added on top, and in the tiny model it's sizeable (0.4706 next to the highway's 0.6724 in row 2). The highway guarantees a floor, not the whole gradient.
  • The stream still grows, and the final LayerNorm scales it back. Its own backward divides by σ (1.3361, 1.7421, 0.7061 for the three rows of x2), so dx2 is already reshaped before it reaches this block. GPT-2 scales down the starting weights of each branch's last layer to slow that growth.
  • γ is 1 only at the start. After training, dx̂ = dy ⊙ γ, and LayerNorm also gets its own gradients: dγ (the sum over tokens of dy ⊙ x̂) and dβ (the sum over tokens of dy).
  • The depth table is a cartoon. It uses plain tanh layers with no norms and no training. It shows the direction and rough size of the effect, not a real transformer's numbers.
  • The bias gradient is a sum. dc1 is du summed over the 3 tokens (8 numbers) and dc2 is df summed over tokens. This card skips them because they follow the same linear-layer rule.

Check yourself

1. Why does the + in x2 = x1 + f give df exactly equal to dx2, and why is dx1 not equal to dx2?

Show the answer

Nudging x1 or f by some amount moves x2 by the same amount, so both slopes are 1 and both get a copy. But x1 is also the input to LayerNorm 2, so it gets a second gradient back through the branch, and the two add: dx1 = dx2 + [0.2543, −0.3208, 0.1939, −0.1274] for row 2.

2. u[2,1] = −1.6184 and its incoming gradient is −0.0871. Why does du[2,1] come out positive and small?

Show the answer

GELU's slope at −1.6184 is −0.1219: small, because the unit was nearly closed, and negative, because GELU dips below zero there. −0.0871 × −0.1219 = +0.0106.

3. Every row of LayerNorm's backward output adds to zero. What in the forward pass forces that?

Show the answer

LayerNorm subtracts the row's mean, so adding the same amount to all four inputs leaves the output unchanged (measured: 1.1e-16). A change that can't move the loss gets zero gradient, so the gradient has no "shift everything" part, which means its entries add to zero.

Reveal

It still adds to zero, because the mean subtraction comes after multiplying by γ, so it removes any shift whatever γ is. Its size doubles: with the same dbn and γ = 2, row 2 becomes [0.5086, −0.6416, 0.3878, −0.2548], exactly twice [0.2543, −0.3208, 0.1939, −0.1274]. A γ that differs per column would reshape the row, but the zero sum would still hold.
