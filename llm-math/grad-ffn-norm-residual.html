The residual is a gradient highway

Backprop through the residual add, GELU and LayerNorm · skip connection, shortcut connection, GELU′, LayerNorm backward · Raschka §4.4

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The first layers of a deep model learn only from the gradient that reaches them. Through a plain stack of 24 tanh layers that gradient arrives at size 3.52e-16, so the bottom layers barely train. The backward pass needs a path that doesn't shrink, and it needs correct rules for the GELU and LayerNorm it passes through.

Where it fits: Comes after the output head's gradient (cards 13 and 14), which delivers dx2dx_2dx2​. It runs the forward pass of the feed-forward half of the block in reverse: back through x2=x1+fx_2 = x_1 + fx2​=x1​+f, GELU and LayerNorm 2, reusing the linear-layer rule for W2W_2W2​ and W1W_1W1​.

The first layers of a deep model learn only from the gradient that reaches them, and a gradient that passes through many layers usually shrinks along the way. At every + in the block, the incoming gradient is copied back unchanged, so there's always one path that doesn't shrink. This card follows the gradient for the token sat backward through the feed-forward half of the block, using three rules: through the +, through GELU, and through LayerNorm.

Rule 1: a + copies the gradient to both of its inputs

In x2=x1+fx_2 = x_1 + fx2​=x1​+f, nudge one entry of x1x_1x1​ by 0.001 and that entry of x2x_2x2​ moves by exactly 0.001. The slope is 1. The same is true for fff. The chain rule multiplies the incoming gradient by that slope, so both inputs get an exact copy of dx2dx_2dx2​.

But x1x_1x1​ is used twice in the forward pass: once on the stream, and once as the input to LayerNorm 2 at the start of the branch. When a value feeds two places, its gradient is the sum of what comes back from each.

Rule 3: LayerNorm's backward takes out the mean and the spread

Next, dn=du W1⊤dn = du\, W_1^\topdn=duW1⊤​ (3×83 \times 83×8 times 8×48 \times 48×4) carries the gradient back to LayerNorm 2's output. At the start γ\gammaγ is 1, so the gradient on the normalised row x^\hat{x}x^ is the same thing: dx^=dnd\hat{x} = dndx^=dn.

You might expect LayerNorm's backward to just divide by σ\sigmaσ, because the forward divided by σ\sigmaσ. It doesn't, because every number in the row also moves the row's mean μ\muμ and its spread σ\sigmaσ, and those feed back into all four outputs.

The first subtraction: LayerNorm erased the mean. Add 0.5 to every number of x1x_1x1​ and LayerNorm 2's output changes by at most 1.1e-16, which is rounding noise. If shifting the whole row does nothing to the output, it does nothing to the loss. So the gradient can't point in the "shift everything" direction, and its four numbers must add to zero. Subtracting mean⁡(dx^)\operatorname{mean}(d\hat{x})mean(dx^) does exactly that. All three rows of the LN2 backward output add to 0.0000. Before the subtractions, the rows of dx^d\hat{x}dx^ added to −0.0558, −0.0628 and 1.3760.

The second subtraction: LayerNorm erased the spread. Make the row 1.5 times wider and the output barely moves (at most 6.0e-06, only because of ϵ\epsilonϵ). So the gradient also can't point in the "stretch the row" direction. That direction is x^\hat{x}x^ itself: a wider row is the same row plus more of x^\hat{x}x^. Subtracting x^⊙mean⁡(dx^⊙x^)\hat{x} \odot \operatorname{mean}(d\hat{x} \odot \hat{x})x^⊙mean(dx^⊙x^) removes it. Check: multiply the result by x^\hat{x}x^ entry by entry and add, and every row gives 0.0000.

The gradient can't change what LayerNorm throws away, so LayerNorm's backward throws away the same two things: the mean and the spread.

What a naive backward gets wrong. Dividing by σ\sigmaσ alone gives row 2 = [0.3075, −0.2043, 0.8993, 0.7586][0.3075,\ -0.2043,\allowbreak\ 0.8993,\ 0.7586][0.3075, −0.2043, 0.8993, 0.7586], which adds to 1.7611. It tells x1x_1x1​ to shift up, a move that can't change the loss, and it is wrong on every entry.

Why the highway matters: 0.371 instead of 3.52e-16

In the tiny model, the highway carried 0.6724 of dx1dx_1dx1​'s size of 1.0042 for row 2, and the branch added a part of size 0.4706. Both parts matter in one block. Stack many blocks and the difference becomes the whole story.

Without the add, the gradient reaching the first layer is the product of every layer's slope above it. Here's what why.py measured at the input of a stack of tanh layers, width 64:

DepthWithout residualWith residual
41.79e-050.125
243.52e-160.371

With residuals, each layer's backward is "copy, plus the branch's part", so the copy reaches the bottom at full size however deep the stack is. The first layer of a 24-layer stack gets a gradient about 101510^{15}1015 times bigger with residuals than without. Without them it effectively doesn't train.

Raschka §4.4 shows the same effect his own way: he builds a small network of linear layers with GELU, runs it with and without shortcut connections (the book's name for residual adds), and prints the gradient at each layer. Without the shortcuts, the early layers' gradients are tiny.

Change it

In your copy of llm-math/labs/tiny.py, close the highway. In backward, change

The forward pass is untouched, so the model and its loss are the same. Only the gradient is now wrong. Show what happened

Only the weights below x1x_1x1​ change. Wout\WoutWout​, W2W_2W2​, W1W_1W1​ and LayerNorm 2's γ\gammaγ and β\betaβ are exactly as before, because their gradients were finished before the gradient reached the +. At real scale

Tiny model: dx2dx_2dx2​ 3×43 \times 43×4 → dududu 3×83 \times 83×8 → dW1=n⊤dudW_1 = n^\top dudW1​=n⊤du, (4×3)(3×8)=4×8(4 \times 3)(3 \times 8) = 4 \times 8(4×3)(3×8)=4×8 → LayerNorm backward on rows of 4 → dx1dx_1dx1​ 3×43 \times 43×4. 1 block, 2 residual adds.

GPT-2 124M: for a 1,024-token sequence, dx2dx_2dx2​ is 1,024×7681{,}024 \times 7681,024×768 and dududu is 1,024×3,0721{,}024 \times 3{,}0721,024×3,072. dW1=n⊤dudW_1 = n^\top dudW1​=n⊤du is (768×1,024)(1,024×3,072)=768×3,072(768 \times 1{,}024)(1{,}024 \times 3{,}072) = 768 \times 3{,}072(768×1,024)(1,024×3,072)=768×3,072: every token's product added into each entry. LayerNorm's backward takes its two means over 768 numbers per row. 12 blocks give 24 residual adds, each one a copy on the way back.

Qwen2.5-0.5B: for the 7-token prompt, dx2dx_2dx2​ is 7×8967 \times 8967×896 and the hidden gradient is 7×4,8647 \times 4{,}8647×4,864. RMSNorm never subtracted the mean, so its backward has only the second subtraction (the x^\hat{x}x^ term). SwiGLU has two up-matrices, so the gradient splits into two hidden paths before it adds back. 24 layers give 48 residual adds.

Principle

Each operation passes back exactly what it let through on the way forward

A + let both inputs through at full strength, so it copies the gradient to both. GELU let each hidden unit through by its slope, so it scales each gradient by that slope. LayerNorm threw away the mean and the spread, so it removes them from the gradient too. Put a + around every block and the gradient always has a full-strength path to the bottom.

Buys you: a 24-layer stack whose first layer still gets a gradient of 0.371 instead of 3.52e-16, and a LayerNorm whose gradient never wastes effort on a shift or a stretch it would erase.

Measured in labs/why.py (depth table), labs/tiny.py (every row on this card) and labs/grad-ffn-norm-residual.py (the LayerNorm steps, row sums, shift test and naive backward)

Where this simplification breaks
  • The highway isn't the only path. The branch's part is added on top, and in the tiny model it's sizeable (0.4706 next to the highway's 0.6724 in row 2). The highway guarantees a floor, not the whole gradient.
  • The stream still grows, and the final LayerNorm scales it back. Its own backward divides by σ\sigmaσ (1.3361, 1.7421, 0.7061 for the three rows of x2x_2x2​), so dx2dx_2dx2​ is already reshaped before it reaches this block. GPT-2 scales down the starting weights of each branch's last layer to slow that growth.
  • γ\gammaγ is 1 only at the start. After training, dx^=dy⊙γd\hat{x} = dy \odot \gammadx^=dy⊙γ, and LayerNorm also gets its own gradients: dγd\gammadγ (the sum over tokens of dy⊙x^dy \odot \hat{x}dy⊙x^) and dβd\betadβ (the sum over tokens of dydydy).
  • The depth table is a cartoon. It uses plain tanh layers with no norms and no training. It shows the direction and rough size of the effect, not a real transformer's numbers.
  • The bias gradient is a sum. dc1dc_1dc1​ is dududu summed over the 3 tokens (8 numbers) and dc2dc_2dc2​ is dfdfdf summed over tokens. This card skips them because they follow the same linear-layer rule.

3. Every row of LayerNorm's backward output adds to zero. What in the forward pass forces that?

Show the answer

LayerNorm subtracts the row's mean, so adding the same amount to all four inputs leaves the output unchanged (measured: 1.1e-16). A change that can't move the loss gets zero gradient, so the gradient has no "shift everything" part, which means its entries add to zero.
