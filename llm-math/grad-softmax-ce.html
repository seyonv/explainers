The gradient at the top is p − y

softmax + cross-entropy backward, gradient of the logits, dlogits, dz · Raschka §5.1.2, A.4

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The loss is one number, 2.2067, but there are 220 weights. Backprop has to start somewhere, and its first step, through softmax and −log, looks like it needs a 5 × 5 table of slopes for every position.

Where it fits: The first backward card. It starts from the loss built on the logits-and-loss card and the softmax from card 7, and hands dz (3 × 5) down to the output head on the next card.

  2. Where exactly does the 1/p from −log meet a p from softmax, and what is left after they cancel?
  3. Why does the right word get pushed by 1 − p while each wrong word gets pushed by its own share?
  4. Why must each row of dz add to 0, and how is that the same fact as softmax ignoring a shift of the whole row?

The forward pass ended in one number, the loss 2.2067. To make it smaller, every weight needs to know which way to move, and backprop answers that by starting at the top. The first gradient, on the logits, looks like it needs a 5 × 5 table of softmax slopes. It comes out as a subtraction instead: the model's probabilities minus the right answer.

Try this first

Guess first: at the last position the loss reads only one probability, the share for the right word on (0.0673). So which of the five logits in that row get a gradient that isn't zero? And which one gets pushed down hardest?

Show the answer

All five. Every logit sits in softmax's total, so moving any of them changes p[on]. The hardest push down goes to the confident wrong guess, mat, with a gradient of +0.2468. The right word gets −0.3109, the only negative entry, which means "raise me". The three small words get small pushes: 0.0359, 0.0128, 0.0155.

Where this sits: Part 2 starts at the top and walks back down

Part 2 is the backward pass. Every card in it uses one piece of notation. A gradient is written dX, which means ∂L/∂X: how much the loss L changes per unit of nudge to each entry of X. dX always has the same shape as X, one slope per number.

Backprop visits the forward stops in reverse. The forward pass went embeddings → attention → FFN → h → z → p → L. The backward pass starts at L and goes back down the same road, one stop per card.

This stop: L (one number) and p (3 × 5) come in, along with the targets cat sat on. Out comes dz, 3 × 5, the same shape as the logits. Next stop (the next card): dz flows through z = h · Wout to give dWout (4 × 5) and dh (3 × 4).

Deriving it for one row: a Jacobian, a log, and a cancellation

Take row 2 on its own. Its loss is L2 = −ln pon, and the mean will divide it by 3 at the end. The chain is z → p → L2, so the chain rule needs two pieces: how p moves when z moves, and how L2 moves when p moves.

Step 1, the softmax Jacobian. A Jacobian is the table of every output's slope with respect to every input. Softmax has 5 inputs and 5 outputs, so it is 5 × 5. Each share is pj = ezj / Σ ezk. Moving zj itself raises the top and the bottom. Moving any other zi only raises the bottom. Working that through with the quotient rule gives one formula for both cases:

∂pj∂zi=pj(δij−pi)

plain text: J = diag(p) - outer(p, p) # J[j, i] = dp_j / dz_i

pj, pi
two entries of the same row of p (1 × 5)
zi
one logit in that row
δij
1 when i = j, 0 otherwise (the "Kronecker delta")
J
the Jacobian, 5 × 5: row j says how pj moves as each logit moves

Two entries of the on row: ∂pon/∂zon = 0.0673 × (1 − 0.0673) = 0.0628, and ∂pon/∂zmat = 0.0673 × (0 − 0.7404) = −0.0498. Raising your own logit raises your share; raising a rival's lowers it.

Step 2, the −ln. The slope of −ln p is −1/p. At the right word that is −1 / 0.0673 = −14.8579: a small share means a steep loss.

Step 3, multiply, and watch pon cancel. The chain rule says ∂L2/∂zi = (−1/pon) × ∂pon/∂zi:

All five: [0.1076, 0.0384, 0.0464, −0.9327, 0.7404] = p − y. Step 4: divide by 3 for the mean, giving dz row 2.

In plain words: raise the right word, lower each wrong one by its share

A training step moves each logit against its gradient. So p − y reads as two rules:

  • The right word gets pushed up by 1 − p. At position 2 that is 1 − 0.0673 = 0.9327, almost the full amount, because the model nearly missed it.
  • Each wrong word gets pushed down by its own share p. mat took 0.7404, so it gets the biggest push. cat took 0.0384 and is barely touched.

A word the model already gets right costs nothing to fix. If pon were 1, every other p would be 0 and the whole row of p − y would be 0.

Each row of dz adds to 0. p adds to 1 and y adds to 1, so p − y adds to 1 − 1 = 0. On the rounded numbers:

The ±0.0001 is rounding to 4 places. Unrounded, the lab prints sums of 0.0e+00, −1.2e-17, 2.8e-17: zero, to the last bit a float keeps. This ties back to the softmax card: adding the same number to every logit in a row leaves p unchanged. So the loss can't change in that direction, and the gradient must have no part along it. The lab checks this directly. Adding 1.0 to all of row 2's logits changes the loss by 0.0e+00. The gradient only reshuffles a row's scores; it never lifts them all.

Check it with a nudge

A derivative is a nudge ratio: change one input a little and divide the change in the loss by the nudge. Do that straight on the logits. Add 0.001 to z[2, on] (row 2, column 3), keep everything else fixed, and recompute the loss.

Raising on's logit lowers the loss, and raising mat's raises it, by exactly the amounts dz predicts. The bigger nudge of 0.1 is a little off (−0.3098), because the slope changes over that distance. PyTorch agrees too: calling cross_entropy on the same logits and running .backward() gives a dz that differs from (p − y)/3 by at most 5.6e-17.

Why it's this clean: the log undoes the exp

Softmax builds p out of exponentials, and the loss takes a log. A log of an exp gives back what went in. Write the loss straight from the logits and the softmax fraction falls apart into two simple pieces:

Lt=−lnezy∑kezk=ln∑kezk−zy

plain text: L_t = logsumexp(z[t]) - z[t, target[t]]

zy
the right word's logit in this row, one number
ln Σk ezk
the logsumexp of the row, one number. Its slope with respect to zi is ezi / Σ ezk = pi
Filled in, row 2: 2.7254 − 0.0269 = 2.6985, the same loss as −ln 0.0673. Now take the slope of each piece. The logsumexp gives pi. The −zy gives −1 at the right word and 0 elsewhere, which is −yi. Together: p − y, in two lines.

That's also why frameworks fuse the two into one call. PyTorch's cross_entropy takes the logits, not p. It computes logsumexp − zy going forward, and p − y going back. It never forms p and then takes its log.

The separate route breaks once the logits get large. The lab multiplies row 2's logits by 50, in float32. Now mat leads on by 119.90:

  • Separate, softmax then −log: pon = e−119.9 is too small for a float32 and rounds to 0.000e+00. The loss is inf and every gradient is nan. One step like that ruins every weight.
  • Fused, cross_entropy: the loss is 119.8952 and the gradient is [0, 0, 0, −1, 1]. That is p − y with p ≈ [0, 0, 0, 0, 1]: lower mat, raise on.

Fusing softmax and −log is the same math with fewer ways to fail: no tiny p to underflow, and a gradient that is a plain subtraction. Raschka's §5.1.2 does the same: after working the loss out by hand from the probabilities, it hands the raw logits, not the probabilities, to PyTorch's cross_entropy. A.4 shows how autograd fills in .grad from there.

Show what happened

dz row 2 became [0.0359, 0.0128, 0.0155, 0.0224, −0.0865]. The first three didn't change at all, since p didn't change. on is now a wrong word, so it gets 0.0673 / 3 = 0.0224. mat is the answer and gets (0.7404 − 1) / 3 = −0.0865, about a third of the old biggest push. The model was already mostly right, so there is less to fix. Rows 0 and 1 are unchanged. The loss dropped to 1.4074, and numpy and PyTorch still agree.

At real scale. The formula doesn't change, only the width of each row. GPT-2 124M: for one 1,024-token sequence, z and dz are both 1,024 × 50,257. Every row of dz has one negative entry (the target) and 50,256 positive ones, and it adds to 0. With a batch, the mean runs over every position in every sequence, so the divisor is batch size × 1,024, not 3. Qwen2.5-0.5B: on the 7-token prompt, z and dz are 7 × 151,936, divided by 7. The Jacobian route would need a 151,936 × 151,936 table per row. p − y needs one subtraction per entry.

Principle

Pair the output function with the loss that undoes it, and the gradient becomes "prediction minus target"

Softmax's exp and the loss's log cancel. What's left is a gradient that says, word by word, how far the prediction was from the answer. The error p − y is the signal that every weight below will share.

Buys you: a gradient that is exact, costs one subtraction per entry, can't blow up (every entry lies between −1 and 1), and stays well-behaved for huge logits once softmax and log are fused.

Raschka §5.1.2, A.4

Where this simplification breaks
  • The divisor is the number of positions counted. Here it is 3. Real code divides by every position in the batch, and positions marked to be ignored (such as padding, via ignore_index) get a gradient of 0 and don't count in the divisor.
  • y doesn't have to be one-hot. With label smoothing, y spreads a little weight over every word. The gradient is still p − y with that softer y, so it reaches 0 when p matches the soft target, and pushes back if the model grows more confident than that.
  • It's clean only for this pair. Softmax followed by some other loss (1 − p, say, from the last card) needs the full Jacobian, and nothing cancels. Its push on the right logit at position 2 was −0.0628 instead of −0.9327.
  • Fusing fixes the arithmetic, not the model. In the ×50 test the fused loss was 119.8952, but its gradient was only [0, 0, 0, −1, 1]. Every entry of p − y lies between −1 and 1, so a confidently wrong row gets a push of at most 1 on any logit, however large its loss. The loss grows with the gap; the gradient doesn't.

Check yourself

1. The loss reads only p[on]. Use the chain rule to say, in one sentence, why mat's logit still gets a gradient of 0.2468.

Show the answer

z[mat] is in softmax's total, so raising it lowers p[on] (the Jacobian entry is −0.0673 × 0.7404). Times the log's slope −1/0.0673 that gives 0.7404, and dividing by 3 gives 0.2468.

2. Why must every row of dz add to 0, and what does that have to do with the softmax card's max trick?

Show the answer

p and y each add to 1, so p − y adds to 0. The max trick works because subtracting the same number from every logit leaves p unchanged. So the loss has zero slope in that direction, and the gradient has no part along it. The lab added 1.0 to all of row 2 and the loss changed by 0.0e+00.

3. Why does PyTorch's cross_entropy take logits instead of probabilities?

Show the answer

It computes logsumexp(z) − z[target] directly, so it never forms a tiny p and logs it. With row 2's logits ×50 in float32, the separate route gave p[on] = 0, loss inf and nan gradients, while the fused call gave 119.8952 and the clean gradient [0, 0, 0, −1, 1].
