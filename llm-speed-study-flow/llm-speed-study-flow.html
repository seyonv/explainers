Study path · Day 1 · one page

LLM speed study plan

The order to read the LLM speed and reliability course in, primed phase by phase, with deeper sources under every card for when a card isn't enough. Everything is inline: just scroll. Ticks are shared with the checklist version.

What today uses, and why

Day 1 starts from what you can see as a user, the wait for an answer, and works inward. It needs no ML or GPU background. Every later day explains one term of the formula you learn today.

LLM speed and reliability course24 cards, one per thing that makes a response slow or unreliable.
The backbone of today. It starts from the request and its wait, so you can follow it without knowing how a model works, and it gives you the formula the later days zoom into.
The Day 1 drawingOne sketch from training to your screen.
A map to hang each card on. Its numbered boxes say which card explains which part, and Day 1.5 fills in the lettered ones.
Serving and Agents field guides: the principles15 one-line rules each, from the field guides' Serving and Agents tracks.
You skim the Serving rules first to see the whole field, and check yourself against the Agents rules at the end. They turn today's numbers into decisions real teams make.
Horace He, “Making Deep Learning Go Brrrr”A 20-minute blog post.
The clearest explanation of why memory, not maths, caps how fast a model writes. It's the one outside read not to skip today; it appears right after the memory-bandwidth card.
Three checkpointsOne question set per phase.
You try each one cold first, so you know what to look for, then for real, to find which cards to reread.
By the end of today, you'll be able to
  • Break any LLM response time into time to first token + tokens ÷ speed, repeated for every call, and say which term dominates.
  • Explain why writing tokens is capped by memory bandwidth, and how hardware, quantization, MoE and speculative decoding each get around it.
  • Say when prompt caching helps a lot and when it barely matters.
  • Explain why an agent takes minutes rather than seconds: thinking tokens, many calls and a growing history.
  • Reason about time to a correct result: tail latency, retries, compounding errors, parallel calls and model routing.
  • Rank the levers for a real request: cut tokens first, pick the host second, buy hardware last.
How to use this page
  1. Scroll. The bar pinned at the top always says what the current step is and how to read it: Skim Read Pretest Checkpoint Optional Task.

  4. Tick Done as you finish a step. Take a break after each checkpoint.
  5. “Go deeper” sources stay closed. Open them only for a card whose questions you missed; Show here loads one inline.

Phase 1 · What sets the speed of one call

~2 h · 0 / 12

Metrics first, then the root cause (memory bandwidth), then four ways around it, then a shorter start.

Before you read · 2 steps

1 · Prime · 2 min. Read this list of what each card will let you do. Don't open the cards yet.

  • Time to first token. Given a setup, name which part of the time to first token dominates and which change would cut it.
  • Output speed. Compute writing time from speed and length, and say what a host's output speed number does and doesn't tell you.
  • Output length. Predict how much a shorter answer saves on a given setup, and recognise when the prompt, not the answer, is the real cost.
  • Memory-bandwidth-bound decoding. Estimate a model's token-speed ceiling on a given chip, and say which lever raises it.
  • Inference hardware. Place any chip on the bandwidth ladder and predict roughly how fast a model can write on it, and why a host may deliver much less than that.
  • Quantization. Choose a quantization level for a use case, and predict roughly what it does to size, speed and quality.
  • Mixture of experts. Predict an MoE model's speed ceiling and memory need from its total and active parameters, and decide when it's the right pick.
  • Speculative decoding. Estimate the speed-up for a given acceptance rate and draft cost, and say when speculative decoding helps and when it hurts.
  • Prefill and prompt caching. Structure prompts so they hit the cache, and predict when caching helps a lot, a little, or costs extra.
  • Same model, different host. Explain why two hosts of the same model differ in speed, and pick a host with the right numbers in hand.
