Agents study plan · Day 3

Did it work? Evals and trajectories

Day 3 of the agents study path: how to tell whether a change to an agent actually helped. Fixed tasks, hidden tests, repeats and noise, then reading the runs themselves to see why they failed and what each success cost. It follows stations 6 and 7 of the Agents field guide. Tick things off as you go; your progress stays in this browser.

By the end of all of this, you'll be able to
  • Design a small agent eval: fixed tasks, a grader the agent can't see, and repeated runs.
  • Say why one run proves nothing, and give a pass rate with an honest interval.
  • Explain what a benchmark score can measure besides skill: memorised answers, flaky infrastructure, or a leaky grader.
  • Read a trajectory, put each failure in a category, and pick what to build next from the counts.
  • Work out cost per solved task, and why it can favour the more expensive model.
The same routine every phase
  1. Prime (5 min): read the phase's questions below its intro. Nothing else yet.
  2. Pretest: try the phase's checkpoint cold, without opening the answers.
  3. Read in order, with the questions in mind. The phase opens with a card from this hub; the readings after it open in new tabs.
  4. Take the checkpoint for real. For each miss, reopen only the source its answer points to.

  8. Checkpoint: evalsFrom the Agents guide's closed-book check for station 6. Take a break after.

    1. At an 80% per-run success rate, compute pass@3 and pass^3, and say which one a person who runs the agent daily lives with.

      Show the answer
      • pass@3 = 1 − 0.2³ = 99.2%: any of 3 tries succeeds
      • pass^3 = 0.8³ = 51.2%: all 3 succeed
      • A daily user lives with pass^k, so report it next to pass@k and list the tasks that flip between repeats

      Missed it? Agents principle 13 · Demystifying evals for AI agents · Compounding errors in agent loops

    2. Your intervals are too wide at 40 tasks × 3 repeats. Do you add repeats or tasks, and why?

      Show the answer
      • Repeats reduce only the within-task sampling noise, and Miller puts the ceiling at 2/3 of that variance
      • Differences between tasks dominate a task-clustered interval, and only more tasks shrink them
      • So add tasks before repeats, and keep 3 repeats for pass^3 and flip counts

      Missed it? Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations · Agents failure F14 · Binomial and beta-binomial

    3. Your agent scores far higher on a SWE-bench Verified slice than on your own tasks of the same size. What besides skill can that score be measuring, and what does your own task set do about each?

      Show the answer
      • Memory: the issues and fixes are public and in training data, so the model can name the buggy file from the issue alone (F13)
      • Broken instances: some tasks fail with their own gold patch or have flawed tests, so no agent can pass them (F12)
      • Your own planted or post-cutoff bugs with hidden tests, each validated with its gold patch (passes) and an empty patch (fails); keep the public slice only as a sanity check (principle 14)

    1. Your failed runs split 18 wrong fix, 9 edit cascade, 6 loop, 4 budget exhausted and 3 infra. Which capability do you build next, and what happens to the infra runs?

    2. From the pricing notes: Haiku 4.5 solves 4 of 10 at about $0.25 a run, Sonnet 5 solves 7 of 10 at about $0.49. Which is cheaper per resolved task, and what could reverse that?

      Show the answer
      • Haiku: $0.25 ÷ 0.4 = $0.62; Sonnet 5: $0.49 ÷ 0.7 = $0.70, so Haiku is slightly cheaper per resolved task
      • A cache that silently fails on Haiku (its 4,096-token minimum) nearly triples its per-task cost (F20)
      • Retries, the value of the tasks only Sonnet solves, and the wide interval on 10 tasks can all reverse it
