The transformer, explained at a party

Also called: self-attention, scaled dot-product attention, the transformer block. This card follows Dr. Swarnendu Bhattacharya's "finding love at a party" example (LinkedIn, Oct 2025) and uses his three guests and his numbers. It writes every step as an equation, explains each symbol, and then carries the same party through the rest of a transformer block: masking, position, multiple heads, residuals, layer norm and the feed-forward layer.

In one breath

A transformer lets every word look at every other word at the same time. Each word decides how much each other word matters to it (attention weights that add up to 1), takes that weighted blend of what the others offer, and then thinks it over on its own. Stack that a few dozen times and you have GPT.

1The problem: remembering guests one by one

You walk into a party with 50 people. You meet them one at a time and keep a single running impression in your head. Each new person overwrites a bit of the last one, so by guest 50, guest 1 is a blur, even if they were the one.

That is a recurrent neural network (RNN). It reads words in order and squeezes everything so far into one fixed-size memory vector:

ht=tanh⁡ ⁣(Wh ht−1+Wx xt+b) h_t = \tanh\!\big(W_h\, h_{t-1} + W_x\, x_t + b\big) ht​=tanh(Wh​ht−1​+Wx​xt​+b)
ht∈Rdh_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d}ht​∈Rd
Your impression after meeting guest ttt. Its size ddd is the same after 1 guest or 50, and that fixed size is the problem.
ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​
Your impression before this guest. Everything you know about guests 1…t−11\dots t{-}11…t−1 has to fit in here.
xtx_txt​
The guest you're meeting right now.
Wh, Wx, bW_h,\ W_x,\ bWh​, Wx​, b
Learned weights for how much to keep of the old impression and how much to add of the new person.
tanh⁡\tanhtanh
Squashes each entry into (−1,1)(-1,1)(−1,1). Old information fades a little with every step.

Two failures follow. First, compression: nnn guests have to fit in ddd numbers. Second, distance: for guest 1 to affect your decision at guest 50, the signal has to pass through 49 steps, and during training the gradient has to travel all the way back through them too. What we want instead is to see everyone at once and focus on the people who matter. That is attention.

4Three views: query, key, value

Comparing raw personalities isn't quite right. What you're looking for (your query) is different from how someone comes across (their key), which is different again from what they actually bring to your life (their value). Each is a different view of the same person.

Each view is a learned matrix that projects a person's ddd traits into a new space:

q=xyou⊤WQ,K=X WK,V=X WV \hl{q} = x_{\text{you}}^{\top} W_Q, \qquad K = X\,W_K, \qquad V = X\,W_V q=xyou⊤​WQ​,K=XWK​,V=XWV​
WQ∈Rd×dkW_Q \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}WQ​∈Rd×dk​
Query projection. Turns your traits into "what I'm looking for." Learned in training.
WK∈Rd×dkW_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_k}WK​∈Rd×dk​
Key projection. Turns each guest into a dating profile, the face they show. It must land in the same dkd_kdk​-dimensional space as queries so the two can be compared.
WV∈Rd×dvW_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_v}WV​∈Rd×dv​
Value projection. What a guest actually passes on to you once you pay attention to them.
dkd_kdk​
Size of the "compatibility space." Here it's 2, and in GPT-2 it's 64 per head. Usually it's smaller than ddd, so matching happens in a compact space.

Why not one shared projection? If query and key were the same, attraction would always be mutual and you would always be most drawn to yourself. Separate matrices let "funny people like kind people" be one-directional. Separating value from key lets someone who presents modestly still pass on a lot.

6Why divide by dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​

Suppose each entry of qqq and kkk is random with mean 0 and variance 1. Then each product qikiq_i k_iqi​ki​ has variance 1, and a dot product adds up dkd_kdk​ of them:

Var⁡(q⋅k)=∑i=1dkVar⁡(qiki)=dk⟹std⁡(q⋅k)=dk \operatorname{Var}(q\cdot k) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) = d_k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{std}(q\cdot k) = \sqrt{d_k} Var(q⋅k)=i=1∑dk​​Var(qi​ki​)=dk​⟹std(q⋅k)=dk​​
Var⁡\operatorname{Var}Var
How spread out the scores are. With dk=64d_k = 64dk​=64 the typical score is around ±8, so scores get larger just because there are more dimensions.
dk\sqrt{d_k}dk​​
Dividing by the standard deviation brings scores back to about ±1, whatever the head size.
s~=sdk=[ 2.919, 2.592, 1.936 ]2=[ 2.064, 1.833, 1.369 ] \tilde s = \frac{s}{\sqrt{d_k}} = \frac{[\,2.919,\ 2.592,\ 1.936\,]}{\sqrt{2}} = [\,\hl{2.064},\ 1.833,\ 1.369\,] s~=dk​​s​=2​[2.919, 2.592, 1.936]​=[2.064, 1.833, 1.369]

Why it matters: softmax (next step) turns large gaps into near-certainty. If the same scaled scores were 4× larger, Alice would get 0.686 and Carol 0.042. That is close to all-or-nothing, and once softmax saturates its gradient is nearly zero, so the model stops learning which guests to look at. In party terms: without the scaling, a big room makes you fixate on one person before you've really looked.

9Everyone at once: the matrix form

So far only you have been looking. In self-attention, every person in the room, you included, sends a query to every person, also you included. Stack all the queries into a matrix and steps 4 to 8 become a single line:

10The causal mask: no peeking ahead

Say guests arrive in order: Alice, Bob, Carol, then you. When each person forms an impression, they can only take in people who have already arrived. Nobody can be influenced by someone who hasn't walked in yet.

A model that generates text works the same way: when it predicts word ttt, it can't see words after ttt. We enforce that by adding −∞-\infty−∞ to every score above the diagonal before the softmax:

softmax⁡ ⁣(QK⊤dk+M),Mij={0j≤i−∞j>i \softmax\!\left( \frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}} + M \right), \qquad M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & j \le i \\ -\infty & j > i \end{cases} softmax(dk​​QK⊤​+M),Mij​={0−∞​j≤ij>i​
MMM
The mask: 0 for people who have arrived, −∞-\infty−∞ for people who haven't.
e−∞=0e^{-\infty} = 0e−∞=0
A future guest gets exactly zero weight, and the softmax shares the attention among the people who are there.

11Position: who arrived when

Look at step 9 again. Nothing in it uses order. Shuffle the rows of XXX and the outputs get shuffled the same way, with every number unchanged. Attention is permutation-equivariant:

Attention⁡(PX)=P Attention⁡(X)for any permutation matrix P \operatorname{Attention}(PX) = P\,\operatorname{Attention}(X) \quad \text{for any permutation matrix } P Attention(PX)=PAttention(X)for any permutation matrix P
PPP
A matrix that reorders rows (people). Without position information, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" contain the same set of people and look the same to attention.

The fix is to give each guest a name tag for their arrival slot before the party starts, by adding a position vector to each person. The original transformer used sine waves at different frequencies:

xt←xt+pt,pt, 2i=sin⁡ ⁣(t100002i/d),pt, 2i+1=cos⁡ ⁣(t100002i/d) x_t \leftarrow x_t + p_t, \qquad p_{t,\,2i} = \sin\!\Big(\frac{t}{10000^{2i/d}}\Big), \quad p_{t,\,2i+1} = \cos\!\Big(\frac{t}{10000^{2i/d}}\Big) xt​←xt​+pt​,pt,2i​=sin(100002i/dt​),pt,2i+1​=cos(100002i/dt​)
ttt
Position in the sequence (arrival order).
iii
Which pair of dimensions. Low iii is a fast-ticking clock and high iii a slow one, so together they give every position a unique pattern, like a clock's hands.
sin⁡,cos⁡\sin,\cossin,cos pair
Moving forward by kkk positions is a fixed rotation of each pair, so "3 places earlier" looks the same anywhere in the sentence.

Modern models (Llama, Qwen) use RoPE instead. It rotates qqq and kkk by an angle proportional to position, so the score qi⋅kjq_i\cdot k_jqi​⋅kj​ depends on how far apart the two guests arrived, i−ji-ji−j, not on when either of them arrived.

NNN layers
The party runs again and again. Each round, people mingle with guests who have themselves already changed. Early layers tend to pick up local grammar, and later layers more abstract meaning. GPT-2 small has N=12N = 12N=12.

After the last block, the final person's vector is multiplied by WoutW_{\text{out}}Wout​ (one score per word in the vocabulary), and a softmax over those scores picks the next word, which then joins the party as a new guest.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Meet them one by one (RNN)

One fixed memory vector for the whole room. Early guests fade, a signal from guest 1 has to pass through 49 steps, and step ttt can't start until t−1t-1t−1 is done, so it can't run in parallel.

✗ Average everyone equally

A plain mean of all the guests (bag of words). It ignores compatibility entirely, so your soulmate and a stranger count the same, and the result is the same for every listener.

✗ Only talk to the best match

Hard attention: take the arg-max and ignore everyone else. You lose everything Bob and Carol had to offer, and arg-max has no gradient, so the model can't learn whom to pick.

✓ Soft attention

Every guest gets a weight learned from compatibility, the weights add up to 1, and everything stays differentiable. Any guest is one step away, and the whole room is computed in parallel as one matrix multiply.

Is the party picture the whole truth?

Almost, with four corrections. First, the weights aren't hand-picked: WQ,WK,WV,WO,W1,W2W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_O, W_1, W_2WQ​,WK​,WV​,WO​,W1​,W2​ all start random and are learned by gradient descent on next-word prediction. The numbers here are illustrative. Second, in self-attention there is no separate "you": every token is a guest and a seeker at the same time (step 9). Third, the convex-hull limit applies to one head's output only. The residual connection, WOW_OWO​ and the FFN move the vector well outside the hull, so it is not a guard against hallucination, despite what the source suggests. Fourth, the dimensions don't have neat labels like "humor": learned features are spread across many dimensions.

When attention isn't the right tool. The n2n^2n2 score grid gets expensive for very long inputs (a 100k-token context has 101010^{10}1010 pairs per head per layer), and generating text means caching every past key and value. For long sequences people use FlashAttention (same math, done more efficiently), sliding-window or sparse attention, or a different design entirely, such as state-space models (Mamba) and linear-attention RNNs that go back to a fixed memory. For small tabular data, gradient-boosted trees usually beat a transformer.
