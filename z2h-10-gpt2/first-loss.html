Batches, targets and the 10.82 sanity check

Also called: next-token targets, labels shifted by one, the loss at initialization, the expected initial loss. Lecture 9 · let's train: data batches (B,T) → logits (B,T,C) · 0:45:50 and cross entropy loss · 0:52:53

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A model that only produces logits can't be trained: there is no single number that says how wrong it is, and no labelled answers to compare against. Without a quick check at step 0 you also can't tell a broken initialization from a slow start.

Where it fits: You can already load GPT-2 and get logits of shape (B, T, 50257). This card turns raw text into inputs and targets, adds the loss to forward, and reuses the expected-initial-loss idea from the batchnorm course at GPT-2's vocabulary size.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why does the buffer need B·T + 1 tokens, and where does row 0's last target come from?
  2. How does one row of T tokens turn into T separate next-token questions rather than one?
  3. Why must the logits be flattened to (B·T, 50257) before F.cross_entropy, and what does the −1 in view(-1, ...) work out?
  4. Why should an untrained GPT-2 score about ln 50,257, and why does it land a little above it (10.96)?
  5. Which bugs would the 10.82 check catch, and which would sail straight past it?

In one breath: to train GPT-2 you cut a stretch of tokens into a grid of B rows by T columns (the inputs x), and use the same stretch moved over by one token as the answers (the targets y). Every one of the B·T positions is a "guess the next token" question. The loss averages how surprised the model was over all of them, and before any training that average should be about ln 50,257 = 10.82, because an untrained model should spread its bets evenly.

Predict first: a freshly initialized GPT-2 hasn't learned anything yet. Before a single training step, what loss should it give on a batch: about 1, about 5, about 11, or about 50?

About 11. A model that knows nothing should give every one of the 50,257 tokens roughly the same probability, 1/50,257. The loss is −ln of the probability it gave the right token, so it starts near −ln(1/50,257) = ln 50,257 = 10.82. Your M3 measures 10.96 for the lab's batch. A number far from this (say 25) would mean the initialization is broken.

One buffer, two views

A token is one piece of text from the GPT-2 tokenizer, stored as an integer id between 0 and 50,256. Karpathy tokenizes the start of tiny shakespeare (a 1 MB text file of Shakespeare's plays), then takes B·T + 1 tokens in one buffer. B is the batch size (how many rows, trained side by side) and T is the sequence length (how many positions per row). The inputs are the buffer without its last token; the targets are the buffer without its first token. Here is his 4 × 6 teaching example (play.ipynb), with each token's text above its id:

x[0]First5962·Citizen22307:25↵198Before8421·we356 y[0]·Citizen22307:25↵198Before8421·we356·proceed5120 x[1]·proceed5120·any597·further2252,11·hear3285·me502

· = a leading space, ↵ = a newline (both are part of the token). Green = the answer the model is graded on. Read down a column: after "First" comes " Citizen", after "First Citizen" comes ":", and so on.

Two details make this work. First, the last target of row 0 (" proceed") is the first input of row 1, so every row is fully labelled; that is why the buffer needs one extra token (4 × 6 + 1 = 25). Second, .view(B, T) only reshapes: it relabels the same 24 numbers as a 4 × 6 grid without copying them. The full batch, as the lab prints it:

x = [[5962, 22307, 25, 198, 8421, 356], [5120, 597, 2252, 11, 3285, 502], [2740, 13, 198, 198, 3237, 25], [198, 5248, 461, 11, 2740, 13]] y = [[22307, 25, 198, 8421, 356, 5120], [597, 2252, 11, 3285, 502, 2740], [13, 198, 198, 3237, 25, 198], [5248, 461, 11, 2740, 13, 198]]

Because attention is causal (position t can only look at positions 0 … t), the model's output at every column is a fair guess at that column's target. So one row of T tokens gives T training examples, not one.

From (B, T) to one number

The model returns logits: for every position, one raw score per vocabulary token, so the shape is (B, T, 50257). Softmax turns a row of logits into probabilities (exponentiate, then divide by the sum). PyTorch's F.cross_entropy does that softmax and the −ln in one call, but it expects a 2-D table of scores (N, C) and a 1-D list of N correct class ids. So the forward pass flattens both: logits.view(-1, 50257) gives (128, 50257) and targets.view(-1) gives (128,). The −1 tells PyTorch "work this dimension out": 4 × 32 = 128.

expected at init = ln 50,257 = 10.8249 measured (lab) = mean of 128 values of −ln p = 10.9600 mean p(right) = 2.01e-05 vs uniform 1/50,257 = 1.99e-05 why a bit above : logits at init have std 0.555 (computed), and ln V + std²/2 = 10.8249 + 0.1538 = 10.979 (approx.)

The last line is a rule of thumb for small random logits, not from the video: slightly uneven scores make the right token a little less likely on average, which nudges the loss above ln V. Karpathy expected about 10.82 and saw "something around 11" (0:55:49); his model then still had PyTorch's default init.

▶ Build itruns on your M3 · about 3 s

When: pause the video at 0:45:50 for the batch, and at 0:52:53 for the loss. First add targets to GPT.forward:

# end of GPT.forward(self, idx, targets=None)
logits = self.lm_head(x)   # (B, T, vocab_size)
loss = None
if targets is not None:
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, logits.size(-1)),
                           targets.view(-1))
return logits, loss

Then build one batch from tiny shakespeare and call it:

enc = tiktoken.get_encoding('gpt2')
tokens = enc.encode(text[:1000])   # text = input.txt
B, T = 4, 32
buf = torch.tensor(tokens[:B*T + 1])
x = buf[:-1].view(B, T).to(device)   # inputs
y = buf[1:].view(B, T).to(device)    # targets, shifted by one
model = GPT(GPTConfig()).to(device)
logits, loss = model(x, y)
print(logits.shape, loss.item())     # expect about 10.82

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Targets without the shift

Using y = x asks the model to copy the token it can already see. It's the wrong task: OpenAI's trained GPT-2 scores 8.97 on it versus 5.11 on the real targets (computed). The init check doesn't catch it (9.94 at init, computed).

✗ Only the last position

Grading just logits[:, -1] against y[:, -1] gives 4 predictions per batch instead of 128. The same forward pass does the work for all 32 positions, so this throws away 31 of every 32 training signals.

✗ Skip the loss-at-init check

A bad init hides until training looks slow. With every logit 10× too large, the first loss is 24.01 (computed), and early steps are spent just shrinking confident wrong guesses (the batchnorm course's hockey-stick loss).

✓ Shift by one, flatten, check ln V

One extra token gives B·T labelled predictions per batch, one cross_entropy call turns them into a single number to minimise, and 10.82 tells you before step 1 that the scores start out evenly spread.

What the 10.82 check does not prove

It only tests one thing: that the untrained model's scores start out roughly equal. It says nothing about whether your targets are right (the no-shift bug still starts near 10) or whether attention can peek at future tokens (a model that cheats is just as confused at step 0). For those, the next check is to overfit one batch and watch the loss fall towards 0 (the next card).

The expected value also moves with the vocabulary: ln 27 = 3.30 for the 27-character makemore models, and ln 50,304 = 10.826 once training pads the vocabulary (nice numbers card). Always recompute ln V for your own model rather than remembering "10.82".

Understand and remember

  • Targets are the inputs moved one token to the left → position t's target is the token right after it → with a causal mask every column is a fair next-token question → one row of T tokens gives T training examples (128 per 4 × 32 batch)
  • F.cross_entropy wants an (N, C) score table and N class ids → logits (B, T, V) and targets (B, T) are flattened to (B·T, V) and (B·T,) → softmax and −ln are applied row by row → the mean is one loss to minimise
  • An untrained model should spread its probability evenly → p(right token) ≈ 1/V → loss ≈ −ln(1/V) = ln V = 10.82 for V = 50,257 → a first loss far above that (24.01 with logits ×10) means the init is too confident
  • Small random logits (std 0.555 at init) → the right token is slightly less likely than 1/V on average → the loss sits a bit above ln V (≈ ln V + std²/2 = 10.98) → 10.96 is healthy, not a bug
  • detail that matters .view only relabels memory → x and y share the buffer's storage with no copy → the batch costs nothing extra to build, but the tensor must be contiguous for view to work
  • detail that matters The check only tests the starting scale → a no-shift bug (y = x) still starts near 10 (9.94) → you need the overfit-one-batch test to check the data and targets

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain in plain words why the untrained model's loss should be about ln V, and what it tells you if the first loss is 24 instead.
    Check
    • an untrained model has no reason to prefer any token, so each gets about 1/V
    • the loss is −ln of the probability of the right token, so −ln(1/V) = ln V
    • for V = 50,257 that is 10.82
    • 24 means the model is confidently wrong: the logits are too large at init, so the init needs fixing
  2. mechanism Walk through what happens to the 4 × 6 teaching batch from the 25-token buffer to the targets of row 0, naming what view does and doesn't do.
    Check
    • x = the first 24 tokens, y = tokens 1 to 24 (one position later)
    • view(4, 6) relabels the 24 numbers as a grid without copying
    • row 0 of x is 'First', ' Citizen', ':', newline, 'Before', ' we'
    • row 0 of y is the same shifted by one, ending with ' proceed', which is row 1's first input
  3. tradeoff A colleague grades only the last position of each row (logits[:, -1] vs y[:, -1]) because "that's the real next token". What do they give up, and is anything gained?
    Check
    • 4 predictions per batch instead of 128
    • the forward pass already computed scores for all 32 positions, so the compute is the same
    • with a causal mask every position is an honest prediction, so nothing is gained
    • training gets about 32 times fewer signals per step
  4. tradeoff Your loss at init is 10.9, right on target, but training never gets below about 9. Why doesn't the 10.82 check rule out a data bug, and what would you run next?
    Check
    • the check only says the initial scores are evenly spread
    • wrong targets (for example no shift) or a leaky mask also start near 10
    • next: overfit one batch; a correct pipeline drives the loss towards 0
    • then print a few x/y rows decoded to text and check the shift by eye

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs The loss you should see at step 0: The makemore MLP expected ln 27 = 3.30 at init; GPT-2 expects ln 50,257 = 10.82. What is the same idea underneath, what differs, and how does a bad init show up in each?

Check
  • same idea: uniform guesses give −ln(1/V) = ln V
  • only V differs: 27 characters vs 50,257 tokens
  • in both, too-large logits make the first loss far above ln V (27 in the MLP lecture, 24.01 here with logits ×10)
  • GPT-2 avoids it with std 0.02 init, so its first loss is already near 10.82

vs Chunks and batches: block_size 8 gives 8 examples: The character GPT also built x and y by shifting a chunk by one. What is fundamentally the same, and what changed when moving to GPT-2's tokens?

Check
  • same shift-by-one: every position predicts the next unit
  • same (B, T) grid and the same flatten before cross-entropy
  • the unit is now a BPE token (up to 50,257 choices) instead of one of 65 characters
  • so each row covers more text, and the expected initial loss is ln 50,257 instead of ln 65

vs Logits to loss, and why F.cross_entropy: In the MLP course you replaced a hand-written softmax plus −log with F.cross_entropy. Why does GPT-2 need the extra view calls that the MLP didn't?

Check
  • the MLP made one prediction per example, so its logits were already (N, 27)
  • GPT-2 makes a prediction at every position, so its logits are (B, T, V)
  • F.cross_entropy reads a 3-D tensor's second dimension as the classes, so passing (B, T, V) directly would be wrong
  • flattening to (B·T, V) makes each position one row, like the MLP's examples

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

On tiny shakespeare, a model that learns only how often each token appears (and ignores the context) is the obvious first thing training can find. Guess its loss on the whole text: closer to 10.8, 6.3, or 2?

Reveal

About 6.3. Tiny shakespeare is 338,025 GPT-2 tokens using 11,706 distinct ids, and the entropy of their frequencies is 6.32 nats (computed for this card). That is why the next card's data loader reaches about 6.8 after 50 steps: most of the early drop from 10.82 comes from learning token frequencies, not grammar.
