Where the time goes: fp32 → TF32 → bf16

Also called: mixed precision, automatic mixed precision (AMP), TensorFloat-32, bfloat16 autocast. Lecture 9 · SECTION 2: Let's make it fast. GPUs, mixed precision, 1000ms · 1:22:18

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Your training step works but is slow: about 1000 ms per step even on an A100, and you have 19,073 steps to run. Every number is a 32-bit float by default, and the GPU's fastest matmul paths are locked behind formats with fewer bits.

Where it fits: You have a correct training loop that prints ms per step (the overfit card). This is the first card of "make it fast": it changes how numbers are stored and multiplied, not what the model computes.

In one breath: by default every number in PyTorch is a 32-bit float (fp32). A training step doesn't need that much precision. Two changes let the GPU use fewer bits where it's safe: one line switches matrix multiplies to TF32 inside the GPU's matmul units, and torch.autocast runs the forward pass in the 16-bit bfloat16 (bf16) format. On Karpathy's A100 the step goes from about 1000 ms to 333 ms to 300 ms, while the weights themselves stay fp32.

Predict first: the A100's datasheet says TF32 matmuls run 8× faster than fp32 ones. Karpathy flips one line to turn TF32 on. His 1000 ms step becomes how many ms?

333 ms: about 3×, not 8×. The 8× applies only to the arithmetic inside the matmul units. Every number still travels to and from GPU memory as 32 bits, and a lot of the step is spent waiting on that traffic. Karpathy's words: these workloads are "memory bound" (1:38:35).

Why 3× and not 8×: a toy model

Suppose TF32 made only a fraction f of the step 8× faster and left the rest alone. Solve for the f that turns 1000 ms into 333 ms:

333=1000× ( (1−f)untouched part + f8matmul math, 8× faster ) ⇒ f=1−0.3331−1/8≈0.76
fshare of the fp32 step that is tensor-core arithmetic≈ 0.76 (computed) 1 − feverything else: moving numbers between memory and the chip, elementwise ops, Python≈ 240 of 1000 ms

This is Amdahl's law applied as a toy model, not a profile: its only inputs are 1000, 333 and the 8×. It still makes the point. However fast the arithmetic gets, the step can't drop below the part TF32 doesn't touch, which is why the next cards attack memory traffic instead.

What the bits buy

A float is stored as a sign, an exponent (how large or small the number can be: the range) and a mantissa (how many significant digits it keeps: the precision). The formats trade one for the other. Widths are drawn to scale in bits:

What autocast changes, and what it leaves alone

Karpathy wraps only the forward pass and the loss in torch.autocast(device_type, dtype=torch.bfloat16) and leaves backward() and the optimizer step outside it. Inside, PyTorch decides op by op: matmuls get bf16 copies of their inputs, while precision-sensitive ops like softmax, LayerNorm and the loss may stay fp32. Why bf16 and not fp16? bf16 has the same 8 exponent bits as fp32, so gradients keep their range and no gradient scaler is needed. With fp16, a gradient like 1e-8 becomes 0, so you'd need a GradScaler that multiplies the loss by a large factor before backward and divides it out afterwards (1e-8 × 65,536 = 6.55e-4, which fp16 can hold; computed).

▶ Build itruns on your M3 · about 25 s

When: pause the video at 1:39:38 (float16, gradient scalers, bfloat16). Time your training step with and without autocast. x and y are a (4, 256) batch of token ids. The real TF32 → bf16 ladder needs a rented NVIDIA GPU; on the M3 the TF32 line does nothing.

torch.set_float32_matmul_precision('high')  # TF32 on NVIDIA only
device = "mps"
model = GPT(GPTConfig(vocab_size=50304)).to(device)
optimizer = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)
for step in range(8):
    t0 = time.time()
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    with torch.autocast(device_type=device, dtype=torch.bfloat16):
        logits, loss = model(x, y)     # forward + loss only
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    torch.mps.synchronize()            # wait for the GPU to finish
    dt = (time.time() - t0) * 1000
    print(f"step {step} | loss {loss.item():.4f} | dt {dt:.0f} ms")

Without synchronize() you'd time only how long the CPU takes to queue the work, not how long the GPU takes to do it.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ fp32 everywhere

The default. Every matmul uses the slowest 19.5 TFLOPS path and every tensor moves 4 bytes per number: ~1000 ms per step on the A100.

✗ fp16 autocast

Same 16 bits as bf16, but only 5 exponent bits: 70000 becomes inf and 1e-8 becomes 0. It works only with a GradScaler, which is one more moving part to get wrong.

✗ The whole model in bf16

model.bfloat16() halves the memory for the weights too, but an update of 3e-4 to a weight of 1.0 rounds away, and 1000 additions of 0.01 sum to 4.0. Training quietly stalls.

✓ TF32 matmuls + bf16 autocast

One line and one with block. The matmuls get the fast paths, the weights, gradients and optimizer stay fp32, and no scaler is needed: 1000 → 300 ms.

Is lower precision always a win?

Not on every machine. TF32 is an NVIDIA tensor-core format, so the M3 has nothing for that line to switch on. There, bf16 gave 1.06× and 0.97× at T=256 and 1.41× at T=1024 (all under load). One Mac trap: build-nanogpt sets device_type = "cpu" for anything that isn't CUDA, and the lab shows that autocast with 'cpu' on MPS tensors leaves the logits fp32. So on a Mac, Karpathy's script silently trains in fp32. The labs pass 'mps', which torch 2.14 accepts.

Lower precision also isn't free. TF32 and bf16 round every matmul, so the loss won't match fp32 digit for digit. Keep fp32 where tiny differences matter, such as sums over many numbers, optimizer state, or checking your own gradients against PyTorch's, as the backprop-ninja course does. And if the step is memory-bound, fewer bits in the math can't fix that. That's the job of kernel fusion, next.

Understand and remember

  • TF32 rounds only inside the tensor cores → tensors still sit in memory as 32-bit fp32 → the time spent moving numbers is unchanged → 1000 → 333 ms (3×), not the datasheet's 8×
  • bf16 keeps fp32's 8 exponent bits and cuts the mantissa to 7 → same range (tiny gradients like 1e-8 survive) but coarse steps (eps 7.8e-3) → no gradient scaler needed, yet small updates and long sums round away
  • autocast covers only the forward pass and loss → matmuls run in bf16 while the stored weights, gradients and AdamW stay fp32 → speed from the matmuls without losing lr × grad updates that bf16 would round to nothing
  • the GPU runs asynchronously → time.time() right after step() measures only the queueing → you need torch.cuda.synchronize() / torch.mps.synchronize() before reading the clock, or every speedup you measure is fiction
  • detail that matters build-nanogpt sets device_type = "cpu" for anything that isn't CUDA → on MPS tensors that autocast does nothing (logits stay fp32) → on a Mac the script silently trains in fp32 unless you pass 'mps'
  • detail that matters fp16 has only 5 exponent bits → 70000 becomes inf and 1e-8 becomes 0 → fp16 training needs a GradScaler that scales the loss up before backward and back down after

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

The next card adds torch.compile and changes no precision at all, yet takes the A100 step from 300 ms to about 130 ms. Before reading it: where must those ~170 ms have been going, given what bf16 could and couldn't fix?

Reveal

Into memory traffic and overhead, not arithmetic. bf16 already had the fast matmuls and only saved 33 ms, so most of the remaining 300 ms is reading and writing tensors between GPU memory and the chip (every elementwise op like GELU or the residual add is a separate round trip) plus the Python interpreter launching each kernel. torch.compile fuses those ops into fewer kernels, which cuts the round trips.
