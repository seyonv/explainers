Nucleus sampling (top-p)

At each step, keep the smallest set of most-likely next tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p, rescale them, and sample one at random. Codex uses p = 0.95.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A model gives a probability for every token in its vocabulary. Always taking the top one makes every sample identical, but sampling from all of them lets the long tail of unlikely junk tokens in, and over a program of 100+ tokens one bad pick is often enough to break it.

Where it fits: It builds on drawing one outcome from a probability distribution (the toolkit's sampling card) and on temperature, which reshapes that distribution. Top-p is the other half: it cuts the tail before the draw. The structured-outputs course comes back to it when a grammar mask and a top-p cut meet.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Greedy decoding

Always takes the single top token. No variety: 100 samples are 100 identical programs, so pass@k gains nothing from extra tries.

✗ Pure sampling

Samples from every token. Rare junk tokens slip in, and across a 100+ token program, one bad pick is often enough to break it.

✗ Top-k sampling

Samples from a fixed number k of top tokens. Wrong size: keeps bad options when the model is sure, too few when it is unsure.

✓ Top-p (nucleus)

Cutoff adapts to confidence: many options when unsure, one when sure. Variety for pass@k without the junk tail.

Is it always used for code generation?

No. It's a decoding setting you choose, not a rule. Codex used top-p = 0.95 for all its sampled evaluations, combined with temperature (low, like 0.2, for best pass@1; higher, like 0.8, for best pass@100). Other evaluations use greedy decoding when they only need one answer, where it often does just as well, and some use temperature alone or top-k. Top-p is a popular default, not a guaranteed winner.

Understand and remember

  • Sort tokens by probability → add them up until the running total first reaches p → that smallest set is the nucleus → everything below the cut gets probability 0 → the long tail of junk can't be sampled
  • The cut is set by probability mass, not by a count → a confident model reaches p with 1–2 tokens and an unsure one needs many → the nucleus shrinks and grows with confidence, where top-k keeps k tokens either way
  • Kept tokens are divided by their total (0.96 here) → their ratios don't change, only their scale → the draw is still random among plausible tokens → samples differ from each other, which pass@k needs, without the junk that breaks long programs
  • Temperature reshapes the whole distribution first → top-p then cuts its tail → low T concentrates mass so fewer tokens reach p, and high T spreads it so more survive → the two knobs together set how diverse the samples are
  • detail that matters Pure sampling risks the tail at every token → if 4% of the mass sat below the cut at each of 100 steps, a program would dodge the tail every time with probability 0.96100 ≈ 1.7% → cutting the tail matters more the longer the output

Reference only

  • Codex's setting: p = 0.95 on every sampled evaluation (Chen et al. 2021, §3.2)
  • the paper's temperature optima: about 0.2 for pass@1 and 0.8 for pass@100
  • the original name and paper: nucleus sampling, Holtzman et al. 2020
  • API parameter names such as top_p

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Sampling temperature: Temperature and top-p both change how varied Codex's samples are. What does each one do to the distribution, and why does Codex tune temperature per k but leave p fixed at 0.95?

Check
  • temperature rescales every logit: it keeps all tokens but moves mass toward (low T) or away from (high T) the favourite
  • top-p keeps the order and ratios but zeroes the tail
  • temperature is the diversity dial matched to the budget (0.2 for pass@1, 0.8 for pass@100); top-p is a safety cut that stays useful at any T
  • failure modes: T too high lets junk rise; top-p alone can't make samples sharper

vs Sampling settings under a grammar: Under a JSON grammar, top-p 0.9 can leave zero valid tokens. What property of top-p causes that, and what order of operations fixes it?

Check
  • top-p cuts by probability rank, blind to validity
  • if the only valid token (} at 2%) is in the tail, the cut removes it and the mask then leaves nothing
  • apply the grammar mask first, then top-p on what's left; or turn top-p off for strict schemas

vs Sampling names from the table: The bigram sampler draws from the full distribution with torch.multinomial. What would a top-p cut change in its output, and what would it cost?

Check
  • same draw, but over a sorted, truncated and renormalised row
  • rare transitions (like the 0.0065 e → x step in the card's walk) would often be cut, so fewer odd names
  • the cost: less variety, and a model that samples only from the head no longer reflects the full distribution it learned

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Take the card's eight tokens as the whole distribution and apply temperature 0.5 before the p = 0.95 cut. Does the nucleus grow, shrink, or stay at 5 tokens? Commit, then reveal.

Reveal

It shrinks to 3. Squaring each probability (T = 0.5) and renormalising gives about 0.572, 0.322, 0.081, 0.018, …, so total, return and result already reach 0.975. At T = 2 the opposite happens: the distribution flattens and 7 of the 8 tokens survive. Temperature sets how far the mass is spread; top-p then decides how much of it to keep.
