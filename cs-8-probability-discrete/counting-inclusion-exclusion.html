Inclusion–exclusion

Also called the inclusion–exclusion principle, PIE or the sieve formula. The source guide's Discrete Mathematics section has two problems of this kind: integers up to 1000 that are multiples of neither 6 nor 9, and a three-way split of 10,000 insurance policyholders.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Adding group sizes counts everything that sits in two groups twice. Checking items one by one costs O(n), which is hopeless at n = 1018 and impossible when all you have is totals (10,000 policyholders, no list).

Where it fits: The first concept card; it assumes only sets and floor division. Stars and bars (upper caps) and the bridge hands reuse its alternating-sign correction, and the complement 'none = total − union' comes back throughout the course.

You'll meet: singles, pairs, triple: + − + the overlap of ÷6 and ÷9 is ÷lcm = ÷18 the complement: none = total − union one term per nonempty subset of groups (2m − 1)

By the end: Count 'at least one' and 'none' of several overlapping groups from overlap sizes alone, and explain why the signs alternate.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  2. In the insurance problem, why do the young married males vanish if you stop after subtracting the pairs?

  4. What does stopping after an add step, or after a subtract step, tell you about the true answer?

In one breath

To count things that are in at least one of several groups, add the group sizes; everything in two groups got counted twice, so subtract every pairwise overlap; now the things in all three were added three times and removed three times, so add the triple overlap back. Keep alternating until you reach the overlap of all the groups. To count "none of them", subtract the result from the total.

Filled in for problem 1: |÷6 ∪ ÷9| = 166 + 111 − 55 = 222, so 1000 − 222 = 778 are multiples of neither.

Problem 1: multiples of neither 6 nor 9 up to 1000 → 778

both = multiples of lcm(6, 9) = 18: ⌊1000/18⌋ = 55 at least one = 166 + 111 − 55 = 222 neither = 1000 − 222 = 778 (brute force over 1..1000: 778)

The overlap is multiples of 18, not of 6 × 9 = 54: 18 is already a multiple of both. Only-6 = 166 − 55 = 111, only-9 = 111 − 55 = 56. The source's 778 is correct.

Problem 2: young, female and single → 880

10,000 policyholders: 3000 young, 4600 male, 7000 married; 1320 young male, 3010 married male, 1400 young married; 600 young married male. "Female" is not-male and "single" is not-married, so the question asks for young people outside both of the other circles.

young − (young male ∪ young married) = 3000 − (1320 + 1400 − 600) ← inclusion–exclusion inside "young" = 3000 − 1320 − 1400 + 600 = 880 (brute force over 10,000 people: 880)

The source writes it as (3000 − 1320) − (1400 − 600) = 1680 − 800: young females, minus young married females. Same terms, same 880; the source is correct. The other seven regions come from the same subtract-the-overlaps step and add up to 10,000.

Singles, pairs, triple: both problems, term by term

Like a head count at a party where guests belong to clubs: count each club's members, then correct for anyone you counted more than once.

In Python

from itertools import combinations
from math import lcm

def count_none(n, divisors):
    """How many of 1..n are divisible by none of the divisors."""
    total = 0
    for k in range(len(divisors) + 1):
        for group in combinations(divisors, k):
            # lcm() of no numbers is 1, so k = 0 adds n itself
            total += (-1) ** k * (n // lcm(*group))
    return total

def union_size(sets):
    """|A1 u A2 u ... u Am| from the sizes of every intersection."""
    total = 0
    for k in range(1, len(sets) + 1):
        for group in combinations(sets, k):
            total += (-1) ** (k + 1) * len(set.intersection(*group))
    return total

print(count_none(1000, [6, 9]))
# young, male, married: sets of ids for all 10,000 people, rebuilt
# from the 8 Venn regions (see code/)
print(union_size([young, male, married]), len(young - male - married))
# → 778
# → 9470 880

Time O(2m) · Space O(m) for m groups: one term per nonempty subset of the groups, each a floor division (or an intersection). It doesn't depend on n: problem 1 costs 4 terms whether n is 1000 or 1018.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Just add the groups

166 + 111 = 277 multiples, so 723 "neither". Wrong by 55: every multiple of 18 was counted twice.

✗ Subtract the pairs and stop

14,600 − 5,730 = 8,870 in some group. Wrong by 600: the young married males were added 3 times and removed 3 times, so they vanished.

✗ Check every item

A loop over 1..1000 gets 778, but costs O(n): hopeless for n = 1018. And problem 2 has no list of people at all, only totals.

✓ Inclusion–exclusion

Works from overlap sizes alone, with signs that alternate by how many groups overlap. Exact, and 4 terms for two groups, 8 for three.

Is it always the tool?

No. The number of terms doubles with every group: 20 divisors means 220 − 1 = 1,048,575 overlaps. If the groups don't overlap, just add. If the events are independent, P(none) = (1 − p1)(1 − p2)… is one product. And a small universe you can list is often quicker to brute-force, as the code does to check itself.

The classic slip is taking the overlap of "÷6" and "÷9" as multiples of 54: that gives 1000 − 277 + 18 = 741, not 778. Where it shines is the "at least one" and "none" questions: derangements (no letter in its own envelope; 9 of the 24 orders of 4), the bridge hands on the next card, and surjections. Stopping after an add step gives an upper bound and after a subtract step a lower bound (the Bonferroni inequalities); that is standard, not from the source.

Understand and remember

  • Adding group sizes counts an item once per group it's in → an item in j groups is counted j times → subtract the pairs, add back the triples, … → the alternating sum counts every item exactly once
  • Each term needs only an intersection size → ⌊n / lcm⌋ for divisibility, or the given totals for the policyholders → no list of items is needed → the cost is 2m − 1 terms for m groups, whatever n is
  • 'None' is hard to count directly, but 'at least one' is the union → none = total − union: 1000 − 222 = 778, 10,000 − 9,470 = 530
  • detail that matters Partial sums alternate above and below the answer (Bonferroni) → when the terms shrink fast you can stop early with a bracket → but the full sum needs 2m − 1 terms, which is 1,048,575 at m = 20

Reference only

  • itertools.combinations and math.lcm
  • the eight Venn-region counts of the policyholder problem
  • the general formula's (−1)|S|+1 indexing

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Replace 9 with 12: how many of 1..1000 are multiples of neither 6 nor 12? Commit to a number before revealing.

Reveal

834. Every multiple of 12 is already a multiple of 6, so the overlap is the whole ÷12 group (lcm(6, 12) = 12, 83 numbers). The union is 166 + 83 − 83 = 166, and 1000 − 166 = 834. When one group sits inside another, the pair term cancels it exactly.
