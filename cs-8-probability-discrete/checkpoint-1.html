Checkpoint 1: counting without listing

Ties together inclusion–exclusion, stars and bars and choosing without conflicts, and bridge hands: three ways to count a set too big to list. You can correct overlaps after the fact, build a one-to-one match so there are none, or choose in a fixed order so nothing repeats.

How to use this page. Close the three cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure: it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you first have to work out which idea applies.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?Ten identical cookies go to 3 children. Nobody may get more than 4. How many ways are there, and which two ideas from this part do you need together?
    Check
    • stars and bars for the free count: C(12, 2) = 66
    • an upper cap can't be paid in advance, so subtract the overflows with inclusion–exclusion: give one child 5 first, C(7, 2) = 21 each, 3 × 21 = 63
    • two children can both overflow (5 + 5 = 10): add back 3 × C(2, 2) = 3
    • 66 − 63 + 3 = 6 (the orders of 4-4-2 and 4-3-3, 3 each; a brute force agrees)

    Missed it? Reopen stars and bars, "Does it always apply?" (the $8k cap).

  2. How many of 1, 2, …, 1000 are multiples of 10 or of 15 (at least one)?
    Check
    • singles: ⌊1000/10⌋ + ⌊1000/15⌋ = 100 + 66
    • overlap: multiples of lcm(10, 15) = 30, not of 150: ⌊1000/30⌋ = 33
    • 100 + 66 − 33 = 133

    Missed it? Reopen inclusion–exclusion, problem 1 and the "classic slip" paragraph.

  3. which concept?In how many ways can you put 3 rooks on an 8-by-8 chessboard so that no two share a row or a column?
    Check
    • choose the rows first, then give them distinct columns in row order
    • C(8, 3) × 8 × 7 × 6 = 56 × 336 = 18,816
    • nothing is double counted, because the rows are chosen as a set and the columns are assigned in row order

    Missed it? Reopen rooks and bridge hands, worked example 1.

  4. A student computes P(at least one suit's ace and king in a 13-card hand) as 1 − (16/17)⁴. Is that too high or too low, and why?
    Check
    • too low: 0.2153 against the exact 0.2198
    • the product assumes the four suit events are independent
    • they aren't: holding one suit's A+K uses up 2 of the 13 slots, which makes other suits' A+K slightly less likely

    Missed it? Reopen rooks and bridge hands, "Why it beats the alternatives".

  5. which concept?What is the probability that a 13-card hand is missing at least one suit entirely (a void)? Set up the terms before computing.
    Check
    • inclusion–exclusion over the 4 suits, but each event forces cards out: C(52 − 13j, 13) hands avoid j named suits
    • (4·C(39, 13) − 6·C(26, 13) + 4·C(13, 13)) / C(52, 13)
    • = 1621364909/31750677980 ≈ 0.0511; the j = 4 term is 0

    Missed it? Reopen rooks and bridge hands, "The two rules", then inclusion–exclusion, the "none" complement.

  6. Why must the ordered count 30 × 20 × 12 be divided by 6 to count sets of 3 grid cells, while "choose rows, then columns" needs no division?
    Check
    • ordered picks produce each set of 3 cells in all 3! = 6 orders: 7,200 / 6 = 1,200
    • choosing the rows as a set and then assigning columns in row order produces each set exactly once
    • so ordering is either removed after the fact (÷ k!) or never created

    Missed it? Reopen rooks and bridge hands, "same answer, three ways".

  7. $20k in $1k units, 4 options, no minimums: how many strategies are there? If each unit is then assigned to an option at random, is every strategy equally likely?
    Check
    • C(20 + 3, 3) = C(23, 3) = 1,771 strategies
    • no: with random units a split's weight is its multinomial coefficient, so 20-0-0-0 arises one way and 5-5-5-5 in 20!/(5!)⁴ ways
    • stars and bars counts outcomes; it doesn't make them equally likely

    Missed it? Reopen stars and bars, "Forgetting the minimums" and the last paragraph.

  8. After the singles and the pairs, the inclusion–exclusion sum for "at least one four of a kind" stands at 0.034200. What do you know about the exact answer without computing any more terms?
    Check
    • stopping after a subtract step gives a lower bound; after an add step, an upper bound (Bonferroni)
    • the singles alone gave 0.034334, so the answer lies in 0.034200–0.034334
    • that is already 0.034 to two significant figures; the exact value is 0.034200

    Missed it? Reopen inclusion–exclusion, "Is it always the tool?", and bridge hands, worked example 2.

3 · Project: every shape of a bridge hand

What it reinforces: stars and bars for listing the suit-length patterns (stars and bars), inclusion–exclusion for voids (inclusion–exclusion), and choosing structure before picks, including the difference between ordered and unordered arrangements (rooks and bridge hands).

A 13-card hand has a pattern: how many spades, hearts, diamonds and clubs it holds, (a, b, c, d) with a + b + c + d = 13. In about 30 lines of Python:

  1. Count the patterns. By stars and bars, how many patterns are there, and how many have no void (every suit at least 1)? Confirm both by looping over all tuples.
  2. Count the hands per pattern. A pattern holds C(13, a)·C(13, b)·C(13, c)·C(13, d) hands. Sum over every pattern and check that you get C(52, 13) = 635,013,559,600.
  3. Voids two ways. Add up the hands of every pattern that contains a 0, divide by C(52, 13), and compare with the inclusion–exclusion value from question 5.
  4. Shapes. Sort each pattern into a shape (4-4-3-2, 5-3-3-2, …) and add up the probability of each shape. Which four shapes are most common? Before you run it, predict where the most even shape, 4-3-3-3, will rank.
  5. Explain the ranking. For 4-3-3-3, 4-4-3-2 and 5-4-3-1, count how many suit assignments each shape has (the ordered versus unordered idea from the grid). Use it to explain why the most even shape is not the most common.
Done when: you have 560 patterns and 220 with no void, your sum over patterns equals C(52, 13) exactly, both void calculations agree, and you can explain the shape ranking in two sentences. Then compare with the check.
Check: the numbers (computed with Python's integers and Fractions)
  • Patterns: C(13 + 3, 3) = C(16, 3) = 560; with every suit at least 1, C(9 + 3, 3) = C(12, 3) = 220.
  • The sum of C(13, a)C(13, b)C(13, c)C(13, d) over all 560 patterns is exactly 635,013,559,600: this is Vandermonde's identity, and the check confirms that the patterns split the hands without overlap.
  • Voids: 1 − (hands in the 220 no-void patterns)/C(52, 13) = 1621364909/31750677980 ≈ 0.0511, the same as inclusion–exclusion.
  • Top shapes: 4-4-3-2 ≈ 0.2155, 5-3-3-2 ≈ 0.1552, 5-4-3-1 ≈ 0.1293, 5-4-2-2 ≈ 0.1058. The even 4-3-3-3 is fifth, ≈ 0.1054.
  • Why: 4-3-3-3 has only 4 suit assignments (which suit gets the 4), 4-4-3-2 has 12, and 5-4-3-1 has 24. A shape's probability is (hands per pattern) × (number of assignments), so the more lopsided shapes win on arrangements. A 13-0-0-0 hand has 4 assignments and 1 hand each: 4 hands in 635 billion.
