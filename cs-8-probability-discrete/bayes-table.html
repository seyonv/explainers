Bayes' theorem as a table

Also called inverse probability, or "posterior ∝ prior × likelihood". The source guide has three exam-P problems of this kind (smokers, car model years, a vaccine shipment), and one prior × likelihood table solves all of them.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Given some evidence, people read the answer off the likelihood ('1997 cars crash most, so it was probably a 1997') and forget how common each explanation was to begin with. The formula is easy to misapply too: leave out the 'other' row and the denominator is wrong.

Where it fits: The first conditioning card. Counting gave you probabilities of events; this card reverses a conditional, turning P(evidence | explanation) into P(explanation | evidence). Its total row, 'split on the cases, weight them, add', is the step every later conditioning card reuses.

You'll meet: prior × likelihood = joint the column total is P(E) posterior = joint ÷ total the odds form for two rows

By the end: Lay out any 'which explanation produced this?' problem as a table and get exact posteriors, including one with a binomial likelihood.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why can the smokers' problem be solved from ratios of death rates alone, and what cancels?
  2. In the car problem, why does 'other' win at 0.51 when 1997 cars have the highest accident rate?
  3. What does C(25, 2) = 300 do in the vaccine rows, and why can you drop it?
  4. How does the odds form turn two rows into one multiplication, and why is the likelihood ratio 1.777 smaller than 2.25?

In one breath

You saw some evidence and want to know which explanation produced it. List every explanation as a row, multiply how common it was before (prior) by how likely it makes the evidence (likelihood), then divide each product by the column total. The shares you get are the updated probabilities (posteriors).

The rule

P(Hi∣E)posterior = P(Hi)prior P(E∣Hi)likelihood ∑jP(Hj)P(E∣Hj)column total = P(E)

posterior[i] = prior[i] * likelihood[i] / sum(prior[j] * likelihood[j] for all j)

Filled in for the smokers: P(heavy | died) = 0.20 × 2 / (0.20 × 2 + 0.30 × 1 + 0.50 × 0.5) = 0.40 / 0.95 = 8/19 ≈ 0.421.

The five words, two examples each

Think of a detective with three suspects. The prior is how suspicious each one was before the clue, the likelihood is how well each one fits the clue, and the posterior is where the suspicion lands after.

The source gives smokers only as ratios; its table uses 0.40, 0.30, 0.25, which implies it set the nonsmoker death rate to 0.5. Any rate gives the same answer, because it cancels.

In Python

from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import comb

def bayes_table(rows):
    """rows: [(hypothesis, prior, likelihood)] -> table, P(E)."""
    joint = [p * like for _, p, like in rows]
    total = sum(joint)                       # P(E), the column total
    table = [(h, p, like, j, j / total)
             for (h, p, like), j in zip(rows, joint)]
    return table, total

def binom_pmf(n, k, p):
    return comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k)

# show() prints each row of bayes_table; see code/bayes-table.py
show("vaccine", [("A", F(2, 5), binom_pmf(25, 2, F(3, 100))),
                 ("B", F(3, 5), binom_pmf(25, 2, F(2, 100)))])
# → vaccine
#     A      0.40 x 0.1340 = 0.05360 -> 0.5423
#     B      0.60 x 0.0754 = 0.04524 -> 0.4577
#     P(E) = 0.09884
#   exact: 8/19 2/9 0.5423
#   sim: P(A | 2 bad) = 0.5415 from 98,778 shipments

Time O(k) · Space O(k) for k rows: one multiply per row, one sum, one divide per row. Fractions keep the textbook answers exact; the simulation uses numpy.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Going by the likelihood

"1997 cars crash most (5%), so it's probably a 1997." That ignores the prior. 1997 cars are only 16% of the road, and the posterior is 0.22; "other" wins at 0.51.

✗ Plugging into the formula

Same maths, but the denominator is where people slip. Forget the "other" row and you get 0.0080 / 0.0176 = 0.455 instead of 0.222.

✗ A tree diagram

Fine for two branches. Four model years × accident or not is 8 leaves, and you use only 4 of them. The table keeps just the rows you need.

✓ The table

One row per explanation: multiply across, sum down, divide. The total is P(E) for free, every number can be checked, and more evidence is just another likelihood column.

Is the table always the right tool?

No. It needs a short list of explanations that don't overlap and cover everything, which is why the car problem has an "other" row. When the unknown is a continuous number (say, the vaccine's true defect rate), the rows turn into a density, and you use a beta prior with a binomial likelihood instead: see the binomial and beta-binomial card.

With just two rows, the odds form is quicker: posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio = (0.4 / 0.6) × 1.777 = 1.185, so P(A) = 1.185 / 2.185 = 0.542. The 1.777 is 2.25 (3% vs 2% for the two bad vials) times 0.790 (23 good vials, 0.97²³ / 0.98²³), which pulls it back. With hundreds of observations the products underflow, so add log-likelihoods instead. Adding a column per clue also assumes the clues are independent given the explanation, as the 25 vials are.

Understand and remember

  • Evidence observed → each explanation's weight is prior × likelihood (the joint) → the total of the joints is P(E) → posterior = joint ÷ total, so the shares sum to 1
  • Only relative likelihoods matter → any factor common to every row (the nonsmoker death rate, C(25, 2)) cancels in the division → ratios alone solve the smokers problem
  • A high likelihood times a small prior can lose to a moderate likelihood times a large prior → 1997: 0.16 × 0.05 = 0.008 against other: 0.46 × 0.04 = 0.0184 → going by the likelihood alone is the base-rate error
  • The rows must not overlap and must cover every case → a missing row shrinks the total → 0.0080/0.0176 = 0.455 instead of 0.222
  • detail that matters More clues multiply more likelihoods → with hundreds, the products underflow → add log-likelihoods instead. A column per clue also assumes the clues are independent given the explanation (the next card)

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs First-step analysis: The Bayes table's total row and a first-step equation both compute Σ P(case) × value. What plays the role of the case in each, and why can only first-step analysis loop back to its own unknown?

Check
  • Bayes: the cases are explanations, and each value is a likelihood you know
  • first-step: the cases are the first move, and each value is the answer from wherever that move lands
  • a move can land back in the starting state, so the unknown appears on both sides; an explanation never produces itself

vs Binomial and beta-binomial: Two suppliers (two rows) against an unknown defect rate that could be any number in [0, 1]. What replaces the table, and what stays the same?

Check
  • the rows become a density: a Beta prior over the rate
  • prior × likelihood, then normalise, stays the same; the sum becomes an integral
  • a Beta prior with a binomial likelihood gives a Beta posterior, Beta(α + k, β + n − k)

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

A second shipment, independent of the first and from the same unknown company, also shows 2 bad vials of 25. Does P(company A) go above or below 0.5423, and to what? Commit first.

Reveal

Up, to about 0.678. The second shipment is another likelihood column. Given the company, the shipments are independent, so each row's likelihood is squared: posterior odds = (0.4/0.6) × 1.777² ≈ 2.105, so P(A) = 2.105/3.105 ≈ 0.678. Evidence that favoured A once favours it again.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Rerun the vaccine table for k = 0, 1, 2 and 3 bad vials of 25 (same priors, 3% against 2%). Predict first: which k makes company A less likely than its prior of 0.40?

Done when: The posteriors are 0.3403, 0.4388, 0.5423 and 0.6423 for k = 0…3. Zero bad vials points to the cleaner supplier B, so P(A) falls below 0.40; a single bad vial already tips it above.
