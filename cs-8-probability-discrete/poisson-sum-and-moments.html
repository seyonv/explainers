Poisson distribution

Also called the law of rare events, or "counts per interval at a steady rate". The source guide has two problems: accidents on three highways (Poisson rates 0.3, 0.5, 0.7) and a claims count where two claims are four times as likely as three.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: When you count accidents or claims per window, you never know the number of trials or each one's chance, only the average rate. A binomial needs n and p; you need a model whose only parameter is the rate.

Where it fits: Straight after the binomial, because the Poisson is Bin(n, λ/n) as n grows. The MGF card's 'MGFs multiply' gives its sum rule, and the negative binomial later relaxes its variance = mean.

In one breath

Count how many times something happens in a fixed window (accidents per day, claims per year) when events arrive independently at a steady average rate λ. The count is Poisson(λ): its mean is λ, its variance is also λ, and independent Poisson counts add up to another Poisson count whose rate is the sum of the rates.

Problem 1: three highways, rates 0.3, 0.5, 0.7 a day

The question asks for the expected number of accidents across all three. Expectation is linear, so it's 0.3 + 0.5 + 0.7 = 1.5, which is what the source gives; that step doesn't even need independence. With independence you also get the whole distribution. Multiply the three MGFs and the exponents add:

exp(0.3(e^t − 1)) · exp(0.5(e^t − 1)) · exp(0.7(e^t − 1)) = exp((0.3 + 0.5 + 0.7)(e^t − 1)) = exp(1.5(e^t − 1)) → Poisson(1.5)

Where it comes from: Binomial(n, λ/n) as n grows

Split the day into n tiny slots, each with an accident chance λ/n. The count is Binomial(n, λ/n); let n grow with the mean held at 1.5 and the binomial settles onto Poisson(1.5).

Total variation distance = ½ Σk |Bin(k) − Pois(k)|: the most any event's probability can differ between the two. It shrinks about 10× for each 10× in n, i.e. like 1/n, and stays under Le Cam's bound. P(0) for the binomial is (1 − 1.5/n)n → e−1.5.

In Python

from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import comb, exp, factorial

def pois(k, lam):
    return exp(-lam) * lam**k / factorial(k)

def binom(k, n, p):
    return comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k)   # comb = 0 if k > n

def tv(n, lam, kmax=60):
    """Total variation distance, Binomial(n, lam/n) vs Poisson(lam)."""
    return sum(abs(binom(k, n, lam / n) - pois(k, lam))
               for k in range(kmax)) / 2

lam = 0.3 + 0.5 + 0.7                  # highways: rates add
print(f"{lam:.1f} {pois(0, lam):.4f}")   # → 1.5 0.2231

lam2 = F(3, 4)                         # P(3)/P(2) = lam/3 = 1/4
print(lam2 + lam2**2)                  # → 21/16

for n in (10, 100, 1000):
    print(n, f"{tv(n, lam):.5f}")
# → 10 0.04193
#   100 0.00395
#   1000 0.00039

The full file adds the convolution check and the 10⁶-day simulation. Each pmf value is O(1) arithmetic; the TV sum is O(kmax) terms, cut at k = 60 where the Poisson(1.5) tail is far below double precision.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Exact binomial

Needs n and p: how many cars drove the highway, and each one's crash chance. You never know them; you only know the average, 1.5 a day.

✗ Normal approximation

N(1.5, 1.5) puts 5.1% of its mass on negative counts and gives P(0) = 0.156 instead of 0.223 (with continuity correction). Too skewed at small λ.

✗ Convolve term by term

Correct, but P(total = k) needs a double sum over how the k accidents split across three roads. The MGF product gives all k at once.

✓ Poisson(λ)

One number, the rate. Rates add across independent sources, mean and variance come for free, and it is the limit of the binomial when you can't count the trials.

Is every count Poisson?

No. Real claim and accident counts are usually over-dispersed: the variance is bigger than the mean, because some drivers or some days are riskier than others. A mix of Poissons with a gamma-distributed rate gives the negative binomial, the usual next model. Too many zeros (most policyholders never claim) calls for a zero-inflated model.

The sum rule also needs independence. A storm that raises the accident rate on all three highways at once makes the counts correlated, so their sum has variance above 1.5 and is not Poisson, even though the mean is still 1.5. Quick test on data: compare the sample mean and variance; if they're far apart, don't use Poisson.

Understand and remember

  • Many slots, each with a small chance λ/n → Bin(n, λ/n) → as n → ∞ at a fixed mean, Poisson(λ); the error is at most λ²/n (TV 0.04193 at n = 10, 0.00039 at n = 1,000)
  • P(k)/P(k − 1) = λ/k → e−λ cancels in any ratio → P(3)/P(2) = λ/3 = 1/4 → λ = 3/4 → E[X²] = λ + λ² = 21/16
  • Independent Poissons → their MGFs eλᵢ(eᵗ−1) multiply → the exponents add → Poisson(0.3 + 0.5 + 0.7); without independence the means still add, but the total isn't Poisson
  • One parameter → the variance is forced to equal the mean → real counts with varying rates are over-dispersed → move to the negative binomial
  • detail that matters P(0) = e−λ → P(at least one) = 1 − e−λ, which is 0.7769 for the highways, in one line

Check

  • the gaps between events are exponential with mean 1/λ
  • P(no event in time t) = e−λt is both P(count = 0) and P(wait > t)

The number of trials (or of failures before it) is geometric.
