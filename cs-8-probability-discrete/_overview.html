Counting, then conditioning

The overview for course 8 of the core CS canon series. It ties together the 16 cards on the source guide's General Probability page and the discrete half of Univariate Random Variables: counting without listing, splitting a problem on what you don't know, and the family of named distributions that those two moves keep producing.

In one breath

Almost every question here comes down to two moves. Count the equally likely outcomes without listing them, by correcting overlaps, placing bars or choosing rows first. Or condition: split on the one thing you don't know (which explanation, the first roll, how much of a sum the first part takes, the hidden rate), answer each piece where it is easy, and average the pieces by how likely they are. The named distributions are those two moves done once and remembered: binomial, geometric, negative binomial, hypergeometric and Poisson are all built from repeated yes/no trials, so learn how they are related and you only need to memorise a few of them.

The words people use

With vs without replacement

With replacement each draw has the same odds: binomial. Without, each draw changes the pool: hypergeometric, whose variance is smaller by (N − n)/(N − 1). In the recapture problem that is 3.53 against 5.40.

Trials vs failures

Geometric and negative binomial come in two forms that differ by a constant: count every trial (mean 1/p) or only the failures (mean q/p). The MGF tells you which: an eᵗ in the numerator means trials. Mixing them up is the usual off-by-one.

Overdispersion

Variance above what a one-rate model allows. It shows up whenever the rate differs between units: the beta-binomial is 2.8× the binomial's spread here, and the negative binomial adds μ²/r to the Poisson's μ.

✓ Conditioning

Fixing an unknown so the rest becomes simple, then averaging over it: P(E) = Σ P(H) P(E | H). Bayes, convolution, first-step analysis and every mixture are this one step. Say which variable you conditioned on: votes are dependent overall but independent given guilt.

When this advice doesn't apply

The closed forms rest on assumptions the problems hand you: independent trials, one fixed rate, a memoryless state. Real counts break them. Claim counts are usually over-dispersed, a storm makes three highways' accidents move together, and cards dealt without replacement carry their history, so the "state" has to include the whole remaining deck. When the assumption fails, the formula still returns a number, and the number is wrong. Check it on data (compare the sample mean and variance) or model the dependence directly.

Brute force and simulation still earn their place as checks: every card here confirms its exact answer against one or the other. Use Fractions while you derive, because inclusion–exclusion's alternating signs cancel and floats lose the digits. When the state space is too big for equations (a whole deck, many interacting players), simulate and report the standard error. For continuous and joint distributions, the same conditioning step carries over with integrals in place of sums: see the next course.
