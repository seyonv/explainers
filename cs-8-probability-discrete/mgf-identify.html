Reading a distribution off its MGF

Also called the moment generating function, or E[etX]. The cheat-sheet opens its discrete-distributions page with an MGF to decode (mean, variance and the distribution of Y) and later names NB(5, 0.70) from another MGF without showing the match. Both are worked here.

In one breath

An MGF packs a whole distribution into one function of t. For a discrete variable it is just a sum of et·value terms, each weighted by that value's probability. So you can read the values and probabilities straight off the terms, get the mean and variance by differentiating at t = 0, and name a distribution by matching its MGF to a known one, because two distributions with the same MGF (finite near 0) are the same distribution.

The rule

M(t) = sum(p[x] * exp(t*x)); M'(0) = sum(x * p[x]); M''(0) = sum(x*x * p[x]) Var(X) = M''(0) - M'(0)**2

Filled in for problem 1: M′(0) = (1·1 + 2·2 + 3·3)/6 = 14/6 = 7/3; M″(0) = (1·1 + 4·2 + 9·3)/6 = 36/6 = 6; Var = 6 − 49/9 = 5/9.

In Python

from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import exp

def moments_from_terms(terms):
    """terms: {k: c} for M(t) = sum c*e^(k t). Returns E[X], Var."""
    m1 = sum(c * k for k, c in terms.items())       # M'(0)
    m2 = sum(c * k * k for k, c in terms.items())   # M''(0)
    return m1, m2, m2 - m1 * m1

def nb_mgf(t, r, p):
    """MGF of NB(r, p) counting failures before the r-th success."""
    return (p / (1 - (1 - p) * exp(t))) ** r

def derivs_at_0(f, h=1e-4):
    """M'(0) and M''(0) by central differences."""
    d1 = (f(h) - f(-h)) / (2 * h)
    d2 = (f(h) - 2 * f(0) + f(-h)) / (h * h)
    return d1, d2

print(moments_from_terms({1: F(1, 6), 2: F(2, 6), 3: F(3, 6)}))
d1, d2 = derivs_at_0(lambda s: nb_mgf(s, 5, 0.7))
print(round(d1, 4), round(d2, 4), round(d2 - d1 * d1, 4))
# → (Fraction(7, 3), Fraction(6, 1), Fraction(5, 9))
# → 2.1429 7.6531 3.0612
# full file also prints:
#   M(0.2): closed 1.646212, series 1.646212

The series line sums the NB pmf times e0.2k for k < 400 and lands on the closed form, so the match is real. numpy's negative_binomial(5, 0.7) also counts failures; its simulated mean is within one standard error (±0.0017) of 15/7. Time O(terms) for a finite MGF; three function calls for the finite differences.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Matching mean and variance

Two numbers don't pin a distribution. A coin flip between 7/3 ± √5/3 (1.588 or 3.079) has mean 7/3 and variance 5/9 too, yet it is not problem 1's Y.

✗ Expanding the series by hand

Writing (1 − 0.3et)−5 as a power series in et does give the pmf, and it is how you'd prove the match. On an exam it costs minutes; the catalogue lookup costs seconds.

✗ Matching the wrong entry

Take the trials form and you'd say X counts trials, with mean r/p = 50/7 ≈ 7.14 instead of 15/7 ≈ 2.14. The et (or its absence) on top is the whole difference.

✓ Read, differentiate, match

Finite sums give the pmf directly; derivatives at 0 give any moment; a whole-function match gives the name, and uniqueness makes it a proof rather than a guess.

Does every distribution have an MGF?

No. E[etX] must be finite for t in an interval around 0, and heavy-tailed laws fail that: a Pareto tail, or the lognormal, which has every moment but no MGF. Then use the characteristic function E[eitX], which always exists and is also unique.

For counts (values 0, 1, 2, …) the probability generating function G(s) = E[sX] is often handier: M(t) = G(et), and P(X = k) is the coefficient of sk. The MGF's real payoff is sums: the MGF of independent X + Y is MX·MY, which is why NB(5, p) is the product of five geometric MGFs.
