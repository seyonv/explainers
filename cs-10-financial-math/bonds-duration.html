Bond pricing and duration

Also called present-value pricing, Macaulay duration, modified duration, DV01 and convexity. The source guide lists Financial Mathematics with no page, so this card is supplemented from standard fixed-income references. Running example: a 5-year bond paying a 4% annual coupon, priced at a 5% yield.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A bond's cash flows are fixed, but its price swings every time rates move. Two "5-year" bonds can lose different amounts on the same rate move, and repricing every scenario gives no single number to hedge or add across a portfolio.

Where it fits: Third card of the money part. It applies time value of money (price = NPV at the yield) and then asks a new question: how sensitive is that sum to the rate? Duration is the first of the course's three slopes, with beta and delta to come.

In one breath

A bond's price is its future payments discounted at one rate, the yield. Duration is the payment-weighted average time until you get your money back, and it tells you how many percent the price drops when the yield rises by one percentage point. That straight-line estimate is a little too pessimistic; convexity corrects the curve.

Duration alone always underestimates the price: it overstates the loss when yields rise and understates the gain when they fall. At 1 bp the miss is 0.000012, which is why DV01 is quoted from duration; at 300 bp it is almost a full point.

The words, two examples each

Think of a bond as a stack of IOUs with different due dates. Price is what the stack is worth today; duration is where the stack balances on a timeline.

In Python

def cash_flows(face, coupon, years):
    """(t, cash) for t = 1..years; the last year repays face."""
    c = face * coupon
    return [(t, c + (face if t == years else 0))
            for t in range(1, years + 1)]

def price(flows, y):
    return sum(cf / (1 + y) ** t for t, cf in flows)

def macaulay(flows, y):
    p = price(flows, y)
    return sum(t * cf / (1 + y) ** t for t, cf in flows) / p

def modified(flows, y):
    return macaulay(flows, y) / (1 + y)

def convexity(flows, y):
    p = price(flows, y)
    s = sum(t * (t + 1) * cf / (1 + y) ** (t + 2)
            for t, cf in flows)
    return s / p

flows = cash_flows(100, 0.04, 5)

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Just use maturity

"Both are 5-year bonds, so same risk." The zero moves 4.76% per 100 bp, the 4% coupon bond 4.40%. Maturity ignores that coupons return money early.

✗ Current yield

Coupon ÷ price = 4 / 95.6705 = 4.18%. It ignores the 4.33 pull back to face at maturity and says nothing about rate risk.

✗ Reprice every scenario

Exact for one bond, but it gives no single number to add across a portfolio or hedge with. You'd rerun it for every yield move.

✓ Duration + convexity

Two numbers that combine across bonds (a value-weighted average), give a hedge ratio from DV01, and land within 0.003 of the full repricing at ±100 bp.

Is duration always enough?

No. Both formulas assume every yield moves by the same amount and the cash flows are fixed. Real yield curves also twist (short rates up, long rates down), so desks add key-rate durations, one sensitivity per maturity bucket. For large moves, repricing beats the Taylor estimate: at +300 bp even convexity misses by 0.066.

Bonds whose cash flows change with rates break the formulas outright. A callable bond or a mortgage-backed security gets paid off early when rates fall, so its price curve can bend the wrong way (negative convexity). There you use effective duration: reprice with a rate model at y ± Δy and take the slope numerically.

Understand and remember

  • Price = Σ CFt/(1+y)t → each flow's sensitivity to y is proportional to its time t → the price's slope is −(PV-weighted average time)/(1+y) × P → modified duration = Dmac/(1+y) is the % price drop per unit rise in yield
  • Coupons return money early → early flows carry weight at small t → Dmac < maturity (4.6203 vs 5) → a zero has all weight at maturity → it is riskier per unit of price than a coupon bond of the same maturity (4.76% vs 4.40% per 100 bp)
  • Price is a convex function of yield → the tangent (duration) lies below the curve → duration alone overstates losses and understates gains → the ½·C·Δy² term closes most of the gap (−4.2098 → −4.0927 vs actual −4.0953 at +100 bp)
  • Duration and convexity are value-weighted averages across bonds → one pair of numbers summarizes a portfolio → DV01 gives a hedge ratio → but only for parallel shifts, so curve twists need key-rate durations
  • detail that matters Callable bonds and MBS get repaid early when rates fall → cash flows shrink exactly when price would rise → the curve bends the wrong way (negative convexity) → closed-form duration is wrong, so desks reprice at y ± Δy (effective duration)

Reference only

  • the convexity formula Σ t(t+1) CF/(1+y)t+2 / P
  • semiannual convention for US Treasuries
  • DV01 = Dmod × P × 0.0001
  • the example's table values (95.6705, 4.6203, 4.4003, 24.4724)

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Black–Scholes and the Greeks: Duration and convexity vs delta and gamma: what's the same about them, and why does a delta hedge lose money on a jump while a duration-hedged long bond gains on big moves?

Check
  • same: first and second derivatives of price with respect to one input, used in a Taylor estimate
  • bond price is convex in yield, so owning it means positive convexity: big moves either way beat the tangent
  • a short call is short gamma: big stock moves either way lose against the delta hedge (−0.2231 on a move to 105)
  • the sign of the second derivative decides who gains on big moves

vs Sharpe ratio, beta and CAPM: Duration is the bond's sensitivity to yields; beta is a stock's sensitivity to the market. How do you get each, and which one can you compute exactly?

Check
  • duration: a derivative of a known formula, exact given the flows
  • beta: a regression slope estimated from noisy data (1.374 ± 0.040 for a true 1.3)
  • both are slopes of a line and miss when the relationship bends or changes

Two bonds at a 5% yield: a 10-year zero (price 61.39) and a 10-year 8% annual coupon bond (price 123.17). Which loses the larger percentage for +100 bp, and for which one is the duration-only estimate further off? Commit before revealing.

Reveal

The zero: its Macaulay duration is the full 10 years (modified 9.52), while coupons pull the coupon bond's down to 7.54 (modified 7.18). Repriced at 6%, the zero drops 9.04% and the coupon bond 6.86%. The zero also has more convexity (99.8 vs 67.6), so duration alone overstates its loss by more: 9.52% predicted vs 9.04% actual, against 7.18% vs 6.86%. Long, back-loaded cash flows mean more duration and more bend.
