Pricing and sizing risk

The overview for course 10 of the CS core reference series. The source guide names Financial Mathematics but has no page for it, so all 12 cards are written from standard references. This page ties them together: what a future payment is worth today, why the typical path grows slower than the average one, and how the shared option contract (S₀ = K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 1) gets priced three different ways.

In one breath

A price is an average of future payoffs, pulled back to today. For a bond or a project the payoffs are known and you only discount them. For an option they are uncertain, and the trick is that you can copy the option with stock and cash, so its price is the discounted average under the weights that make that copy cost the same: the risk-neutral measure Q, not a forecast. Trees, the Black–Scholes formula and Monte Carlo are three ways to compute that one average. The other half of the course is about holding risk, not pricing it. Wealth compounds, so what you end up with follows the average log return, which sits about σ²/2 below the average return. That gap is why diversifying helps, why the median path lags the mean, and why staking twice the Kelly fraction loses money on a bet you win 60% of the time.

  • Shreve, Stochastic Calculus for Finance I (random walks, binomial pricing) and II (Brownian motion, quadratic variation).

The words people use

Simple vs log return

Simple: P₁/P₀ − 1, which combines across assets (a portfolio's return is the weighted sum). Log: ln(P₁/P₀), which adds over time. +50% then −50% is 0% by adding simple returns and −25% in fact.

Arithmetic vs geometric mean

The arithmetic mean is the expected return of one period. The geometric mean (CAGR) is the rate you actually compounded at. The gap is the volatility drag, about σ²/2: 2.014 bp a day on the returns card's path.

Duration, delta, beta

Three sensitivities: price change per move in yield, in the stock, and in the market. Each is the slope of a tangent line, so each misses on big moves, and convexity or gamma measures how much.

✓ Risk-neutral probability

The weight q that makes the stock earn exactly r on average. It isn't anyone's forecast: it's the only weighting under which the option's price matches the cost of the hedge that copies it. On the one-step tree, q = 0.5 and C = 5 whatever the real odds of an up move.

When this advice doesn't apply

Every number on this page comes from a model, not a market. The pricing cards assume a constant σ, lognormal prices with no jumps, no dividends and free, continuous hedging. Real options show a volatility smile, so desks use Black–Scholes mostly to translate between prices and implied σ, and the price comes from the market. Real returns have fat tails and volatility that clusters, and correlations tend to rise in a sell-off, exactly when diversification should help.

The growth cards assume you know the odds. Kelly with a mistaken p (0.6 believed, 0.55 true) loses money slowly, which is why people bet half Kelly or less. A measured Sharpe is noisy too: the Sharpe card's simulated market has a true Sharpe of 0.79 and measured 0.03 over 1,000 days. CAPM is the textbook line, not the one practitioners use for expected returns; they use multi-factor models. And expected value is only the right price when you can repeat the bet or hedge it away. For one large bet that can't be hedged, variance matters.
