Pricing and sizing risk

The overview for course 10 of the core CS canon series. The source guide names Financial Mathematics but has no page for it, so all 12 cards are written from standard references. This page ties them together: what a future payment is worth today, why the typical path grows slower than the average one, and how the shared option contract (S₀ = K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 1) gets priced three different ways.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Twelve financial-math cards read as twelve formulas unless you see that almost all of them answer one of two questions: what is an uncertain future payment worth today, and how fast does wealth that compounds actually grow? In one breath

A price is an average of future payoffs, pulled back to today. For a bond or a project the payoffs are known and you only discount them. For an option they are uncertain, and the trick is that you can copy the option with stock and cash, so its price is the discounted average under the weights that make that copy cost the same: the risk-neutral measure Q, not a forecast. Trees, the Black–Scholes formula and Monte Carlo are three ways to compute that one average. The other half of the course is about holding risk, not pricing it. Wealth compounds, so what you end up with follows the average log return, which sits about σ²/2 below the average return. That gap is why diversifying helps, why the median path lags the mean, and why staking twice the Kelly fraction loses money on a bet you win 60% of the time.

The words people use

Simple vs log return

Simple: P₁/P₀ − 1, which combines across assets (a portfolio's return is the weighted sum). Log: ln(P₁/P₀), which adds over time. +50% then −50% is 0% by adding simple returns and −25% in fact.

Arithmetic vs geometric mean

The arithmetic mean is the expected return of one period. The geometric mean (CAGR) is the rate you actually compounded at. The gap is the volatility drag, about σ²/2: 2.014 bp a day on the returns card's path.

Duration, delta, beta

Three sensitivities: price change per move in yield, in the stock, and in the market. Each is the slope of a tangent line, so each misses on big moves, and convexity or gamma measures how much.

✓ Risk-neutral probability

The weight q that makes the stock earn exactly r on average. It isn't anyone's forecast: it's the only weighting under which the option's price matches the cost of the hedge that copies it. On the one-step tree, q = 0.5 and C = 5 whatever the real odds of an up move.

When this advice doesn't apply

Every number on this page comes from a model, not a market. The pricing cards assume a constant σ, lognormal prices with no jumps, no dividends and free, continuous hedging. Real options show a volatility smile, so desks use Black–Scholes mostly to translate between prices and implied σ, and the price comes from the market. Real returns have fat tails and volatility that clusters, and correlations tend to rise in a sell-off, exactly when diversification should help.

The growth cards assume you know the odds. Kelly with a mistaken p (0.6 believed, 0.55 true) loses money slowly, which is why people bet half Kelly or less. A measured Sharpe is noisy too: the Sharpe card's simulated market has a true Sharpe of 0.79 and measured 0.03 over 1,000 days. CAPM is the textbook line, not the one practitioners use for expected returns; they use multi-factor models. And expected value is only the right price when you can repeat the bet or hedge it away. For one large bet that can't be hedged, variance matters.

1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • Money at different dates can't be added → discount each flow by e−rT (or (1+r)−t) → a certain payoff's price is just its discounted value (bonds, NPV) → an uncertain payoff needs an average first
  • An option's payoff can be copied with stock + cash → its price must equal the copy's cost → that cost equals the discounted average under the weights Q that make the stock earn r → trees, Black–Scholes and Monte Carlo are three ways to compute the same EQ
  • Wealth multiplies over time → what you end with follows the average log return → the log average sits ≈ σ²/2 below the simple average → the median path lags the mean, diversification raises growth, and 2× Kelly gives negative growth
  • A small edge adds n·(edge) of drift while noise grows only like √n → over few bets noise hides the edge, over many it decides everything → ruin odds, Kelly sizing and market-making edge all follow from this race
  • detail that matters Each pricing method has its own error law: the tree's error falls like 1/N (10 → 1,000 steps: 0.1972 → 0.0020), Monte Carlo's like 1/√n → so trees win for one asset with early exercise, simulation wins for path-dependent or many-asset payoffs

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain in four sentences why the price of the shared call doesn't depend on the stock's real expected return μ, even though its payoff obviously depends on where the stock goes.
    Check
    • stock + cash can copy the option's payoff
    • no-arbitrage forces the option price to equal the copy's cost
    • the copy's cost is computed with weights under which the stock earns r, so μ never enters
    • μ matters for what you expect to earn by holding the option, not for its price
  2. mechanism Walk the σ²/2 through three cards: the +50%/−50% example, GBM's mean vs median at μ = 8%, σ = 20%, and the Kelly curve's zero at about 2f*.
    Check
    • returns: average simple return 0% but the log sum is ln 0.75, a 25% loss
    • GBM: the mean grows at μ (108.33), the median at μ − σ²/2 (106.18)
    • Kelly: growth ≈ edge·f − f²/2 (variance of the bet), so doubling f makes the variance term eat the whole edge
  3. tradeoff You need a price for (a) a European call, (b) an American put, (c) an Asian call on 12 monthly closes. Pick one method for each from this course and say what makes the other two worse.
    Check
    • (a) Black–Scholes: exact, O(1); a tree only converges like 1/N, Monte Carlo leaves ±0.015 at 10⁶ paths
    • (b) binomial tree: backward induction can compare exercise vs hold at each node; the formula has no early exercise, and forward simulation doesn't know the value of waiting
    • (c) Monte Carlo: any path payoff, error 1/√n regardless of dimension; the tree would need 2¹² paths, and there is no closed form for an arithmetic average
  4. tradeoff A colleague says "0.49 is basically a coin flip, and betting 0.4 of the bankroll on a 60/40 coin is just aggressive, not wrong". Which two cards refute each claim, and with what numbers?
    Check
    • random walks: $10 → $20 at p = 0.49 is 0.4013, and $100 → $200 is 0.0180, not 0.5
    • Kelly: at f = 0.4, g = −0.00245 per bet; median wealth after 1,000 bets is 0.0866, and 56% end below where they started

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Markov chains and martingales: The course uses "a price is a martingale after discounting" (pricing) and "rSn is a martingale" (gambler's ruin). What is the martingale doing in each, and why is it the same trick?

Check
  • in both, a quantity whose expected future value equals its present value turns a path question into one equation
  • ruin: optional stopping on rSn gives the hitting probability
  • pricing: under Q the discounted stock (and so the discounted option) is a martingale, so today's price is the expected discounted payoff
  • the difference is where the weights come from: the coin's real p, or the hedge's q

vs Sums and the central limit theorem: Why does the CLT justify both the lognormal price model and Monte Carlo's error bar, and where does it fail each time?

Check
  • many small independent log returns add up to a near-normal log price, so the price is lognormal
  • the average of n independent payoffs has error s/√n and is near-normal, so ±1.96 SE is a 95% interval
  • it fails for prices when returns have fat tails or clustered volatility, and for Monte Carlo when the payoff variance is huge or infinite

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose volatility doubles to 40% while everything else stays fixed. Before checking any card, predict the direction of change for: the shared call's price, the GBM median after one year at μ = 8%, and the Kelly-style growth of holding the stock. Which half of the course gets better for the holder, and which gets worse?

Reveal

Pricing: the call gets more valuable (vega is positive; at σ = 30% it is already 14.23 against 10.45), because the option keeps the upside and caps the downside. Growth: the median falls to 100 · e0.08 − 0.08 = 100, so the typical stockholder goes nowhere, and the growth rate μ − σ²/2 drops from 6% to 0%. Volatility is worth paying for in an option and costs you in a compounding holding: the same σ²/2 on opposite sides.
