Checkpoint 1: discounting, compounding and sizing risk

Ties together time value of money, simple vs log returns, bond pricing and duration, portfolio mean and variance, Sharpe, beta and CAPM and the Kelly criterion: moving money across dates, what compounding does to volatile returns, and how much risk to hold.

How to use this page. Close the six cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure; it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you have to work out which idea applies before you can answer.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?A fund's brochure says it averaged +12% a year over ten years, with annual volatility of 30%. An investor who held it the whole time is disappointed with their account. Which idea explains the gap, and roughly what yearly rate did they actually compound at?
    Check
    • volatility drag: the average of yearly returns overstates compounded growth by about σ²/2
    • σ²/2 = 0.30² / 2 = 4.5%, so the geometric (compounded) rate is roughly 12% − 4.5% = 7.5% a year
    • a loss year shrinks the base the next gain works on; the brochure reports the arithmetic mean

    Missed it? Reopen simple vs log returns, "The words, two examples each" (arithmetic mean, geometric mean, volatility drag).

  2. You hold 1,000 of the 5-year 4% coupon bonds (100 face each, yield 5%). Yields rise 10 bp. About how much do you lose, and would you trust the same method for a 300 bp move?
    Check
    • DV01 is 0.0421 per bond per bp, so 1,000 × 10 × 0.0421 ≈ 421
    • for 10 bp duration alone is fine: the miss grows with Δy²
    • at +300 bp duration overstates the loss by 0.99 per bond (−12.63 vs −11.64); add convexity or reprice

    Missed it? Reopen bond pricing and duration, "Predicted vs actual: where convexity shows up".

  3. which concept?Two projects, cost of capital 5%. A is [−1000, 300, 400, 500] with an IRR of 8.90%. B is [−10000, 3900, 3900, 3900] with an IRR of 8.28%. A colleague picks A "because it returns more". Which idea says otherwise, and what numbers settle it?
    Check
    • NPV at your cost of capital, not IRR: IRR is a percentage with no size
    • NPV(5%): A = +80.44, B = +620.67; if you can only do one, B adds about 7.7× more value
    • both clear the 5% hurdle, so each is fine alone; the ranking is where IRR misleads

    Missed it? Reopen time value of money, "Why NPV beats the other decision rules".

  4. On the 60/40 even-money coin, one friend stakes 30% of wealth each bet and another stakes 10%. Who grows faster over many bets, and who has the rougher ride?
    Check
    • g(0.3) = 0.01475 and g(0.1) = 0.01504 per bet: almost the same growth, with 0.1 slightly ahead
    • 0.3 is past f* = 0.2, so its swings are much bigger for no extra growth
    • the growth curve is flat at the top and steeper on the right: under-betting is the cheaper mistake

    Missed it? Reopen the Kelly criterion, "Trace: growth at each stake".

  5. which concept?Your portfolio is all in asset A (σ = 20%). You move about a quarter of it into asset B, which is more volatile (σ = 30%), and your total risk goes down. Which idea explains it, and what makes it possible?
    Check
    • the portfolio variance's cross term: risk combines as variance, and ρ = 0.2 < 1 makes the cross term small
    • at w* = 0.7358 in A the portfolio's σ is 18.06%, below either asset alone
    • it would fail at ρ = +1, where σ is just the weighted average and all-A would be the minimum

    Missed it? Reopen portfolio mean and variance, "The rule" and the sweep table.

  6. A strategy earns a daily mean of 0.05% with a daily sd of 1% (rf = 0). Levering it 3× doesn't change its Sharpe ratio. Does that mean leverage doesn't matter? Use the continuous Kelly rule f* = μ/σ² (quoted, not derived, on the Kelly card) on the annual numbers.
    Check
    • Sharpe: mean and sd both scale by 3, so it stays 0.79
    • growth does depend on leverage: g(L) ≈ L·μ − L²σ²/2 with μ = 12.6%, σ² = 0.0252
    • Kelly leverage = 0.126 / 0.0252 = 5; growth is zero at 2× Kelly, L = 10; at L = 3 it's 0.2646 a year
    • Sharpe scores the bet; Kelly sizes it

    Missed it? Reopen Sharpe, beta and CAPM, "Rank by raw return", and the Kelly criterion, "So should you always bet full Kelly?".

  7. A risk report turns a daily volatility of 1.5% into an annual one by multiplying by 252 and gets 378%. What is wrong, what is the right number, and which assumption does the right method rely on?
    Check
    • variances add over independent days, so sd scales with √time, not time
    • 1.5% × √252 = 23.81% a year
    • it assumes independent days (and log returns, which add); autocorrelated returns break it

    Missed it? Reopen simple vs log returns, "Checked on a simulated path".

3 · Project: size a two-asset portfolio for growth

What it reinforces: the covariance cross term (portfolio mean and variance), Sharpe as the score of a risky mix (Sharpe and CAPM), volatility drag (returns), Kelly leverage (Kelly), discounting a future amount (time value of money) and duration on the safe leg (bonds).

assets A: mu 8%, sigma 20% B: mu 12%, sigma 30% rho = 0.2 cash r_f = 4% a year (continuous) levered L in the risky mix, 1 - L in cash: growth g(L) = r_f + L (mu_p - r_f) - L² sigma_p² / 2

The expected returns are the portfolio card's illustrative ones. In about 30 lines of Python (numpy is fine):

  1. Three mixes. Compute μ, σ and the Sharpe ratio (rf = 4%) for the minimum-variance mix, the 50/50 mix and the tangency mix w ∝ Σ−1(μ − rf), rescaled to sum to 1.
  2. Drag. For the tangency mix held without leverage, compute the compounded growth μ − σ²/2 and compare it with μ.
  3. Size it. Find the Kelly leverage L* = (μp − rf)/σp² and the growth at L*, at L*/2 and at 2L*.
  4. Ten years. Give the median value of 1,000 after ten years at L*, at L*/2 and unlevered, then discount the L* figure to today at 4% continuous.
  5. Stretch: the safe leg. Suppose the cash is instead held in the card's 5-year 4% bond at a 5% yield. How much does 1,000 in it lose if yields jump 100 bp, by duration alone and by a full reprice?
Done when: you have a table of the three mixes with Sharpes, the three growth rates, the three ten-year medians and the discounted value, plus the bond loss, and you can say in one sentence why 2L* earns only the risk-free rate. Then compare with the check.
Check: the numbers (computed with a short Python script)
  • Minimum variance: wA = 0.7358, μ 9.06%, σ 18.06%, Sharpe 0.280. 50/50: μ 10.00%, σ 19.62%, Sharpe 0.306. Tangency: wA = 0.4925, μ 10.03%, σ 19.72%, Sharpe 0.306, the highest (barely above 50/50).
  • Drag: the tangency mix compounds at 10.03% − 1.94% ≈ 8.09% a year unlevered.
  • Kelly: L* = 0.0603 / 0.0389 ≈ 1.55. Growth rf + SR²/2 = 8.68% at L*, rf + ⅜SR² = 7.51% at L*/2, and exactly rf = 4% at 2L*, because the extra return 2L*(μ − rf) is cancelled by the drag 4·L*²σ²/2.
  • Ten-year medians of 1,000: 2,381 at L*, 2,118 at L*/2 and 2,245 unlevered. The L* median is worth 2,381 × e−0.4 ≈ 1,596 today.
  • Bond leg: 1,000 buys 10.45 bonds; +100 bp loses ≈ 44.00 by duration alone and 42.81 on a full reprice (42.78 with convexity).
