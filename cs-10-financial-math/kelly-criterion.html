The Kelly criterion

Also called the Kelly bet, Kelly sizing, or growth-optimal betting; "half Kelly" is its common cautious variant. It answers how much of your bankroll to stake on a repeated favourable bet. The cheat-sheet lists Financial Mathematics with no page, so this card follows Kelly (1956) and Thorp.

In one breath

If you get the same edge over and over and reinvest your winnings, what matters is not the average payoff of one bet but how fast your wealth compounds. Kelly picks the fraction of wealth to stake that makes the expected log of wealth grow fastest. Bet less and you grow slower; bet more and you grow slower and swing harder; bet about twice as much and you stop growing at all.

Filled in for a 60/40 even-money bet: f* = 0.6 − 0.4/1 = 0.2, and g(0.2) = 0.6 ln 1.2 + 0.4 ln 0.8 = 0.10939 − 0.08926 = 0.02014, so wealth grows about 2.0% per bet (e0.02014 = 1.02034).

Worked example: the growth curve for the 60/40 bet

The curve is flat at its peak and falls off steeply on the right. That asymmetry is the whole argument for betting a little under Kelly.

g(f) = 0.6 ln(1 + f) + 0.4 ln(1 − f), computed at 101 points. Near the peak the curve is almost a parabola, so it falls back to 0 at about 2f* (0.389 here, 0.4 in that approximation).

In Python

import math, random, statistics as st

def growth(f, p, b=1.0):
    # expected log growth per bet at stake fraction f
    return p * math.log1p(b * f) + (1 - p) * math.log1p(-f)

def kelly(p, b=1.0):
    return p - (1 - p) / b          # from g'(f) = 0

def simulate(fs, p, n_bets, n_paths, seed, b=1.0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    finals = {f: [] for f in fs}
    for _ in range(n_paths):
        wins = [rng.random() < p for _ in range(n_bets)]
        for f in fs:                    # same flips, every f
            w = 1.0
            for win in wins:
                w *= 1 + b * f if win else 1 - f
            finals[f].append(w)
    return finals

p = 0.6
print(round(kelly(p), 4), round(growth(kelly(p), p), 5))
finals = simulate((0.1, 0.2, 0.4), p, 1000, 2001, seed=1)
for f, ws in finals.items():
    print(f, f"{st.median(ws):.4g}")
# → 0.2 0.02014
#   0.1 3.409e+06
#   0.2 5.556e+08
#   0.4 0.08656

Time O(paths × bets × stakes) · Space O(paths × stakes): every flip is replayed once per stake. f* itself is O(1); the full file also tracks each path's low point, the exact binomial tail, and the 2:1 example.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Maximize expected wealth

Each bet's mean is 1 + 0.2f, so the average says stake everything. All-in 1,000 times has E[W] = 1.21000 ≈ 1079, but you survive only with probability 0.61000 ≈ 10−222.

✗ Bet a fixed dollar amount

Stake 0.20 of your starting wealth every time and gains add instead of compound: 600 wins leave 1 + 0.2 × 200 = 41, against 5.56 × 108 for Kelly. A losing streak early on can still wipe you out.

✗ Bet bigger to get there faster

Past f* growth drops, and at 2× Kelly it turns negative: the median gambler ends with 0.087 of the stake and 56% end below where they began, despite winning 60% of bets.

✓ Kelly: maximize expected log wealth

Stake f* = edge / odds of current wealth. It never risks ruin (you always keep 1 − f), and over enough bets it ends with more wealth than any other fixed fraction, with probability tending to 1.

So should you always bet full Kelly?

Usually not. Full Kelly is a rough ride: in the simulation 45.5% of Kelly bettors halved their money at some point. It also assumes you know p. If the true p is 0.55 but you believe 0.6, your "Kelly" stake of 0.2 has g = −0.00014 per bet and slowly loses, while half Kelly (0.1) earns +0.00501, the true maximum. Overbetting hurts far more than underbetting, so practitioners bet half Kelly or less: about ¾ of the growth, and the chance of ever halving drops from about 50% to about 12.5% (Thorp's formula below the simulation table).

Kelly is also the wrong target when you have a deadline or a hard floor (a fund that is shut down after a 20% drawdown), when outcomes are not independent repeats, or when losses can exceed the stake (leveraged positions, fat tails). For a continuous asset with excess return μ and volatility σ, the same idea gives leverage μ/σ2 (Thorp §7.1, not derived here); see the portfolio cards for the mean-variance side.
