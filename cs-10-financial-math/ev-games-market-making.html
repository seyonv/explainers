Expected-value games and market making

Also called fair value, optimal stopping or backward induction; on the trading side, bid–ask quoting and adverse selection. This is the classic quant-interview warm-up: "what would you pay for this game?", then "make me a market on it". The source guide only names "Financial Mathematics", so this card is written from standard references.

In one breath

A game's fair price is its expected payoff when you play it as well as possible. If you get choices along the way, work backwards: the value of playing on is the bar a result has to beat before you keep it. A market maker quotes a bid a bit below that value and an ask a bit above, and earns the gap from traders who know no more than it does. It loses that gap, and more, to traders who know more.

The game and the rule

Roll a fair die and get paid the face in dollars. You may reroll, but you must keep the last roll. With n rerolls left, keep a face only if it beats V(n−1), the value of rolling again:

Filled in for one reroll: keep 4, 5, 6 (they beat 3.5), reroll 1, 2, 3. V₁ = (4 + 5 + 6)/6 + (3/6) · 3.5 = 2.5 + 1.75 = 4.25.

Make a market: one die, quoted 3.0 / 4.0

Uninformed traders buy or sell at random, so each one pays you half the spread, 0.5. An informed trader has seen the roll and only trades when it pays: they buy at 4.0 on a 5 or 6 and sell at 3.0 on a 1 or 2.

Break-even = the narrowest symmetric quotes with P&L ≥ 0 (bisection in the code). At α = 0.2 by hand: for half-width h < 0.5 the informed buy on 4, 5, 6 and sell on 1, 2, 3, losing (9 − 6h)/6 each, so 0.8h = 0.2 · (9 − 6h)/6 gives h = 0.3. At α = 1/3 the ask equals E[roll | buy]: your price already reflects what a buy tells you, which is the zero-profit condition in Glosten and Milgrom's model. At α = 0.5 a buy says the die is worth 4.30 on average, more than the 4.0 you sold at.

In Python

from fractions import Fraction as F
FACES = range(1, 7)

def game_value(n):
    """Value with n rerolls left: V(n) = E[max(X, V(n-1))]."""
    v = F(sum(FACES), 6)                  # n = 0: must keep, 3.5
    for _ in range(n):
        v = sum(max(F(x), v) for x in FACES) / 6
    return v

def mm_pnl(half, alpha, fair=F(7, 2)):
    """Expected P&L per trader, quoting fair +/- half."""
    bid, ask = fair - half, fair + half
    loss = sum(max(x - ask, 0) + max(bid - x, 0)
               for x in FACES) / 6         # informed only
    return (1 - alpha) * half - alpha * loss

[game_value(n) for n in range(4)]
# → [7/2, 17/4, 14/3, 89/18]  (3.5, 4.25, 4.6667, 4.9444)
[float(mm_pnl(F(1, 2), a)) for a in (0, F(1, 5), F(1, 3), F(1, 2))]
# → [0.5, 0.2, 0.0, -0.25]

Time O(n · 6) for n rerolls: each stage is one pass over the faces. Space O(1). Fractions keep 17/4 and 14/3 exact. The full file adds the trace, the break-even bisection and E[roll | buy].

Why backward induction beats the shortcuts

✗ Price it at 3.5

Ignores the reroll, which is an option you own. Sell the one-reroll game at 3.5 and you give away 0.75 a play.

✗ Best of two rolls

E[max of 2 dice] = 161/36 = 4.4722 assumes you see both rolls before choosing. You don't: you must give up the first roll blind. Overprices by 0.22.

✗ One fixed threshold

"Keep anything above average" (above 3.5) at every stage gives 37/8 = 4.625 with two rerolls. Keeping a 4 early is a mistake, costing 0.042.

✓ Backward induction

Solve the last decision first, then use its value as the bar for the one before. Exact (17/4, 14/3), and the same idea prices American options on a binomial tree.

Is the expected value always the price?

No. EV is the break-even price for a risk-neutral player who plays many times. For one big bet, variance matters: at $1 a pip you'd pay 4.25 for the reroll game, but at $1M a pip few people would pay $4.25M, and the Kelly criterion says how much of a bankroll to risk. Option prices use a risk-neutral EV only because the risk can be hedged away, which a die roll can't.

On the market side, EV is only the midpoint. The spread must cover informed flow: every buy is evidence that the value is higher, so a real market maker moves its quotes up after being lifted, widens when it suspects informed traders, and skews quotes to shed inventory. In an interview, a tight market you update after each trade beats a wide one you never move.
