Brownian motion and geometric Brownian motion

Also called the Wiener process (W) and GBM, or the lognormal price model. The source guide lists Financial Mathematics with no page, so this card follows Hull and Shreve, using a stock at S₀ = 100 with drift μ = 8% and volatility σ = 20% for one year.

In one breath

Brownian motion is a random walk shrunk to infinitely many tiny steps: it starts at 0, its moves over separate time spans are independent, and its move over a span of length t is normal with variance t. Geometric Brownian motion puts that randomness into the log of the price, so the price compounds, stays positive, and ends up lognormal. The price model under Black–Scholes is GBM.

Trace it on paper: four quarterly steps

Each step multiplies the price by ex, where x = (μ − σ²/2)Δt + σ√Δt · z. With Δt = 0.25: x = 0.015 + 0.1 z. The z values are picked by hand (illustrative).

Stepping in log space is exact for GBM at any step size, so four steps and 252 steps give the same distribution at T = 1. Only the path in between gets finer.

Why the σ²/2? Two views

Averaging (Jensen). Take log returns of +0.2 or −0.2 with equal odds. The median stays at 100, but the prices are 122.14 and 81.87, which average to 102.01: the up move gains more than the down move loses. For a normal log return, E[eσZ] = eσ²/2 = 1.0202 at σ = 0.2, so to make the mean grow at μ, the log must grow at μ − σ²/2.

Itô's lemma. Brownian paths are so rough that (dW)² doesn't vanish: it equals dt. So d(ln S) = dS/S − ½(dS/S)² = μ dt + σ dW − ½σ² dt. The sum of squared steps of W over one year tends to 1, while the path length blows up:

One draw per row, numpy default_rng(1). Σ(ΔW)² has mean 1 at every step count and its spread shrinks like √(2/n); Σ|ΔW| grows like √(2n/π). Smooth functions have zero quadratic variation, which is why ordinary calculus has no such term.

In Python

import math
import numpy as np

def gbm_paths(s0, mu, sigma, t, steps, n, seed=0):
    """n GBM paths on a grid of `steps`; exact in log space."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt = t / steps
    z = rng.standard_normal((n, steps))
    dlog = (mu - sigma**2 / 2) * dt + sigma * math.sqrt(dt) * z
    logs = np.cumsum(dlog, axis=1)             # log steps add
    return s0 * np.exp(np.hstack([np.zeros((n, 1)), logs]))

paths = gbm_paths(100, 0.08, 0.20, 1.0, 252, 10_000, seed=0)
st = paths[:, -1]
se = st.std(ddof=1) / math.sqrt(len(st))
print(f"mean   {st.mean():.2f}  (+/- {se:.2f} s.e.)")
print(f"median {np.median(st):.2f}")
print(f"P(S_T < 100) {np.mean(st < 100):.4f}")
# → mean   108.42  (+/- 0.22 s.e.)
#   median 106.31
#   P(S_T < 100) 0.3774

Time O(n · steps) · Space O(n · steps): one normal draw per path per step, all kept for plotting. If you only need ST, draw one normal per path: the log-space step is exact, so O(n).

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Arithmetic Brownian motion

S = 100 + 8t + 20W adds dollars, not percentages, so prices can go negative: P(S < 0) is 3.3 × 10⁻⁸ after 1 year but 0.0022 after 10. It was Bachelier's 1900 model.

✗ Drop the σ²/2

Writing S = 100 e0.08 + 0.2W makes 108.33 the median and pushes the mean to 110.52. Every price and Monte Carlo estimate built on it comes out about 2% too high.

✗ A discrete random walk

Fine to simulate, but the answer depends on the step size, and there is no closed form for the distribution. Brownian motion is the limit as steps shrink (Donsker), and that limit is what gives formulas.

✓ Geometric Brownian motion

Prices stay positive, returns are independent of the price level, ST has an exact lognormal law, and two numbers (μ, σ) fix it all. That's what makes Black–Scholes solvable in closed form.

Do real prices follow GBM?

Only roughly. GBM assumes constant σ and normal log returns. Real returns have fat tails (crashes much likelier than a normal allows), volatility that comes in clusters, and occasional jumps. That's why option markets show a volatility smile instead of one σ for all strikes. When those effects matter, people use stochastic-volatility models (Heston), jump-diffusion (Merton), or GARCH for forecasting.

GBM is still the default: it's the baseline in Black–Scholes and Monte Carlo pricing. For interest rates or spreads that can go negative or pull back to a level, people use mean-reverting models (Vasicek, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) instead. And for pricing, μ is swapped for the risk-free rate r; the real-world μ = 8% here only describes where the stock tends to go.
