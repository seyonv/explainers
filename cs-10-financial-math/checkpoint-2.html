Checkpoint 2: from random walk to option price

Ties together random walks, Brownian motion and GBM, binomial option pricing, Black–Scholes and the Greeks, Monte Carlo pricing and expected-value games and market making: how a random process becomes a price, three ways to compute it, and when an expected value is the right price at all.

How to use this page. Close the six cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure; it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you have to work out which idea applies before you can answer.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?A desk needs a price for a one-year European option on the shared stock that pays the highest daily close minus 100. Which method from this part fits, and why do the other two struggle?
    Check
    • Monte Carlo: simulate daily GBM paths under Q, compute the maximum on each path, discount the average
    • a binomial tree recombines on price, but the payoff depends on the path, so it stops recombining
    • there is no Black–Scholes-style formula for this payoff in the course; the formula covers the plain European payoff
    • the error bar falls like 1/√n whatever the path length

    Missed it? Reopen Monte Carlo pricing, "Why it beats the alternatives", and binomial pricing, "Is the tree always the tool?".

  2. Price the shared call (S₀ = K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 1) with a one-step CRR tree. Give u, d, q, the price and the hedge ratio.
    Check
    • u = e0.2 = 1.2214, d = 1/u = 0.8187
    • q = (e0.05 − 0.8187) / (1.2214 − 0.8187) = 0.5775
    • C = e−0.05 × 0.5775 × 22.14 = 12.1623, the N = 1 row of the convergence table (Black–Scholes 10.4506)
    • Δ = 22.14 / (122.14 − 81.87) = 0.5498 shares

    Missed it? Reopen binomial pricing, "The rule for one step of a tree".

  3. which concept?Your Monte Carlo pricer's sanity check prints E[ST] = 107.25 for the shared stock under Q. The payoff code is correct. Which idea did the path generator get wrong, and what should the check print?
    Check
    • the Itô correction: the log drift must be r − σ²/2 = 0.03, not r = 0.05
    • without it E[ST] = 100·e0.05 + 0.02 = 107.25; with it, 100·e0.05 = 105.13
    • every price from that generator is biased high

    Missed it? Reopen Brownian motion and GBM, "Why the σ²/2? Two views", and the estimator on Monte Carlo pricing.

  4. You sold 100 of the shared calls and hold 63.68 shares as a delta hedge. The stock jumps straight to 105 before you can re-hedge. What is your P&L, and which Greek predicted its sign?
    Check
    • per call, the hedged book loses 0.2231 (delta predicts +3.1842 on the shares, the call rises 3.4073)
    • × 100 = about −22.31
    • gamma: a short option is short curvature, so a big move either way loses against a linear hedge

    Missed it? Reopen Black–Scholes and the Greeks, "Checks: finite differences and a delta hedge".

  5. which concept?A gambler with $100 wants to reach $200 on a bet that wins 49% of the time at even money. A friend says "bet $1 at a time, so the luck evens out". Which idea says that is the worst plan, and what are the two odds?
    Check
    • drift vs noise: with a negative edge, more bets give the drift more time to win
    • $1 at a time: 1/(1 + r¹⁰⁰) with r = 51/49, ≈ 0.0180, over about 4,820 bets
    • bold play, $100 at once: 0.49

    Missed it? Reopen random walks, the ruin table and "Is a random walk the right model for prices?".

  6. With 10,000 paths the shared call's Monte Carlo standard error is 0.1462. How many paths do you need for a standard error of 0.01, and what is the cheaper way to get the same answer for this particular option?
    Check
    • s ≈ 0.1462 × √10⁴ = 14.62, and n = (14.62 / 0.01)² ≈ 2.1 × 10⁶
    • error falls like 1/√n, so 14.62× less error costs about 214× the paths
    • for a European call, Black–Scholes gives 10.4506 exactly in O(1)

    Missed it? Reopen Monte Carlo pricing, "More paths: the error bar shrinks like 1/√n".

  7. Someone offers to buy the two-reroll dice game from you for 4.50. Should you sell? Name the step it shares with pricing an American put.
    Check
    • V₂ = 14/3 ≈ 4.667, so selling at 4.50 gives away about 0.167 a play
    • backward induction: at each stage take max(keep this face, value of rolling on)
    • the American put does the same at every node: max(exercise now, value of holding), with q-weights and discounting instead of 1/6 each

    Missed it? Reopen EV games and market making, the trace, and binomial pricing, the american step in the code.

3 · Project: price one put four ways, then hedge the call

What it reinforces: exact GBM stepping and the Itô drift (GBM), risk-neutral pricing by tree, formula and simulation (binomial, Black–Scholes, Monte Carlo), early exercise as backward induction (EV games), and √n scaling of noise (random walks).

contract S0 = K = 100, r = 5%, sigma = 20%, T = 1 (the course's shared one) put payoff max(K - S_T, 0) hedge sell 1 call at the Black-Scholes price, hold N(d1) shares, rebalance m times a year, bank the cash at r

Reuse the course's code files. In about 40 lines:

  1. Formula. Black–Scholes put, and check put–call parity against the call.
  2. Tree. CRR with N = 1,000 for the European put and the American put. How much is early exercise worth?
  3. Simulation. Monte Carlo put with 10⁵ paths (seed 0) with its standard error; then with 50,000 antithetic pairs. Is the put's variance reduction as good as the call's?
  4. Hedge. Simulate 10,000 real-world GBM paths (seed 1) with μ = 8% and again with μ = 20%. Delta-hedge a short call with m = 12, 52 and 252 rebalances and report the mean and standard deviation of the final P&L.
Done when: you have the four put prices with error bars where they apply, the early-exercise premium, and a 3 × 2 table of hedge P&L. You can say in one sentence why the mean hedge P&L barely depends on μ, and in another why its spread halves when you rebalance about four times as often. Then compare with the check.
Check: the numbers (computed with the course's code and a short Python script)
  • Black–Scholes put 5.5735; parity 10.4506 − 5.5735 = 4.8771 = 100 − 100e−0.05.
  • CRR, N = 1,000: European 5.5715, American 6.0896, so early exercise is worth about 0.52.
  • Monte Carlo, 10⁵ paths: 5.5772 ± 0.0274. Antithetic, 50,000 pairs: 5.5893 ± 0.0209, a variance ratio of about 1.72, less than the call's 2.00 on its card: how much pairing helps depends on the payoff's shape in Z, as the straddle in the Monte Carlo card's study questions shows.
  • Hedge P&L (mean, sd) with μ = 8%: m = 12 → (0.01, 1.88); m = 52 → (0.00, 0.94); m = 252 → (0.01, 0.43). With μ = 20%: (−0.14, 1.75), (−0.03, 0.86), (0.00, 0.39). The means sit near zero and shrink with m, whatever μ is: the hedge, not the drift, sets the price. The spread falls like 1/√m (4.3× the rebalances → about half the sd), the random-walk card's √n law.
