Simple vs log returns

Simple return is also called the arithmetic, percentage or holding-period return. Log return is also called the continuously compounded return. Every return series in finance starts with this choice: simple returns for reporting and portfolios, log returns for adding up over time and for volatility.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Percentages apply to a moving base, so +50% then −50% doesn't cancel: the price ends at 75. Average simple returns and you overstate how fast money actually grows, by about σ²/2 per period.

Where it fits: Second card of the money part. Time value of money compounded at a known rate; this card compounds uncertain returns, and its σ²/2 gap reappears in GBM (median vs mean) and Kelly (overbetting).

Builds on: Time value of money · Sums and the central limit theorem

You'll meet: simple return R = P₁/P₀ − 1 log return r = ln(1 + R) arithmetic vs geometric mean volatility drag ≈ σ²/2 annualizing: ×252 for means, ×√252 for sd

By the end: Chain returns correctly over time, combine them correctly across assets, and predict the gap between average return and compounded growth from σ.

In one breath

A simple return is the percentage change, P₁/P₀ − 1. A log return is ln(P₁/P₀), the same move measured on a log scale. Over time you must multiply simple returns (as growth factors), but you can just add log returns. And because the average of simple returns overstates growth by about σ²/2, a stock that goes +50% then −50% has an average return of 0% but lost 25%.

For small moves the two are nearly equal: +1% simple is ln 1.01 = 0.995% log. They split apart for big moves: +50% is 40.5% log, −50% is −69.3% log.

Checked on a simulated path

2,520 trading days (10 years) of normal daily log returns, mean 0.0003 and sd 0.02, seed 7 (illustrative parameters). The price goes from 100 to 304.68.

arithmetic mean of R 6.436 bp/day geometric mean 4.422 bp/day = mean of r (4.421) as ln(1+g) gap 2.014 bp/day σ²/2 = 0.020068² / 2 2.014 bp/day ✓ arith ≈ geo + σ²/2 annualize (252 days): mean log 0.000442 × 252 = 11.14% a year sd 0.02007 × √252 = 31.86% a year drag 252 × σ²/2 = 5.07% a year

Means scale with time (252×) but standard deviations scale with its square root (√252 ≈ 15.87×), because the variances of independent days add. That's only true for log returns, which add; it assumes days are independent, which real prices only roughly are.

In Python

import math, statistics as st

def simple_returns(p):
    return [b / a - 1 for a, b in zip(p, p[1:])]

def log_returns(p):
    return [math.log(b / a) for a, b in zip(p, p[1:])]

def geometric_mean(rets):          # the CAGR per period
    growth = math.prod(1 + r for r in rets)
    return growth ** (1 / len(rets)) - 1

p = [100, 150, 75]
R, r = simple_returns(p), log_returns(p)
print(sum(R), math.prod(1 + x for x in R) - 1)
print(round(sum(r), 6), round(math.log(0.75), 6))
print(round(geometric_mean(R), 4), st.pvariance(R) / 2)
print(round(0.01 * math.sqrt(252), 4))   # 1% daily sd, annual
# → 0.0 -0.25
#   -0.287682 -0.287682
#   -0.134 0.125
#   0.1587

Time O(n) · Space O(n) for n prices: one division (and one log) per step. The full file also simulates the 2,520-day path and prints the annualized numbers above.

Why it beats the alternatives

For chaining returns over time:

✗ Add simple returns

+50% − 50% = 0%, but you lost 25%. Each percentage applies to a different base, so the pieces don't add.

✗ Compound the average

Average 0% per period predicts 100 → 100. The true per-period rate is −13.4%. Over 10 years of the simulated path, 16.2% a year vs the real 11.8%.

✗ Dollar changes

+50 then −75. Depends on the price level, so you can't compare a $10 stock with a $1,000 one, or this year with last.

✓ Log returns

Just add: −0.2877 = ln 0.75, and ex − 1 converts back. Sums of many small independent pieces are near-normal (CLT), which is why prices get modelled as lognormal.

So always use log returns?

No. Across assets on the same day, simple returns are the ones that combine: a portfolio's return is the weighted sum of its assets' simple returns. Half in a +50% stock and half in a −50% stock is exactly 0%; averaging their log returns and converting back gives −13.4%, which is wrong.

Simple returns are also what people report (fund statements, "up 3% today") and what the arithmetic mean should use when you want the expected return of one period. Rule of thumb: log returns over time, simple returns across assets. For daily moves of 1 or 2% the two differ in the third decimal, so the choice matters for long horizons and big moves, not for one quiet day.

Understand and remember

  • A percentage applies to the current price → successive simple returns act on different bases → they multiply as (1 + R) factors → take logs and the factors become a sum: log returns add over time
  • Returns fluctuate → the up moves and down moves don't cancel in growth terms (1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75) → the geometric mean sits below the arithmetic mean by ≈ σ²/2 → averaging simple returns overstates compounded growth (16.2% vs 11.8% a year on the simulated path)
  • Independent daily log returns → their variances add → sd over N days = daily sd × √N while the mean grows × N → annual vol = daily sd × √252 → Sharpe ratios and option vols are quoted per √time
  • A portfolio's value on one day is a weighted sum of asset values → simple returns combine linearly across assets → rule: log returns over time, simple returns across assets
  • detail that matters Log returns are unbounded both ways while the price stays positive → sums of many small independent log returns are near-normal (CLT) → prices are modelled as lognormal, which is the GBM card's model

  • the 2,520-day path's exact figures (304.68, 4.422 bp/day)
  • 252 trading days as the convention

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Brownian motion and geometric Brownian motion: The σ²/2 here separates the arithmetic and geometric mean; in GBM it separates the mean and median price. Is it the same gap? What causes it in each?

Check
  • same cause: Jensen, E[eX] > eE[X] for a random X
  • here: averaging (1 + R) overstates the growth of the product
  • in GBM: the mean price grows at μ but the log (and so the median) at μ − σ²/2
  • both vanish as σ → 0 and grow with σ²

vs The Kelly criterion: Volatility drag says swings cost growth; Kelly says betting more increases swings. How does this card's σ²/2 explain why 2× Kelly has zero or negative growth?

Check
  • staking f raises the mean return linearly (edge × f)
  • it raises the variance with f², and growth ≈ mean − variance/2
  • at about 2f* the drag term equals the gain (for the 60/40 coin, g turns negative past 0.389)

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

A stock alternates +10% and −10% every day for 20 days. Before computing: what is the average simple return, what is the total change, and does the loss grow if the moves become ±20%? Commit before revealing.

Reveal

The average simple return is exactly 0%, but each up-down pair multiplies by 1.1 × 0.9 = 0.99. After 10 pairs, 0.99¹⁰ = 0.9044: a 9.56% loss (log sum 10 × ln 0.99 = −0.1005, close to σ²/2 × 20 = 0.1). With ±20% each pair is 0.96, so the loss roughly quadruples: drag scales with σ², not σ.
