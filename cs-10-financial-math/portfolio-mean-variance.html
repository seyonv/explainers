Portfolio mean and variance

Also called Markowitz mean-variance analysis, modern portfolio theory, or "diversification maths". The source guide lists Financial Mathematics with no page, so this card uses one running pair: asset A with σ = 20%, asset B with σ = 30%, correlation ρ = 0.2, and expected returns of 8% and 12% (illustrative).

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: If you treat risk like return and average it, a 50/50 mix of a 20% and a 30% volatility asset looks like 25% risk. It is really 19.62%, and some mixes are safer than either asset alone. Without the cross term you can't see diversification or which holdings are never worth owning.

Where it fits: First card of the portfolio part. The returns card taught that simple returns combine across assets as a weighted sum; this card works out what that weighted sum does to variance. The Sharpe card then adds a risk-free asset.

In one breath

A portfolio's expected return is just the weighted average of its assets' returns, but its risk is not: the variance picks up a cross term that shrinks as the assets move less in step. So unless the correlation is exactly +1, a mix can be less risky than either asset held alone, and the mix with the least risk has a closed-form weight.

So the least risky mix is 73.58% in A and 26.42% in B: σ = √0.03260 = 18.06%, below A's own 20%. Its mean is 0.7358 × 8% + 0.2642 × 12% = 9.06%.

In Python

import math

def portfolio(w, mu1, mu2, s1, s2, rho):
    """Mean and sigma with weight w in asset 1 and 1 - w in asset 2."""
    mean = w * mu1 + (1 - w) * mu2
    var = (w * s1) ** 2 + ((1 - w) * s2) ** 2 \
        + 2 * w * (1 - w) * rho * s1 * s2
    return mean, math.sqrt(var)

def min_var_weight(s1, s2, rho):
    cov = rho * s1 * s2
    return (s2 ** 2 - cov) / (s1 ** 2 + s2 ** 2 - 2 * cov)

def min_var_n(cov):
    """n assets: w = inv(Cov) @ 1, rescaled so the weights sum to 1."""
    import numpy as np
    x = np.linalg.solve(cov, np.ones(len(cov)))
    return x / x.sum()

w = min_var_weight(0.20, 0.30, 0.2)
m, s = portfolio(w, 0.08, 0.12, 0.20, 0.30, 0.2)
print(f"{w:.4f} {m:.2%} {s:.2%}")    # rho = 0.2
# → 0.7358 9.06% 18.06%
print(f"{min_var_weight(0.20, 0.30, 1.0):.4f}")    # rho = +1
# → 3.0000   (short B: outside long-only 0..1)
print(min_var_n([[0.04, 0.012], [0.012, 0.09]]).round(4))
# → [0.7358 0.2642]

The __main__ demo in code/ also prints the sweep table and the ρ = 0 and −1 cases. Time O(1) for two assets; O(n³) for n assets, because solving against the n × n covariance matrix dominates. The n-asset rule is the same idea in matrix form and gives the same 0.7358 here.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ All in the high-return asset

All B earns 12% but carries 30% volatility. Moving 25% to A cuts risk to 24.01% while giving up only 1 point of return.

✗ All in the "safe" asset

All A (8%, 20%) feels cautious but is dominated: the 50/50 mix gets 10% at 19.62%, more return and less risk.

✗ Averaging the volatilities

Treats risk like return and says 50/50 has σ = 25%. That's only true at ρ = +1; at ρ = 0.2 it overstates risk by 5.38 points.

✓ Mean-variance with covariance

Uses the cross term, so it finds mixes safer than either asset (18.06% at w* = 0.7358) and shows which mixes are never worth holding.

Should you just run the optimiser?

Not blindly. The inputs are estimates, and the optimiser trusts them fully: small errors in expected returns can swing the "optimal" weights a lot. DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) found that none of 14 optimised models consistently beat a plain equal-weight (1/N) portfolio out of sample. The minimum-variance portfolio needs no expected returns at all, which (our inference) is part of why it tends to be steadier in practice than return-seeking mixes.

Variance also counts upside and downside swings the same and says nothing about fat tails, and correlations measured in calm markets tend to rise in sell-offs (Longin and Solnik 2001), which shrinks the cross-term benefit exactly when you want it. With a risk-free asset in the mix, the best risky portfolio is the one with the highest Sharpe ratio, not the minimum-variance one: see the Sharpe and CAPM card.

Understand and remember

  • Portfolio return is w·RA + (1−w)·RB → its mean is linear in w and ignores ρ → but its variance squares the sum → a cross term 2w(1−w)ρσAσB appears → only at ρ = +1 is portfolio σ the weighted average of the σs
  • ρ < 1 → the cross term is smaller than in the "average the vols" picture → the risk curve bows left of both assets → there is a mix safer than either (18.06% at w* = 0.7358) → diversification is a free reduction in risk
  • Minimize σp² in w → set the derivative to 0 → w* = (σB² − cov)/(σA² + σB² − 2cov) → it needs no expected returns → which is why the minimum-variance portfolio is steadier to estimate than return-seeking ones
  • Mean-variance optimisation trusts its inputs fully → small errors in expected returns move the weights a lot → optimised portfolios often fail to beat 1/N out of sample (DeMiguel et al.: none of 14 consistently)
  • detail that matters For n assets the rule is w ∝ Σ−1·1 → solving against the n × n covariance matrix is O(n³) → and estimating Σ's n(n+1)/2 entries from limited data is the real bottleneck

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

You hold N equal-weighted stocks, each with σ = 20%, and every pair has correlation 0.3. As N grows from 10 to 100 to a million, what does the portfolio's σ approach? Commit before revealing.

Reveal

It floors at about 20% × √0.3 = 10.95%, not zero: 12.17% at N = 10, 11.08% at N = 100, 10.95% at N = 10⁶. Diversification removes each stock's own risk (the 1/N term) but not the shared part (the cross terms, which don't shrink). That shared part is the market risk the Sharpe/CAPM card says is the only risk that's paid for.
