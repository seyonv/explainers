Study path · overview

The LLM inference study path

Why an LLM answers as fast as it does, from the wait you see as a user down to measuring a real server on a rented GPU. Five days, taken in order. Each one answers a single question, and each question only makes sense once the one before it is answered.

The thread: one question per day
Day 1Why does a response take as long as it does?Day 1.5What is the model actually computing?Day 2What does the GPU do on each step, and why does a server batch?Day 3How fast is one step, and how many users fit on a chip?Day 4How does a real server behave under load, and how do you know?Days 5–9How do real engines and many GPUs do it, and what does a token cost?

Day 1 names the terms of a response's time. Day 1.5 opens up the model those terms come from. Day 2 shows what the model does to a chip, and why serving many users at once helps. Day 3 turns that into numbers you can compute. Day 4 checks those numbers against a real server. After that, each later day zooms into one piece.

How every day works
  1. Each day comes in two versions with shared ticks: a one-page version (every card inline, just scroll) and a checklist.
  2. Every phase runs the same routine: prime (read what each card will let you do), pretest (try the checkpoint cold), read in order (then do each card's “Study this card” box), and take the checkpoint for real.
  3. Each step is tagged with how to treat it: Skim Read Pretest Checkpoint Optional Task.
  4. “Go deeper” sources stay closed unless you missed a card's questions.
Day 1

Why a response takes as long as it does

~5 h · 30 steps · 5 phases

Why does a response take as long as it does?

Day 1 starts from what you can see as a user, the wait for an answer, and works inward. It needs no ML or GPU background. Every later day explains one term of the formula you learn today.

After it, you'll be able to

  • Break any LLM response time into time to first token + tokens ÷ speed, repeated for every call, and say which term dominates.
  • Explain why writing tokens is capped by memory bandwidth, and how hardware, quantization, MoE and speculative decoding each get around it.
  • Say when prompt caching helps a lot and when it barely matters.
  • Explain why an agent takes minutes rather than seconds: thinking tokens, many calls and a growing history.
  • Reason about time to a correct result: tail latency, retries, compounding errors, parallel calls and model routing.
  • Rank the levers for a real request: cut tokens first, pick the host second, buy hardware last.

What it uses

  • LLM speed and reliability course. The backbone of today. It starts from the request and its wait, so you can follow it without knowing how a model works, and it gives you the formula the later days zoom into.
  • The Day 1 drawing. A map to hang each card on. Its numbered boxes say which card explains which part, and Day 1.5 fills in the lettered ones.
  • Serving and Agents field guides: the principles. You skim the Serving rules first to see the whole field, and check yourself against the Agents rules at the end. They turn today's numbers into decisions real teams make.
  • Horace He, “Making Deep Learning Go Brrrr”. The clearest explanation of why memory, not maths, caps how fast a model writes. It's the one outside read not to skip today; it appears right after the memory-bandwidth card.
  • Three checkpoints. You try each one cold first, so you know what to look for, then for real, to find which cards to reread.
The 5 phases
  1. Prime the whole field ~25 min · 5 steps
    Skim only. You're building the map that everything later hangs on, not learning details yet.
  2. What sets the speed of one call ~2 h · 12 steps
    Metrics first, then the root cause (memory bandwidth), then four ways around it, then a shorter start.
  3. What multiplies the tokens ~1 h · 4 steps
    Three multipliers, which are why an agent takes minutes rather than seconds.
  4. Agents: time to a correct result ~1.5 h · 7 steps
    From speed to correctness: slow calls, failures, compounding errors, then two design fixes.
  5. Consolidate ~15 min · 2 steps
    Close the cards and check today against the Agents guide's rules for building agents.

Day 1.5

Just enough about the model

~2 h · 18 steps · 6 phases · For readers new to ML

What is the model actually computing?

Day 2 opens up what the model computes on a GPU. If you've never seen what's inside a model, today gives you that picture with the gentlest cards in the hub, and nothing more. There's no training maths, and you skip every code block.

After it, you'll be able to

  • Say what a language model is: a function that gives a probability to every possible next token. Generating text is calling it in a loop.
  • Explain what a token is, and why tokenization makes numbers, spelling and non-English text behave oddly.
  • Trace one forward pass (token ids → embeddings → attention and MLP blocks → logits → sampling) and say in one sentence what each part does.
  • Say what “the weights” are, and why a whole model is one file of learned numbers plus a fixed recipe.
  • Explain how pretraining makes a base model, and how fine-tuning and RL turn it into an assistant that follows instructions and thinks first.
  • Point at each of these on the Day 1 drawing, and say which ones set the speed: the weights and the KV cache.

What it uses

  • Zero to Hero cards. They explain each part of a GPT from zero, with pictures, which suits a first look. You read them only for the ideas.
  • Nucleus sampling card. Sampling is the last box of every decode step on the Day 1 drawing. This is the plain-language version.
  • Models field guide: two stations and three principles. You read only the intros and questions. You need the map of how a base model becomes an assistant, and where Day 1's thinking tokens come from, not the training details.
  • Your five sentences. Comparing the two versions shows you what you actually learned. This page keeps both in your browser.
  • The Day 1 drawing, bands 1 and 3. Today fills in the boxes you couldn't explain on Day 1. You finish by redrawing band 3 from memory.
The 6 phases
  1. Prime ~15 min · 3 steps
    Look at the whole thing once, then find out what you can already say.
  2. What a language model is ~30 min · 2 steps
    One idea carries everything: predict the next token, then do it again.
  3. Tokens ~20 min · 2 steps
    The model never sees letters. This is where the numbers come from, and what every token count on Day 1 was counting.
  4. Inside one forward pass ~40 min · 5 steps
    Band 3 of the drawing, one box at a time. Read for what each part does, not how to code it.
  5. From base model to assistant ~25 min · 3 steps
    Band 1 of the drawing. Why a model that only continues text ends up answering questions, and where thinking tokens come from.
  6. Consolidate ~10 min · 3 steps
    Close everything.

Day 2

Inside the GPU and the engine loop

~5 h · 31 steps · 5 phases

What does the GPU do on each step, and why does a server batch?

Day 1 told you that writing speed is capped by memory bandwidth. Day 2 shows why on a real chip, then why a server batches many users together to get past that cap.

After it, you'll be able to

  • Count Llama-3.1-8B's 8.03B parameters from its config, and explain why every decode step reads all 16 GB of them.
  • Compute a small attention by hand, and explain why the number of KV heads, not query heads, sets the cache size.
  • Sketch an H100 from memory, and classify any operation as compute- or memory-bound from two numbers.
  • Measure prefill and decode on your own Mac, and back out the effective memory bandwidth.
  • Compute TTFT, TPOT, throughput and goodput for a request, and explain why goodput sets the cost.
  • Explain why batching is the biggest serving lever, what stops it scaling forever, and how static → dynamic → continuous batching → chunked prefill each fix the previous step's problem.

What it uses

  • perf-0: the map. For orientation only: one map from GPU price to cost per token, so you know where today's two courses sit. You skim it, and keep its glossary open all day.
  • perf-1 Foundations. What one token costs: the parameters, attention and FLOPs, then the GPU, the roofline and the serving metrics. Its one formula, step time = max(maths, memory), is what every later day builds on.
  • Request scheduler course, Part 1. It explains batching, the biggest serving lever, as a chain: each kind of batching fixes the problem the previous one leaves.
  • Serving field guide: reference architecture, stations 1 and 3. They check each half of the day against real systems (a production deployment and vLLM's engine), so what you learn isn't only theory.
  • Labs. Optional. Run one when a card's numbers feel abstract. Prefill vs decode on your Mac is the one most worth doing.
The 5 phases
  1. Prime the day ~30 min · 5 steps
    Two maps and one trace. Skim for shape; don't try to learn the numbers yet.
  2. What the model computes ~1 h · 5 steps
    The parameters, the attention and the FLOPs behind one token.
  3. What the machine does, and what you measure ~1.25 h · 6 steps
    The chip, the roofline and the four serving metrics. Roofline lands now because you know what a decode step reads.
  4. Why and how a server batches ~2 h · 12 steps
    Part 1 of the scheduler course. Each batching card fixes the problem the previous one leaves.
  5. Consolidate ~20 min · 3 steps
    Close everything and rebuild the day from memory.

Day 3

The math of serving

~6 h · 25 steps · 5 phases

How fast is one step, and how many users fit on a chip?

Day 2 gave you the intuition: decode reads memory, and batching spreads the reads over many users. Day 3 turns that into numbers you can compute: how long one step takes, how many users fit, and how many chips a model needs.

After it, you'll be able to

  • Explain why caching K and V is exact, and why a 183× cut in FLOPs becomes only a 3.6× cut in time.
  • Compute any operation's arithmetic intensity, compare it with the chip's ridge point, and derive the critical batch size.
  • Fill in the step-time formula for any model, chip, batch and context, and predict where throughput flattens.
  • Count a model's parameters and KV bytes from its shapes, and work out how many sequences fit on given hardware.
  • Explain why throughput ∝ 1 ÷ KV bytes per token once memory is full, and rank the tricks that shrink the KV cache.
  • Choose a sharding for prefill and for generation, and size a Llama-3-70B deployment end to end.
  • Build a capacity calculator on paper and check it against a real server's numbers.

What it uses

  • perf-2: the Scaling Book inference course. The most rigorous serving maths that's still readable. The course reproduces the chapter's numbers one step at a time.
  • The Scaling Book chapter itself. Open it when a card compresses a derivation too far.
  • Serving principles 3 and 8, and station 2. The principles tell you this morning what you're about to prove. Station 2 checks the step-time formula against real measurements (gpt-fast going from 25 to 245 tok/s).
  • Serving project 1: napkin to meter. You read it at the start and do its paper half at the end. Predicting a real server's capacity before you see its numbers is the proof the day worked.
  • The chapter's worked problems. Do two from a blank page. Derivations only stick once you've redone them yourself.
The 5 phases
  1. Prime the day ~20 min · 4 steps
    One map, two principles and the project you'll finish with.
  2. What one decode step costs ~2 h · 8 steps
    Six cards that build the step-time formula one term at a time.
  3. Memory decides the batch ~1.25 h · 4 steps
    Why KV bytes, not FLOPs, set how many users one chip can serve.
  4. From one chip to a fleet ~1.5 h · 7 steps
    Sharding, engine design and one full deployment sized end to end. The Day 4 lab uses one GPU and doesn't need this phase, so if you're heading straight to the lab you can do this phase after it.
  5. Apply: napkin to meter ~1–2 h · 2 steps
    Prove it. Predict a real server's capacity before you look at its numbers.

Day 4

Measure a real server

~4 h + the lab · 26 steps · 6 phases

How does a real server behave under load, and how do you know?

Days 1 to 3 explained why a server behaves as it does and let you predict it on paper. Day 4 is where you measure one. The reading is short and covers only what the lab needs that earlier days didn't: how to load-test, why the curve bends, and what to watch while it runs.

After it, you'll be able to

  • Design a load test: a fixed, realistic workload, a warm-up, repeats, and the difference between holding the number of requests in flight fixed and holding the request rate fixed.
  • Explain why the wait grows much faster than the load near saturation, and use Little's law to turn requests in flight into a request rate.
  • Predict where the curve bends and which limit breaks first, before running anything.
  • Read the engine's own counters during a run, and tell a client that isn't sending load from a server that is out of memory or queueing.
  • Say why the lab runs on a dedicated GPU pod and not a serverless platform, and what each costs.
  • Serve an 8B model with vLLM behind an API, produce its latency-throughput curve with repeats, mark the knee and name the metric that breaks first.

What it uses

  • Serving project 2: serving lab v1. The point of the day. You read its brief first, so every card answers a line of it, and you do it last.
  • Benchmarking a server, and Measuring a server. They hold the load-testing method: what to send, warm-up and repeats, and the difference between a test that waits for replies and one that doesn't. The lab's numbers mean nothing without them.
  • Queueing and the knee. The lab asks you to mark the knee of the curve and say what breaks first. This card is the explanation: waiting grows much faster than load.
  • Reading the server's counters. The lab asks you to plot the engine's own counters. This card says which ones matter and what each reading means, so you can tell a client problem from a memory limit from a queue.
  • Modal's two pages. A second opinion from outside the field guide. They sweep the request rate where the lab sweeps users, and you should be able to say why.
  • Serving failures F03, F05, F14 and F21. Each is a way the lab goes wrong. Reading them first means you recognise the symptom when it happens to you.
  • Serving warm-ups. Rungs A to C are the cheap practice runs from Days 1 to 3. Today uses the page for where to run the lab and for the two checks to pass before the full sweep.
The 6 phases
  1. Prime the day ~25 min · 7 steps
    The lab's brief, the five rules it tests, and a guess you'll check tonight.
  2. How to load-test ~1.25 h · 6 steps
    What to send, how to send it, and how to know the numbers can be trusted.
  3. Why the curve has a knee ~50 min · 3 steps
    The one idea the lab's plot is about: waiting grows faster than load.
  4. What to watch while it runs ~50 min · 3 steps
    The server's side of the story. A number from the load generator is only trustworthy when the engine's counters agree with it.
  5. Where it runs ~35 min · 3 steps
    Which machine, which platform and what it costs, before you pay for any of it.
  6. Apply: the load-curve lab ~12 h · 4 steps
    Predict, check the setup on two points, then run the whole sweep.
