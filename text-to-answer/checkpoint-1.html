Checkpoint: the whole route

Ties together the whole route, embeddings, positions, the feed-forward part and experts, decoding and the loop: one question, What is the capital of India?, from 29 characters to ␣The capital of India is New Delhi. on Qwen2.5-0.5B.

What this ties together. The six cards each zoom in on a stop or two of the same route. Here you put the stops back in order and check that you can say, without looking, what goes into each one, what comes out, and which choice at that stop changes the answer.

How to use this page. Close the cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. Where a question asks you to predict a number, commit to a rough value first; the direction and the reason matter more than the digits. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure: the Check names the card and section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so part of the work is picking the right idea.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. mechanismWalk our question from text to the first answer token. Name each stop in order and give the shape of the data after it, up to the token the greedy decoder picks.
    Check
    • tokenize: 29 characters → 7 tokens; token IDs: [3838, 374, 279, 6722, 315, 6747, 30]
    • embed: each ID picks one row of the 151,936 × 896 table → 7 × 896
    • positions, attention and feed-forward form one block, run 24 times; the shape stays 7 × 896 throughout (RoPE acts inside attention, with no table)
    • logits: only the last row is multiplied by the same embedding table (tied weights) → 151,936 scores, top ␣The at 16.52
    • softmax → probabilities that sum to 1: ␣The 0.277, ␣India 0.134; greedy picks ␣The, and the loop appends it and runs again

    Missed it? Reopen the whole route, "Every stop has a shape you can check".

  2. predictIn Qwen's embedding table, put these in order from most to least similar, and guess roughly where each lands: ␣India·␣China, ␣India·␣capital, ␣India·␣banana, and the average of two random rows. Then: a teammate compares "river bank" and "money bank" sentences by averaging their raw input rows. Why are they too alike, and where should they look instead?
    Check
    • India·China 0.449, then the random-pair average 0.153, then India·banana 0.013 and India·capital 0.009
    • read cosines against the 0.153 baseline, not 0: India and capital share our sentence, yet their rows are less alike than two random rows. Rows record how tokens are used, not facts
    • the table has one row per token whatever the sentence, so ␣bank adds an identical row to both (cosine 1.000 before any block)
    • after 6 of the 24 blocks, river bank · river bank is 0.849 while river bank · money bank drops to 0.494: use vectors after the blocks, where attention has mixed in the neighbours

    Missed it? Reopen embeddings, the cosine table after "An ID has no meaningful distance", and the failure box "one row for every meaning of a word".

  3. predictFeed the 7 token vectors to Qwen's first attention layer, shuffled to India of capital the is What ?, with no position encoding and no causal mask. How much does each token's output change? Now switch the causal mask on, still with no positions. How many of the 7 outputs stay the same, and which?
    Check
    • no positions, no mask: nothing changes. Every token gets the output it had, just in a different slot; the largest difference is 0.0000014, float rounding
    • why: each score is a dot product of two tokens' contents, and the weighted sum treats the tokens as a set
    • with the mask on: only 1 stays the same, ?, because it is last in both orders and sees all 7 either way. What sees only itself in the original order but six tokens when shuffled to slot 5
    • so the mask leaks which tokens came before, but not their order or distance: position encodings are still needed

    Missed it? Reopen positions, the four-row table under "Why: every score compares two contents" and the box "Why the demo turns the causal mask off".

  4. mechanismExplain how RoPE puts order back. A query at slot 105 and a key at slot 103 get the same score as at slots 5 and 3: why? And why does each head split its 64 numbers into 32 pairs turning at different speeds instead of using one pair?
    Check
    • RoPE turns the query by its slot × θ and the key by its slot × θ; a dot product depends only on lengths and the angle between them, which shifts by (105 − 103) × θ, the same as (5 − 3) × θ
    • measured in layer 0: 1.598 both times; one slot closer the score changes to 1.879
    • one pair wraps round: at 24.16° per slot it is back almost where it started after 15 slots (362°), so it can't tell a gap of 0 from a gap of 15
    • 32 pairs from fast (a full turn every 6.3 positions) to very slow (every 4,080,185) work like clock hands. RoPE turns queries and keys, never values, and adds no weights

    Missed it? Reopen positions, "RoPE: turn the query and key, and only the gap is left".

  5. predictFor our 7-token question, rank these by FLOPs per token, biggest first: the feed-forward parts, the output layer that scores the vocabulary, the attention projections, and the attention scores themselves. Then say which one grows with the length of the text, and roughly when it catches the leader.
    Check
    • feed-forward 627.6 M, output layer (the shared embedding table) 272.3 M, attention projections 88.1 M, attention scores 0.6 M
    • a matrix multiply costs 2 FLOPs per weight per token, which is why the feed-forward part, with 313,786,368 of the 494,032,768 weights (63.5%), leads
    • only the attention scores grow with context, because each token is compared with every earlier one; at 7,296 tokens of context they cost as much as the feed-forward part
    • short chats are feed-forward-bound; long documents shift the balance

    Missed it? Reopen the feed-forward part, "Two-thirds of the weights and the arithmetic sit in the FFN".

  6. which concept?A teammate gives every layer of Qwen2.5-0.5B 8 copies of its feed-forward part plus a router that sends each token to one copy. Predict: the total weights, the weights each token actually uses, and the memory in bfloat16 (2 bytes per weight). Then: a Switch layer gets 800 tokens with 8 experts and a capacity factor of 1.25, and 200 tokens pick the same expert. What happens to them?
    Check
    • total 2.69 billion: only the feed-forward part is copied, so about 0.365 + 8 × 0.635 ≈ 5.45 times the original, not 8 times
    • used per token: 494.2 million, barely more than before (the routers add 0.2 million), because each token still runs one feed-forward part per layer
    • memory grows from 0.99 GB to 5.38 GB, because every expert must be loaded: weights and compute are separate dials, and memory pays for the weights
    • capacity = 800 ÷ 8 × 1.25 = 125 tokens, so 125 are processed and 75 are dropped: they skip the layer and ride through on the residual stream (computed here from the card's formula)

    Missed it? Reopen the feed-forward part, "If the FFN is most of the model, keep many and use one at a time", and its check question 2.

  7. predictAfter The capital of India is, the model gives ␣New 0.698 and ␣Delhi 0.164. Predict ␣New's probability at temperature 0.7 and at 1.5, and how many tokens top-p keeps at p = 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95. Why does temperature 0.01 act like greedy?
    Check
    • T = 0.7: ␣New rises to 0.869. T = 1.5: it falls to 0.289, and the tail outside the top 8 grows to 0.484, nearly half
    • top-p keeps 2 tokens at 0.8, 5 at 0.9 and 12 at 0.95: the kept set grows when the model is unsure
    • dividing logits by a tiny T multiplies the gaps between them, so the top token takes all the probability; temperature reweights but never removes a token
    • a logit gap of 1.45 (17.28 vs 15.83) is already a factor of e1.45 ≈ 4.3 in probability

    Missed it? Reopen decoding, "Temperature reshapes the same scores before the draw" and the softmax-by-hand box.

  8. trade-offChoose a decoder for each job and give the reason: (a) a short factual answer, (b) 100 candidate programs to run against tests, (c) a translation, (d) a short story. Then explain why greedy, the "safest" choice, wrecked our 400-token run.
    Check
    • (a) greedy or a low temperature: one right answer, and variety only adds ways to be wrong
    • (b) sampling at a higher temperature: pass@100 counts any one success, and greedy would return the same program 100 times (Codex's best T for pass@100 was 0.8)
    • (c) beam search: the output is pinned down by the input, so finding the most probable whole answer pays off
    • (d) top-p around 0.9–0.95 with T near 1: variety without the unreliable tail; top-p 0.95 came closest to human text in Holtzman et al.'s Table 1
    • greedy always takes the most probable continuation, and a phrase gets more probable each time it repeats; from token 225 on, our run repeated one 46-token block. In the paper, greedy text repeated 73.66% of the time

    Missed it? Reopen decoding, "Pick the decoder for the job" and "Over long outputs, the safest choice turns into a loop".

  9. mechanismMost tokens of the greedy answer were above 0.9. Walk through how to get the probability of the whole answer, and predict roughly what it is. Why does code add logs instead? And why did a Q: … A: wrapper change the first step so much on this model?
    Check
    • the chain rule: P(answer | question) is the product of each step's probability given everything before it
    • 0.277 × 0.964 × 0.919 × 0.994 × 0.969 × 0.698 × 0.994 × 0.680 ≈ 0.111; including the decision to stop (0.682), 0.076. The first token, at 0.277, pulls it down most
    • logs: products of many numbers below 1 underflow to exactly 0.0 (0.51100 does), while the sum of logs stays ordinary: −2.58 here, and e−2.58 = 0.076
    • the base model doesn't know an answer is expected after a bare question; after "A:" it has seen answers follow, so the first token became ␣New at 0.797. The chat template, which it was never trained on, made things worse: "ratulations" at 0.127

    Missed it? Reopen the loop, "The probability of an answer is the product of its steps" and "A wrapper around the question changes what the model thinks comes next".

  10. which concept?Generating 32 tokens took about 1.4 s with a KV cache and about 5.9 s without. What exactly does the cache store, why does it give exactly the same next token as recomputing, and how big is it per token for this model in bf16 (16-bit numbers)? How much for a 4,096-token conversation?
    Check
    • it stores every earlier token's keys and values at every layer, so each new step only runs the newest token through the 24 layers
    • the causal mask means a token's K and V depend only on itself and earlier tokens, so text appended later never changes them: stored equals recomputed
    • 24 layers × 2 key/value heads × 64 numbers × 2 (K and V) × 2 bytes = 12,288 B = 12 KB per token; 4,096 tokens → 48 MB, per conversation
    • measured: 38 ms per cached step vs 200 ms for uncached step 32, and the gap widens with length because uncached step n re-reads all n tokens (the timings were rough, other jobs were running)

    Missed it? Reopen the loop, "The loop is what makes generation slow, and the KV cache is what keeps it bearable".
