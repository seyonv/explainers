Attention can't tell first from last

Position encodings: sinusoids, learned positions, rotary (RoPE), ALiBi · Attention Is All You Need, Vaswani et al., 2017 · RoFormer, Su et al., 2021 · Train Short, Test Long, Press, Smith & Lewis, 2022

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Attention mixes tokens with a weighted sum, and a sum ignores order. Without something that marks position, a transformer can't tell "the capital of India" from "India of the capital".

Where it fits: Stop 4 on the route. The embedding card turned each token into a vector that says what it is; this card adds where it is, just before the vectors enter the attention layers.

Attention lets each token look at the others and mix in what it finds. The catch is that the mix is a weighted sum, and a sum doesn't care what order its terms came in. On its own, attention would treat "What is the capital of India?" and "India of capital the is What?" as the same bag of words. This card shows that on the real model, then walks through the four ways people have put order back in.

Try this first

Guess first: take the 7 token vectors of our question and feed them to Qwen's first attention layer in a shuffled order: India of capital the is What ?. Turn off everything that marks position. How much does the output for India change?

Show the answer

Not at all. Every token gets exactly the output it got before, only in a different slot. The largest difference across all 7 × 896 output numbers was 0.0000014, which is float rounding.

Three words first

Attention weight

The share of one token's attention that goes to another. A token's weights over all the tokens it can see add up to 1. Its output is the weighted sum of what those tokens offer.

Shuffle in, shuffle out

Reorder the inputs and the outputs come out reordered the same way, each one unchanged. The jargon is permutation equivariance. It means order carries no information.

Position encoding

Anything that tells the model where a token sits: a vector added to its input, a turn applied to its query and key, or a penalty on its attention scores.

Shuffle the words, and attention without positions doesn't notice

Without positions the weight is 0.1268 both times, and every output is unchanged. With RoPE the weight moves from 0.1386 to 0.1824, and all 7 outputs change. Measured on Qwen2.5-0.5B: run it.

Why: every score compares two contents, and nothing else

Here is what one attention head does for India. It turns India's vector into a query (what it is looking for), and every token's vector into a key (what it offers to be found by) and a value (what it hands over). The score for India → capital is the dot product of India's query with capital's key. A softmax turns the scores into weights that add up to 1, and the output is the weighted sum of the values.

Look at what goes into that score: two vectors, made from two tokens' contents. Nothing in it says "slot 3" or "slot 5". Move capital to slot 2 and its key is the same vector, so the score, the weight and India's output are the same. Order only enters if something writes it into the vectors or the scores.

The lab checks this the honest way. It rebuilds layer 0's attention by hand from Qwen's own weights (the normalisation, the query, key and value matrices with their biases, and 14 query heads sharing 2 key/value heads). It matches the real layer to within 0.00000003. Then it shuffles the input under four settings:

Why the demo turns the causal mask off

Qwen is a decoder: a causal mask stops each token from looking at tokens after it. That mask leaks order by itself. In the original order What sees only itself; shuffled to slot 5, it sees six tokens. So with the mask on, 6 of 7 outputs change even with no position encoding at all. Only ? stays the same, because it is last in both orders and sees all 7 either way.

That is why the first row turns the mask off: to test attention itself. The mask tells a token which tokens came before it, but not in what order or how far back. The last row is the real model, where both are on.

Four ways to put order back

← scroll the sketch →

The first two add a position vector to each token's input, once, before the first layer. The last two act inside every attention layer, on the queries and keys or on the scores. The sinusoid values (sin 5, cos 5) and the ALiBi slope are illustrative; the formulas are from the papers.

Sinusoids (2017). Each position gets a fixed vector of sine and cosine waves at different speeds, with wavelengths running from 2π up to 10,000 × 2π positions, added to the token's embedding (§3.5). The authors also tried a learned table and got nearly the same translation score (25.7 vs 25.8 BLEU, Table 3 row E). They kept the waves because they "may" let the model handle longer inputs than it trained on.

Learned positions (GPT-2, and GPT-3, which uses GPT-2's architecture with 2,048 positions). A trained table with one row per slot, added to the input. The token and position embeddings card builds one. There is simply no row for slot 2,048.

RoPE (2021; Qwen2.5, Llama 3.1). Turn each query and key by an angle set by its position, so the score depends only on the gap between the two tokens. Next section.

ALiBi (2022). Add nothing to the vectors. Subtract m × distance from each attention score, with a fixed slope m per head: 1/2, 1/4, …, 1/256 for 8 heads (§3). For ? looking back over our 7 tokens, a head with slope 1/2 subtracts 3, 2.5, 2, 1.5, 1, 0.5, 0; a head with slope 1/256 subtracts at most 0.023. Steep heads look nearby, gentle heads look far.

The headline ALiBi result: a 1.3-billion-parameter model trained on 1,024-token inputs matched the perplexity of a sinusoid model trained on 2,048, while training 11% faster with 11% less memory (abstract, §4.2). Perplexity measures how surprised the model is by the next word; lower is better.

RoPE: turn the query and key, and only the gap is left

← scroll the sketch →

Each dial is the same pair of numbers from India's query (blue) and capital's key (violet, dashed), measured in layer 0. The query is turned by 24.16° per position it sits at, the key by 24.16° per position it sits at. At slots 5 and 3 or slots 105 and 103, the two arrows end up 32.8° apart and the score is 1.598 both times. One slot closer, the gap is 8.7° and the score changes to 1.879.

A dot product only cares about the two lengths and the angle between the arrows. Turn the query by m × θ and the key by n × θ, and the angle between them shifts by (m − n) × θ. Where the pair sits drops out; how far apart they are stays. That is exactly what the RoFormer paper asked for: a score that depends on the two contents and on m − n only (its Eq. 11), solved by a rotation (Eq. 12–16). "capital of India" gets the same score pattern at the start of a document or 10,000 tokens in.

One pair is not enough, because the angle wraps around: a pair turning 24.16° per slot is back almost where it started after 15 slots (362°), so on its own it can't tell a gap of 0 from a gap of 15. So each head's 64 numbers are split into 32 pairs, each turning at its own speed, from fast to very slow. In Qwen the speed of pair i is 1,000,000−2i/64 radians per position, where 1,000,000 is the RoPE base (rope_theta in its config; the paper used 10,000). The fastest pair makes a full turn every 6.3 positions; the slowest every 4,080,185. Together they work like the hands of a clock.

Two details matter. RoPE turns the queries and keys but never the values, so what a token hands over carries no position stamp. And it adds no parameters. The modern block card puts RoPE next to the other three upgrades in Llama 3.1, including its base of 500,000.

Principle

Attention compares contents; order has to be written into what it compares

A weighted sum can't see order, so every transformer writes position into something the sum depends on: the input vectors (sinusoids, learned tables), the queries and keys (RoPE) or the scores (ALiBi). The closer to the score you write it, the more naturally it comes out as "how far apart" instead of "which slot".

Buys you: a way to judge any new position scheme: ask where it writes position and whether the score sees absolute slots or gaps.

Vaswani et al. 2017 §3.5 · Su et al. 2021 §3.1–3.2 · Press et al. 2022 §2.2, §3

The failure you will hit: the model falls apart past its training length

Failure · position encoding
Symptom
A model trained on 1,024-token inputs does fine on 1,000 tokens and gets steadily worse on 1,500.
Cause
It never saw those positions. A learned table has no row for them; sinusoids have values, but the model never learned to read them. The ALiBi paper measured sinusoid models degrading once inputs ran about 50 tokens past their training length.
Isolate
Score long documents and plot the loss by position. A jump right after the training length points at positions, not content.
Fix
Choose a relative scheme (RoPE, ALiBi) before training, or train on longer inputs. Stretching RoPE for longer context after training is its own topic, not covered here.

A model only knows the positions it practised on.

What this card doesn't settle

  1. Which models use which. Qwen2.5 (measured here) and Llama 3.1 use RoPE; GPT-2 and GPT-3 used learned tables. This card doesn't name ALiBi models beyond the paper's own, because none of its sources do.
  2. Long context. Tricks that stretch RoPE's slow pairs to reach longer inputs (RoPE scaling) exist. They are out of scope here.
  3. "Extrapolates" means perplexity. ALiBi's long-input wins are measured as next-word surprise. In its own Appendix B, when every prediction gets a full window of context, ALiBi's perplexity stays flat on longer inputs. The authors conclude the gains came from fewer tokens starting a chunk with little context, not from using text further back.
  4. The mask leaks order. As the table shows, a decoder's causal mask alone changes outputs when you shuffle. Position encodings are still needed to tell how far back a token is.

Check yourself

1. With no position encoding and no mask, why does shuffling the input only shuffle the output?

Show the answer

Each score is a dot product of one token's query and another's key, which depend only on the tokens' contents. The softmax and the weighted sum then treat the tokens as a set. Move a token and its query, key and value move with it, so its output moves too, unchanged. Measured: largest difference 0.0000014.

2. A query sits at slot 105, a key at slot 103. Why is their RoPE score the same as at slots 5 and 3?

Show the answer

RoPE turns the query by 105 × θ and the key by 103 × θ. The dot product depends on the angle between them, which changes by (105 − 103) × θ, the same as (5 − 3) × θ. Measured: 1.598 both times.

3. You need a model that reads inputs twice as long as anything it trained on. Which of the four would you pick, and which would you rule out?

Show the answer

ALiBi had the strongest evidence in its paper (best around 2× the trained length). Rule out a learned table: it has no rows for the new positions. Sinusoids degraded within about 50 tokens. RoPE did better than sinusoids but less well than ALiBi in that test; stretching RoPE (scaling) is the usual workaround, beyond this card.

Understand and remember

  • A score is query · key, built from two tokens' contents only → softmax and the weighted sum treat the tokens as a set → shuffling the input just shuffles the output (measured: largest change 0.0000014)
  • RoPE turns the query by m × θ and the key by n × θ → a dot product depends only on lengths and the angle between → the angle shifts by (m − n) × θ → the score depends on the gap, not the slot (1.598 at slots 5/3 and at 105/103)
  • A causal mask lets each token see only earlier tokens → shuffling changes what each token can see → outputs change even without positions (6 of 7 in our test) → but the mask gives a set of earlier tokens, not their order or distance
  • A learned table has one row per trained slot → no row past the training length → no extrapolation; ALiBi subtracts m × distance from scores → the same rule works at any length → best at about 2× the trained length in its paper
  • detail that matters RoPE turns queries and keys but never values → what a token hands over carries no position stamp → position only shapes who attends to whom
  • detail that matters Each pair turns at its own speed (Qwen: from one turn per 6.3 positions to one per 4,080,185) → fast pairs resolve nearby gaps, slow pairs tell far gaps apart → together they avoid the wrap-around of a single angle

Reference only

  • Qwen2.5-0.5B: RoPE base 1,000,000 (rope_theta), head size 64 → 32 pairs; Qwen pairs dimension i with i + 32
  • Sinusoids: PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 100002i/d), PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(…); wavelengths 2π to 10,000 × 2π (Vaswani §3.5)
  • ALiBi slopes for 8 heads: 1/2, 1/4, …, 1/256; for n heads a geometric sequence starting at 2−8/n (Press §3)
  • Learned vs sinusoidal in 2017: 25.7 vs 25.8 BLEU (Table 3 row E)
  • ALiBi headline: 1.3B model trained on 1,024 tokens matches a sinusoid model trained on 2,048, 11% faster and 11% less memory
