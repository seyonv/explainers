Most people start with Attention Is All You Need, and its Q, K and V float with nothing attached: the paper assumes you already know what a token is, where its vector comes from and what the model outputs. So this card follows one question, What is the capital of India?, through all ten stops of a real model, and writes down the shape of the data at each one. Every number on it was measured.

Try this first

Guess first: to choose the first word of its answer, how many numbers does the model hand to the last step? 1, 7 (one per token of the question), 896, or 151,936?

Show the answer

151,936: one score for every entry in the model's vocabulary, computed from the last position only. The decoder then picks one of them. Here it picked ␣The, which got just 0.277 of the probability.

Three words first

Token

A chunk of text, often a word or part of one, that the model treats as one unit. Each token has an ID: an integer from 0 to about 151,000.

Block

The repeating unit of the model: an attention step, where tokens read each other, then a feed-forward step, where each token is processed alone. This model stacks 24 of them.

Logits

The model's raw output: one score per vocabulary entry. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and a decoder picks one token.

Every stop has a shape you can check

The shape of the data tells you what each stop does. In one breath: text is cut into tokens, each token becomes an integer and then a vector, a stack of identical blocks mixes the vectors, the last vector becomes one score per vocabulary entry, and the decoder picks one token.

Stops 4–6 are the only part Attention Is All You Need is about. The stops before and after are what give its Q, K and V something to mean. The embedding table (136,134,656 weights) is 27.6% of the model's 494,032,768, and the feed-forward weights are 63.5%.

At stop 9, the decoder decides what kind of answer you get

Failure · decoding
Symptom
You sample from the model and it answers with another question: Which is the capital of Kenya?…, or The capital of India is not Delhi but…
Cause
Pure sampling (T = 1, no cut) draws from the whole list. Each tail token is unlikely, but together the tail holds about 0.09 on this step, so over a dozen steps sampling lands in it often. A base model also has no reason to answer rather than continue.
Isolate
Print the top few tokens and their probabilities at each step. Seeds 0 to 3 gave four different off-route answers here.
Fix
Greedy or a low temperature for facts; top-p to cut the tail when you want variety; a chat model and its template when you want an answer.

The model gives a distribution. The decoder decides what you read.

Follow one input before you read the famous paper

Start with Attention Is All You Need

It assumes tokens, embeddings, softmax and decoding, and it describes a 2017 translation model with an encoder and a decoder. Q, K and V have nothing to attach to yet.

Reading trending papers first is worse: FlashAttention and GQA speed up a stop you can't place yet.

Follow one question through

Every stop has an input and an output you can check: 7 tokens, 7 × 896, 151,936 scores, one token.

Then each paper answers a question you already have, like "where does the order go back in?"

Principle

To understand a model, follow one input and write down its shape at every stop

Shapes are what make the boxes checkable. If you know 7 × 896 goes into a block and 7 × 896 comes out, you can predict what changes when the input gets longer (the 7) and what doesn't (the 896, the weights, the 151,936).

Buys you: a place to hang every later paper, and a way to check your own code.

The reading list, in the order the question meets it

  1. Tokens: Sennrich et al., BPE for rare words · SentencePiece (the hub's SentencePiece card)
  2. Embeddings: word2vec · Distributed representations of words and phrases
  3. Positions: Attention Is All You Need, positional encoding section (sinusoids) · RoFormer (RoPE), which Qwen uses · ALiBi, a bias on attention scores instead of a rotation
  4. The transformer: Attention Is All You Need · The Illustrated Transformer
  5. Attention, made cheap: MQA · GQA, which Qwen uses · FlashAttention · FlashAttention-2 (the hub's GQA and FlashAttention cards)
  6. Feed-forward → experts: Switch Transformers: dense → mixture of experts → sparse compute
  7. Logits and the loop: GPT-3
  8. Decoding: The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration, where top-p comes from

Where the route picture isn't the whole truth

  1. The blocks repeat. Stops 4–6 aren't stages the data passes once. They form one block that runs 24 times, and a residual connection adds each block's output back onto its input.
  2. Q, K and V are three learned projections of the same vector. "What I'm looking for, what I contain, what I give" is a mnemonic, not what the weights do.
  3. Attention isn't always interest. In our run, layers 6, 12 and 18 each sent 35–63% of the last token's attention to the very first token, What. That's an "attention sink", a learned parking spot, not interest in the word.
  4. A base model isn't a chat model. This one only continues text, which is why its first answer token got just 0.277. A chat model's template would make that first token near-certain. The route is identical; only the weights differ.

The order is also wrong for some goals. If you write GPU kernels, start at FlashAttention; if you serve models, the inference path starts from latency and works inward.

Check yourself

1. The embedding table is used twice in this model. Where is the second time, and what does it produce?

Show the answer

At stop 7. The last position's 896-number vector is multiplied by the same 151,936 × 896 table (tied weights), giving one score per vocabulary entry: the 151,936 logits.

2. Make the question twice as long, 14 tokens. Which of these change: the shape between blocks, the parameter count, the number of logits that pick the next token?

Show the answer

Only the shape: 14 × 896. The weights don't depend on input length, so the model stays at 494,032,768 parameters, and only the last row becomes the 151,936 logits that pick the next token.

3. Why is ␣Delhi at 0.164 on one step and 0.994 on the next?

Show the answer

On the first step it competes with ␣New as a way to name the city. Once ␣New is chosen and appended, the input is …is New, and almost the only sensible continuation is ␣Delhi. Each step is a fresh distribution over the whole input so far.

1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • Text is cut by learned merges (BPE) → each piece is an integer ID → the ID selects one row of the embedding table → the model only ever works with those vectors, never with letters, which is why spelling and digits behave oddly
  • Attention mixes tokens but ignores order → order has to be put back (Qwen rotates Q and K with RoPE in every layer) → without it, India of capital would look the same as capital of India
  • One block = attention (tokens read earlier tokens) + feed-forward (each token alone), each added back through a residual → the block repeats 24 times with the shape fixed at tokens × 896 → only the last position's vector becomes the 151,936 logits
  • Logits → softmax → a decoder picks one token → it is appended and the model runs again → the answer's probability is the product of each step's choice (≈ 0.11 here) → the decoder, not the model, decides whether you get the safe answer or a strange one
  • detail that matters The embedding table is also the output layer (tied weights) → it holds 136M of the 494M parameters (27.6%) → in a small model the vocabulary is a big share of the size
  • detail that matters Pure sampling keeps the whole tail → about 0.09 of the probability sat below the top-p 0.9 cut on one step → over a dozen steps sampling lands in the tail often, which is why the four sampled answers wandered off

Reference only

  • Qwen2.5-0.5B: 24 layers, hidden 896, 14 query heads, 2 KV heads, head dim 64, FFN 4,864 (SwiGLU), vocab 151,936 rows (151,665 real tokens)
  • our question's IDs: [3838, 374, 279, 6722, 315, 6747, 30]
  • the paper per stop: BPE (Sennrich 2015), SentencePiece, word2vec, RoFormer, ALiBi, Attention Is All You Need, MQA, GQA, FlashAttention 1–2, Switch Transformers, GPT-3, Holtzman et al. 2019
  • Hugging Face generate flags: do_sample, temperature, top_k, top_p, num_beams

vs Nucleus sampling (top-p): That card cuts a code-token distribution at p = 0.95 and keeps 5 tokens. Here p = 0.9 also keeps 5. What does that say about how top-p sizes its set, and what would top-k 5 do on each?

Check
  • top-p sets the size by cumulative probability, so the count depends on how peaked the distribution is
  • here two tokens hold 0.862 and three small ones close the gap to 0.9
  • top-k 5 keeps 5 in both cases whatever the shape, including tokens the model barely believes in

vs Token and position embeddings: nanoGPT adds a learned position vector to each token vector. Qwen has no position table. What does each one do, and what does RoPE gain?

Check
  • nanoGPT: wte[id] + wpe[pos], added once at the input, limited to the trained length
  • Qwen: rotates Q and K in every attention layer by an angle set by the position
  • after the rotation the dot product depends on the distance between tokens, which carries over to longer inputs better

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Imagine the same model with a 32,000-entry vocabulary instead of 151,936, everything else unchanged. Which stops change shape, and what happens to the embedding table's share of the parameters? Commit, then reveal.

Reveal

Stop 3's table becomes 32,000 × 896 = 28,672,000 weights, and stop 7 produces 32,000 logits instead of 151,936. The model shrinks to 386,570,112 parameters, and the table's share falls from 27.6% to about 7.4%. The blocks don't change at all. The catch: with fewer merges, the same text usually splits into more tokens, so every later stop runs on a longer input.
