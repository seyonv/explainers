Most of the model is the feed-forward part

Feed-forward network (FFN), MLP, SwiGLU, mixture of experts (MoE) · Switch Transformers, Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, Google, 2021

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: If you think of a transformer as "attention", you'll misjudge where its weights live and where its time goes. Every estimate of memory, speed or cost, and every argument for mixture of experts, starts from the fact that the feed-forward parts are most of the model.

Where it fits: Stop 6 of the route. The tokens are now vectors (stop 3) that know their order (stop 4) and have read each other through attention (stop 5). This card covers the other half of every block, then how the biggest models split that half into experts.

Attention gets the headlines, but it's the smaller half of every block. In Qwen2.5-0.5B, the feed-forward networks hold 63.5% of the weights and do 63.5% of the arithmetic for each token. That one fact explains why the biggest models split the FFN into many "experts" and use only one at a time.

Try this first

Guess first: Qwen2.5-0.5B has 494 million weights. Every one of its 24 blocks has an attention part and a feed-forward part. Roughly what share of all the weights sits in attention, and what share in the feed-forward parts?

Show the answer

Attention: 8.9%. Feed-forward: 63.5%, about seven times as much. Most of the rest (27.6%) is the embedding table from stop 3.

Three words first

Residual stream

Each token's running vector of 896 numbers. Every part of every block reads it and adds its result back in, so nothing is overwritten.

Feed-forward network (FFN)

Three matrix multiplies applied to one token's vector at a time. It widens the vector, bends it with a non-linear function, then shrinks it back.

Expert and router

An expert is one copy of the FFN among several. The router is a tiny layer that picks which expert each token goes to.

Attention mixes the tokens; the FFN works on each token alone

← scroll the sketch →

Our 7 tokens each carry a vector down their own lane. In the attention half, tokens read from earlier tokens: that's the only place information crosses between lanes. In the FFN half, each lane goes through the same function with the same weights, and no lane can see another. Both halves add their output to the lane instead of replacing it. Qwen repeats this pair 24 times.

You can check the "each token alone" claim directly. The lab runs layer 23's FFN on ·India's vector by itself, outside the sentence, and compares it with what the FFN produced for ·India inside the full forward pass. The largest difference is 0.000034 on values of about 50: rounding noise. The FFN never looks at the neighbours; whatever it knows about context, attention had to put into the vector first.

Two-thirds of the weights and the arithmetic sit in the FFN

← scroll the sketch →

Measured by counting every weight in the model. To count arithmetic we use FLOPs (floating-point operations, one multiply or one add): a matrix multiply costs 2 FLOPs per weight for each token. Looking up a row of the embedding table costs nothing, but the same table is reused as the output layer that scores all 151,936 tokens, and that costs 272 million FLOPs.

One honest caveat. Comparing each token with every earlier token is cheap for our 7-token question, but its cost grows with the length of the text. At 7,296 tokens of context, the attention scores alone cost as much as the FFN. Long documents shift the balance; short chats don't.

Inside the FFN: widen to 4,864, gate it, shrink back to 896

← scroll the sketch →

Measured on ·India at the last layer (layer 23). Two matrices turn the 896 numbers into two lists of 4,864: a gate and an up list. The gate goes through a smooth on/off function called SiLU and multiplies the up list number by number. Then a third matrix (down) shrinks the result back to 896. This gated design is called SwiGLU.

Two things stand out in the measurements. First, few of the 4,864 hidden units do most of the work for this token: sort them by size and the top 7 carry half of the total activity (measured as the sum of squared values), and the top 50 carry 90%. The middle unit's value is only 0.11 while the biggest is 30.1. Different tokens light up different units.

Second, the FFN nudges the vector rather than rewriting it. At layer 23 the FFN's update has length 64.6, added to a residual vector of length 277.8, so about 0.23×. Across layers 1 to 23 the ratio for ·India stays between 0.21 and 0.52. (Layer 0 is the exception, at 15.4×, because the residual is still the raw embedding row, which is tiny.)

What do the units stand for? A popular intuition, from Geva and colleagues (2021), treats each hidden unit as a key–value memory: its row in the first matrix is a pattern to match, and its column in the down matrix is what gets written when it matches. Treat that as a picture, not a law. When we projected ·India's strongest units straight onto the vocabulary, the words that came out were unreadable.

In code these are gate_proj, up_proj and down_proj, with no biases.

If the FFN is most of the model, keep many and use one at a time

← scroll the sketch →

An illustrative what-if, computed by the lab: Qwen2.5-0.5B is dense. Give every layer 8 copies of its FFN (experts) and a router that sends each token to one of them. The model grows to 2.69 billion weights, but each token still runs exactly one FFN per layer, so the work per token barely moves (494.2 million weights used, the extra 0.2 million being the routers). The memory doesn't stay put: every expert has to be loaded.

That's the idea of the Switch Transformer. Its starting point (§2) is that the number of weights and the compute per token are two separate dials, and that turning up weights alone can help. Earlier mixture-of-experts work argued that each token needed at least 2 experts for the router to learn. Switch sends each token to just one (top-1 routing), scales that expert's output by the router's probability for it, and found that this simpler choice kept quality while cutting routing work (§2.1).

Three pieces make it work in practice (§2.2):

  1. Expert capacity. Each expert accepts at most (tokens in the batch ÷ number of experts) × a capacity factor tokens. A token that arrives at a full expert is dropped: it skips the layer and rides through on the residual stream. In the paper's runs that was typically under 1% of tokens, and Switch did better at low capacity factors of 1.0 to 1.25.
  2. A load-balancing loss. An extra training term that is smallest when tokens spread evenly over the experts. It's weighted at α = 0.01, enough to balance the load without disturbing the main objective.
  3. Experts every other layer in the Base, Large and XXL models (Table 9), not in every block.

The payoff, from the paper. With the same FLOPs per token, Switch-Base with 64 experts reached the dense T5-Base model's quality in one-seventh of the training time (§3.2, Figure 5). It was 2.5× faster than T5-Large, a dense model doing 3.5× more FLOPs per token (§3.3). Switch-Base has 7 billion weights against T5-Base's 0.2 billion at the same compute per sequence (Table 9). The 1.6-trillion-weight Switch-C, with 2,048 experts, reached a fixed quality 4× faster than T5-XXL (Table 9).

The serving side, where a batch of tokens gets split across experts and the memory bill comes due, is covered in Mixture of experts: routers, top-k and imbalance and Mixture of experts (MoE).

Principle

Weights and compute are separate dials

In a dense model every weight is used for every token, so more weights always means more work. The FFN is where the weights are, and since it works on each token alone, each token can be handed to a different FFN. A router then lets you add weights without adding work per token.

Buys you: more knowledge capacity per FLOP, paid for in memory.

Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, §2 and §3

The failure you will hit: one expert gets everything

Failure · routing imbalance
Symptom
Quality stalls, or many tokens are dropped, even though the model has plenty of experts.
Cause
The router learns to favour a few experts. Those fill up past their capacity while the others sit idle, so you pay memory for weights that barely train.
Isolate
Log, per layer, the fraction of tokens each expert receives. Balanced routing puts each near 1 ÷ (number of experts); 8 experts should each see about 12.5%.
Fix
Add the load-balancing loss (Switch used α = 0.01), and give experts a little spare room with a capacity factor slightly above 1.

An unused expert still costs its full share of memory.

When mixture of experts isn't the right tool

  1. Memory. All experts must be loaded even though each token uses one. In our what-if, the weights grow from 0.99 GB to 5.38 GB in bfloat16 (2 bytes per weight). On a phone or a laptop, memory is usually the limit, which is why small and on-device models such as Qwen2.5-0.5B usually stay dense.
  2. Balance. The router has to be trained to share the load, with an extra loss and a capacity setting to tune; dropped tokens are a quiet quality cost.
  3. Serving. A batch of tokens gets scattered across experts, so each expert's matrix multiply sees fewer tokens, and in big deployments experts live on different chips that must exchange tokens.

Switch also shows a way back: distilling a large sparse model into a small dense one kept about 30% of the quality gain while shrinking the model by up to 99% (§1, §4.2).

Check yourself

1. The FFN gets the vector for ·India and nothing else. How can its output still depend on the word "capital" earlier in the question?

Show the answer

Attention runs first in the block and adds information read from earlier tokens (like capital) into ·India's vector. The FFN then transforms that already-mixed vector. Attention moves information between tokens; the FFN works on what has arrived.

2. Why does making 8 experts per layer multiply the weights by about 5.45 rather than 8?

Show the answer

Only the FFN is copied. It's 63.5% of the model, so the total becomes 0.365 + 8 × 0.635 ≈ 5.45 times the original. The embedding table, attention and norms stay single.

3. A Switch layer gets 1,000 tokens, has 8 experts and a capacity factor of 1.0. One expert is chosen by 200 tokens. What happens to them?

Show the answer

Capacity is 1,000 ÷ 8 × 1.0 = 125 tokens. 125 of them are processed; the other 75 are dropped and pass to the next layer unchanged through the residual stream.

Understand and remember

  • Attention is the only step that reads other tokens → the FFN gets one token's vector and applies the same weights at every position → context must already be in the vector when the FFN sees it → running the FFN on ·India alone gives the same output (difference 0.000034)
  • Each FFN is three 896 × 4,864 matrices → 24 of them = 313.8 M of 494.0 M weights (63.5%) → a matrix multiply costs 2 FLOPs per weight per token → the FFN is also 63.5% of the 989 M FLOPs per token at short context
  • Attention scores cost grows with the number of earlier tokens, FFN cost doesn't → at 7 tokens attention is about 9% of the work → at 7,296 tokens of context the scores alone match the FFN
  • The FFN works on tokens independently → each token can go to a different FFN → a router picks one expert (top-1) → total weights grow with the number of experts, but work per token stays one FFN → memory pays for every expert (8 experts: 0.99 → 5.38 GB in bfloat16)
  • detail that matters Each block adds its output to the residual stream → the FFN's update is a nudge (0.21–0.52× the residual for ·India in layers 1–23) → a dropped token in a Switch layer simply skips the FFN and keeps its vector
  • detail that matters Expert capacity = tokens ÷ experts × capacity factor → an unbalanced router overflows popular experts and drops tokens → a load-balancing loss (α = 0.01) and a capacity factor near 1.0–1.25 keep drops low (typically under 1%)
