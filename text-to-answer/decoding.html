Choosing the next token is a choice

Decoding, sampling strategies: greedy, temperature, top-k, top-p (nucleus), beam search · The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration, Holtzman, Buys, Du, Forbes & Choi, ICLR 2020

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The model's last layer gives a score to every one of its 151,936 vocabulary rows, not a word. Something has to turn those scores into one token, and different rules give different answers to the same question, from "New Delhi" to a 46-token loop to "not Delhi".

Where it fits: Stops 8–9 of the route. The earlier stops built the logits for the step after …? The capital of India is; this card is the step between those logits and the token the next card (One token at a time) feeds back in. It builds on softmax from the toolkit cards.

At the end of every step the model hands back one score for each of its 151,936 vocabulary rows. It doesn't hand back a word. Something outside the model has to turn those scores into one token, and that rule (the decoder) changes the answer you get. This card puts the common decoders side by side on one real step of our question.

Try this first

Guess first: after What is the capital of India? The capital of India is, the model gives New a probability of 0.698 and Delhi 0.164. If you draw the next token at random using exactly those probabilities, how often do you get neither of them?

Show the answer

About 1 time in 7: 1 − 0.698 − 0.164 = 0.138. That leftover is spread over words like known, Mumbai and not, and over 151,928 other tokens. Every decoder below is a different answer to "what do we do about that leftover?"

Three words first

Logit

The raw score the model gives each possible next token. Any real number; bigger means "more likely". Here New scores 17.28.

Softmax

Turns the scores into probabilities that add up to 1: raise e to each score, then divide by the total.

Decoder

The rule that picks one token from those probabilities: always the top one, or a random draw from some of them.

One step, many ways to pick

The blue column holds the logits the model produced at this step, measured on Qwen2.5-0.5B. Softmax turns them into the bars. Greedy always takes the top bar. Top-k keeps a fixed number of rows, top-p keeps rows until their probabilities reach p, and pure sampling keeps everything, including the long tail at the bottom.

Softmax by hand, on the top logits

Subtract the biggest logit first (it changes nothing in the result and keeps e to the power from overflowing), then raise e to each:

The last column divides by the sum over all 151,936 rows. If you divide by the sum of just the top 8 instead, New comes out at 0.746, not 0.698, because the other 151,928 tokens together hold 0.065. A gap of 1.45 in logits is a factor of 4.3 in probability (e1.45), which is why the top token dominates.

Temperature reshapes the same scores before the draw

← scroll the sketch →

Dividing every logit by T stretches the gaps (T < 1) or squashes them (T > 1). At T = 0.5 New gets 0.946; at T = 1.5 it drops to 0.289 and the tail outside the top 8 grows to 0.484, nearly half. As T heads to 0 the top token takes everything, so sampling at T → 0 is greedy. Temperature never removes a token; it only reweights them. How to choose T for a task is its own card: Sampling temperature.

Top-k: keep a fixed number

Keep the k highest rows, rescale them to add up to 1, draw. At this step k = 2 keeps 0.861 of the probability, so New becomes 0.810 and Delhi 0.190. k = 1 is greedy.

The paper's complaint (§3.2): one fixed k is too few when many words fit and too many when one word clearly fits.

Top-p: keep a fixed share

Keep the fewest top rows whose probabilities add up to at least p. Here p = 0.8 keeps 2 tokens, 0.9 keeps 5 and 0.95 keeps 12. The list grows when the model is unsure and shrinks when it's sure.

This is the paper's proposal, nucleus sampling (§3.1). In depth: Nucleus sampling.

Greedy can lose the best whole answer; beam search looks further

Greedy decides one token at a time and never goes back. Beam search keeps the B best partial answers at every step (B is the beam width; here 4), extends each by one token, and keeps the 4 best again, ranked by the probability of the whole answer so far. Multiplying many probabilities gives tiny numbers, so it adds their logs instead (log P).

On our question it changes nothing: greedy and 4 beams both give The capital of India is New Delhi. (log P = −2.58). We tried two more short questions with 12 new tokens, and both came out different:

← scroll the sketch →

Greedy took - because it was the single most likely first token (0.141). Later it had to name a planet and Mercury edged out Jupiter, 0.149 to 0.145. Beam search kept The (0.115) alive and found a continuation whose total probability is about 55× higher (e4.00). For What is the capital of Australia? greedy started Australia is a country located in the southern hemisphere… (log P −7.87), while beam answered The capital of Australia is Canberra. (−2.56).

Over long outputs, the safest choice turns into a loop

Holtzman et al. found two opposite failures, which they call degeneration. Maximising probability (greedy, beam) gives text that is bland and gets stuck repeating itself. Pure sampling gives text that wanders off, because tens of thousands of unlikely tokens add up to a real chance of picking one, which they call the "unreliable tail" (§1). Our 0.5B model shows both on our question:

Greedy, 400 tokens

The model normally stops after New Delhi., so we blocked its end-of-text token. The first 60 tokens read fine: "It is the seat of the Government of India… on the banks of the River Yamuna…"

From token 225 on, the rest is one 46-token block over and over: "The city is a place that is a true reflection of India's rich history, culture, and heritage. The city is a place that is always changing and evolving…"

The paper measured this at scale: GPT-2 Large (762M parameters), 5,000 continuations of up to 200 tokens each (§4.1). "Repetition" means a phrase repeating at least three times at the end (Figure 9).

Top-p 0.95 lands closest to human on both, and it also scored best in their human evaluation (HUSE 0.97 vs 0.67 for pure sampling). People don't write the most probable next word, so a decoder that always does doesn't sound like people (§4.3).

Pick the decoder for the job

jobdecoderwhy
Short factual answergreedy, or a low temperatureOne right answer; variety only adds ways to be wrong. Our question: greedy and beam both said New Delhi, two of four top-p samples did.

Translation, summarybeam searchThe output is pinned down by the input, so repetition matters less (§2.2), and finding the most probable whole answer pays off.
Brainstorming, storiestop-p around 0.9–0.95, temperature near 1Open-ended: you want variety without the unreliable tail. Top-p 0.95 was closest to human text in Table 1.
Evaluation, pass@ksampling at a higher temperaturepass@k counts a problem solved if any of k tries passes, so variety helps: Codex's best T rose to 0.8 for pass@100 (§3.3).
Principle

The model gives a distribution; the decoder decides how much of it to trust

Greedy and beam trust only the top and are reliable on short answers but loop on long ones. Pure sampling trusts everything, including a tail the model is bad at. Temperature, top-k and top-p sit in between: reshape or cut, then draw.

Buys you: one model, tuned per job without retraining.

Holtzman et al. 2020, §3 and §7

The failure you will hit: the answer goes round in circles

Failure · decoding
Symptom
Long output that repeats a sentence or paragraph, like our greedy run from token 225 on.
Cause
Greedy or beam (or a very low temperature, or a tiny k or p) always takes the most probable continuation, and the paper found that a phrase gets more probable each time it repeats (Figure 4). The loop feeds itself.
Isolate
Rerun with the same input and top-p 0.9–0.95 at T near 1. If the loop goes away, it was the decoder, not the model or the input.
Fix
Sample with top-p for long open-ended text; keep greedy for short answers that end on their own.

Most likely at every step is not the same as good over 400 steps.

When it isn't the right tool

  1. You may not get to choose. Chat APIs usually expose temperature and top-p (temperature, top_p), but not always beam search.
  2. Greedy isn't perfectly repeatable at scale. On GPU servers, batching your request with others and the order of floating-point additions can nudge the logits, so two close tokens can swap. Treat "temperature 0" as nearly, not exactly, deterministic.
  3. Our numbers are one small base model. Qwen2.5-0.5B without chat tuning, one question, a handful of seeds. The paper's numbers are GPT-2 Large on open-ended web text. Bigger, chat-tuned models are much more peaked on factual questions, so the decoders disagree less there.
  4. Beam search isn't a cure. It found better answers in two of our three questions, but the paper shows it still repeats on long text (Table 1) and, with wide beams, GPT-2 tends to stop right away (Figure 1).

Check yourself

1. Why does temperature 0.01 behave like greedy, while temperature 1.5 lets the tail in?

Show the answer

Dividing by a tiny T multiplies the gaps between logits, so the top token's share goes to 1 (New: 1.000). Dividing by 1.5 shrinks the gaps, so the 151,928 low tokens together rise from 0.065 to 0.484 of the probability.

2. Greedy answered "Mercury" for the largest planet; beam answered "Jupiter". What did beam do that greedy couldn't?

Show the answer

It kept the second-best first token (The, 0.115) alive instead of committing to - (0.141), and ranked whole answers by summed log-probability: −6.14 beat −10.14.

3. You need 100 different candidate programs to run against tests. Greedy or sampling, and why?

Show the answer

Sampling at a higher temperature. Greedy returns the same program 100 times. pass@100 rewards any one success, so variety is the point (Codex's best T for pass@100 was 0.8).

Understand and remember

  • softmax raises e to each logit and divides by the total → a logit gap of 1.45 becomes a 4.3× probability ratio → the top token dominates (New 0.698) but 151,928 tail tokens still hold 0.065 together
  • temperature divides logits by T before softmax → T < 1 widens gaps (T 0.5: New 0.946) and T > 1 narrows them (T 1.5: tail 0.484) → T → 0 is greedy; temperature reshapes but never cuts
  • greedy commits to the single best token each step → one early pick can lock in a worse whole answer (- 0.141 → Mercury, log P −10.14) → beam search keeps 4 partial answers and ranks by summed log P (Jupiter, −6.14)
  • always taking the most probable continuation → a repeated phrase becomes more probable each time → loops (our greedy run repeats a 46-token block from token 225; paper: greedy 73.66% repetition) → while sampling everything picks tail tokens and wanders → top-p cuts the tail and keeps variety (0.36%, closest to human)
  • detail that matters top-p keeps the fewest top tokens reaching p, so the kept list follows the model's confidence (here 2, 5 or 12 tokens at p 0.8, 0.9, 0.95) → unlike top-k, it doesn't let junk in when the model is sure or starve it when the model is unsure

Reference only

  • subtract the largest logit before exponentiating (same result, no overflow)
  • API parameter names temperature, top_p, top_k; in Hugging Face generate, num_beams and do_sample
  • Holtzman Table 1 perplexities: human 12.38, greedy 1.50, beam 16 1.48, pure sampling 22.73, top-p 0.95 13.13; HUSE top-p 0.97 vs pure 0.67
  • Codex best temperatures: 0.2 for pass@1, 0.8 for pass@100 (679M model)
