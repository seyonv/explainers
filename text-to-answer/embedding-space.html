Similar words end up close together

Token embeddings · the embedding table · word vectors · word2vec (skip-gram, CBOW) · Efficient Estimation of Word Representations in Vector Space, Mikolov, Chen, Corrado & Dean, Google, 2013

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: After tokenizing, the model has only integers, and integers carry no meaning: ID 6747 (India) sits between Category and _event. Without some way to say "India is like China", the model would have to learn everything about every token separately.

Where it fits: Stop 3 on the route. The tokenizer (stop 2) turned the question into seven IDs; this stop turns each ID into a vector of 896 numbers, which is what the blocks actually work on. It builds on the idea that an embedding lookup is just indexing.

After tokenizing, our question is seven integers, and ␣India is 6747. An integer can't tell the model that India is like China and unlike banana. So the model swaps each ID for one row of a learned table: a list of 896 numbers, placed during training so that tokens used in similar contexts get similar rows.

Try this first

Guess first: in Qwen2.5-0.5B's own table, which row is closest to the row for ␣India? ␣China, ␣Delhi, India (no leading space), or ␣Pakistan?

Show the answer

India without the space, at a cosine of 0.810. The closest rows are other spellings of the same word: ␣india 0.711, the Chinese word for India 0.670, ␣Indian 0.633. China (0.449) and Pakistan (0.443) come next, and Delhi (0.409) after them.

Three words first

Token ID

The integer the tokenizer gives each piece of text. In this model it runs from 0 to 151,935. It's a label, like a row number in a spreadsheet.

Embedding table

A grid of learned numbers with one row per token ID. Here it's 151,936 rows of 896 numbers each. A token's row is called its embedding, or its vector.

Cosine similarity

How closely two vectors point the same way: 1 means the same direction, 0 means unrelated. It ignores length and compares direction only.

An ID has no meaningful distance; a vector does

IDs are just labels

ID 6746 is Category, 6747 is ␣India, and 6748 is _event. Being next to each other as numbers means nothing.

If the model did arithmetic on raw IDs, it would treat India as halfway between those two, which is nonsense.

Rows can carry similarity

Each row has 896 numbers that training is free to adjust. Rows that should behave alike can end up pointing the same way.

Cosine similarity measures that: India·China 0.449, but India·banana 0.013.

The lookup is a matrix multiply in disguise. Write the ID as a one-hot vector: 151,936 zeros with a single 1 at position 6747. Multiply it by the table, and every row is multiplied by zero except row 6747. The lab checks it: one_hot @ table equals table[6747] exactly. Indexing gives the same answer without doing 136 million multiplications for nothing.

The table is big: 151,936 × 896 = 136,134,656 weights, or 27.6% of this model. Qwen2.5-0.5B also reuses the same table at the very end to score every possible next token (tied input and output embeddings).

word2vec: predict the neighbours, and similar words get similar vectors

In 2013, Mikolov and colleagues at Google trained word vectors with a deliberately tiny model, so it could read billions of words. The paper's two set-ups:

CBOW (continuous bag of words)

Average the vectors of the surrounding words and predict the middle one. The best set-up used 4 words before and 4 after (§3.1).

Skip-gram

The reverse: from one word, predict the words around it, up to 10 positions away in the experiments, with distant words sampled less (§3.2).

Why that makes similar words close. India and China both appear before "'s economy", after "trade with", and so on. To predict the same neighbours, the model has to give them vectors that produce the same predictions, so their vectors end up alike. The follow-up paper puts it this way: a word's vector can be seen as representing the distribution of the contexts it appears in (1310.4546, §5). Nobody tells the model that India is a country. Sharing contexts is enough.

The famous test is arithmetic. vector("King") − vector("Man") + vector("Woman") lands closest to Queen (1301.3781, §1.1, citing earlier work by Mikolov, Yih and Zweig). To score this, the paper built 8,869 semantic and 10,675 syntactic analogy questions. Only the exact word counts as correct, and the three input words are skipped in the search (§4, §4.1).

Semantic questions are about meaning (Athens : Greece :: Oslo : Norway); syntactic ones are about grammar (great : greater :: tough : tougher). Even the best model gets about a third of them wrong under exact matching.

Qwen's table wasn't trained with word2vec. It was trained along with the rest of the model to predict the next token. The pressure is similar, though: tokens that lead to the same predictions are better off with similar rows.

A tokenizer gives one word several IDs: with and without a leading space, in different capitals, plural, and in other languages. The table puts all of these next to each other first. Delhi has fewer spelling variants, so its list goes straight to other Indian cities.

king − man + woman lands on queen, but only just

The analogy works in Qwen's table, by word2vec's own rule. The nearest row to the new vector is ␣king itself (0.690). Once the three inputs are skipped, ␣queen is first at 0.652. But queen was already close to king alone (0.635, neighbour #5), so the arithmetic moved it only a little. The same test on our running example, Paris − France + India, puts ␣Delhi 5th after skipping the inputs, behind India, ␣Indian and two other India spellings. Berlin − Germany + France gives ␣Paris first, and bigger − big + small gives ␣smaller first.

Principle

Meaning comes from usage: words that appear in similar contexts get similar vectors

Neither word2vec nor Qwen is told what any word means. Each is trained to predict text, and the cheapest way to do that is to give interchangeable tokens rows that point the same way. Similarity, groups and some analogies come out as by-products.

Buys you: a model that can reuse what it learned about China when it sees India, because their rows already start close.

Mikolov et al. 2013 (1301.3781 §3–4; 1310.4546 §5), measured on Qwen2.5-0.5B

The failure you will hit: one row for every meaning of a word

Failure · context-free rows
Symptom
You average raw input rows to compare sentences. "He sat on the river bank" and "She deposited cash at the bank" look more alike than they should, because the word bank adds the exact same ␣bank row to both.
Cause
The table has one row per token, whatever the sentence. ␣bank's neighbours are ␣Bank 0.799, ␣banks 0.783 and the Chinese word for a money bank 0.735. Nothing in the row says "river".
Isolate
Compare ␣bank's vector across sentences at each depth. Before any block, it's identical (1.000). After 6 of the 24 blocks, river bank · river bank is 0.849, while river bank · money bank drops to 0.494.
Fix
Use the vectors after the blocks, where attention has mixed in the neighbouring words, or a model trained to embed whole sentences.

The table gives a token its starting point; the blocks give it its meaning in this sentence.

What the input table can't tell you

  1. It has no context. ␣capital is closer to the Chinese word for capital as money (0.663) than to the one for capital city (0.316). In our question, the blocks have to sort out which sense is meant.
  2. Only single tokens have rows. ␣New␣Delhi is two tokens, ␣New and ␣Delhi, and ␣Thiruvananthapuram is seven pieces. No row holds "New Delhi". word2vec's test set left out multi-word names such as New York for the same reason (1301.3781 §4.1).
  3. Analogies are a weak test. In Qwen's table, queen wins mainly because it was already close to king, and Delhi comes 5th. Exact-match scoring hides how often the right answer is merely near the top.
  4. The 2-D picture loses most of the spread. Two PCA directions keep 32.8% of it for these 18 rows. Two points that look close on the sketch can be further apart in the full 896 numbers.

For meaning in context, look at the hidden states after the blocks, not at the table. The next stops on the route show how the blocks add position and context.

Check yourself

1. Why does the model look up a row instead of feeding the ID 6747 in as a number?

Show the answer

IDs are arbitrary labels: 6746 is Category and 6748 is _event, so numeric closeness means nothing. A row of 896 learned numbers can be adjusted in training so that tokens that behave alike point the same way, which cosine similarity can measure.

2. Skip-gram never sees a definition of "country". How do India and China still end up with similar vectors?

Show the answer

Each vector is trained to predict the surrounding words. India and China appear in similar contexts, so the vectors that predict those contexts well end up alike. Similar contexts lead to similar vectors.

3. In Qwen's table, king − man + woman gives queen first (with the inputs skipped). Why is that weaker evidence than it sounds?

Show the answer

Queen was already king's 5th nearest row (0.635), and the offset raised it only to 0.652. King itself stays the closest row at 0.690 and only drops out because the rule skips the inputs. Paris − France + India puts Delhi just 5th.

Understand and remember

  • token ID is an arbitrary label → numeric distance between IDs means nothing (6746 Category, 6747 ␣India, 6748 _event) → replace each ID with a learned row of 896 numbers → rows can point the same way, and cosine similarity measures it
  • training predicts surrounding or next tokens → tokens used in similar contexts must produce similar predictions → their rows end up pointing the same way → India·China 0.449 and Delhi·Mumbai 0.483, against a random-pair baseline of 0.153
  • one row per token, whatever the sentence → ␣bank and ␣capital carry every sense at once → identical vectors before any block (1.000) → the blocks separate the senses (river · money bank 0.494 after 6 blocks)
  • the tokenizer gives one word many IDs (space, capitals, plural, other languages) → those rows share contexts most of all → they are each other's nearest neighbours → meaning-level neighbours like China come after them
  • detail that matters one-hot × table equals table[id] exactly → the lookup is a linear layer with a 0/1 input → models implement it as indexing, skipping 136 million wasted multiplications
  • detail that matters Qwen2.5-0.5B ties input and output embeddings → the same 151,936 × 896 table both starts the forward pass and scores every next token at the end → 27.6% of all weights serve both ends

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Bengio 2003: characters as points in space: Bengio's 2003 model learned its embedding table while training a full language model; word2vec threw away the hidden layer to train on billions of words. What did each choice buy, and how does Qwen's table relate to both?

Check
  • Bengio: embeddings learned jointly with a non-linear predictor, but expensive, so less data
  • word2vec: a log-linear model with no hidden layer, cheap enough for billions of words, which gave better analogies
  • Qwen is like Bengio's set-up at huge scale: the table is trained along with the whole model to predict the next token
  • the same pressure applies in all three: shared contexts lead to similar rows

vs Attention can't tell first from last: The embedding row says which token this is. What does it leave out that the next stop has to add, and why can't the table hold it?

Check
  • the row is identical wherever the token appears, so it carries no position
  • attention on its own can't tell first from last
  • position has to be added separately (learned positions, sinusoids, or RoPE rotating Q and K)
  • putting position in the table would need one row per (token, position) pair

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

After 6 blocks, ␣bank in a river sentence and in a money sentence has cosine 0.494. Commit first: after all 24 blocks, will that cosine be lower than 0.494, about the same, or higher?

Reveal

Higher: 0.744 after 24 blocks (12 blocks: 0.623), while the two river-sense banks stay around 0.85. The senses are furthest apart in the middle of the model. One likely reason: the final layers are shaped for predicting the next token, and hidden states there share large common directions, which lifts all cosines. The ordering (same sense more alike than different senses) holds at every depth the lab checks: 6, 12 and 24 blocks.
