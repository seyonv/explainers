Neural networks by hand

Also called: multilayer perceptron (MLP), feed-forward network, dense or fully connected layers. This card works one tiny network from start to finish: one neuron, then a layer, then four stacked layers, then one learning step. Every weight is a small whole number, so you can check each cell with a pen. Inspired by the "AI by Hand" approach (Tom Yeh) and Roan Monteiro's Medium walkthrough of it; the numbers and example here are new.

In one breath

A neural network is only two operations, repeated: multiply and add (a weighted sum), then clip negatives to zero (ReLU). One neuron does it once. A layer does it for many neurons at once, which is a matrix multiply. A deep network passes the result through several layers, and training adjusts the weights until the output is right.

The running example: a tiny network decides whether you stay in or go out tonight. It sees three numbers, x=[ energy, friends going, deadlines ]=[ 2, 1, 3 ]x = [\,\text{energy},\ \text{friends going},\ \text{deadlines}\,] = [\,2,\ 1,\ 3\,]x=[energy, friends going, deadlines]=[2, 1, 3]. The labels are only there to make it concrete. To the arithmetic they're just three signals.

2Why the gate matters

ReLU looks like a trivial step, but without it, depth gives you nothing. Two layers with no gate collapse into a single layer:

W2(W1x+b1)+b2  =  (W2W1)⏟one matrix x+(W2b1+b2)⏟one bias W_2\big(W_1\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1\big) + \mathbf{b}_2 \;=\; \underbrace{(W_2 W_1)}_{\text{one matrix}}\,\mathbf{x} + \underbrace{(W_2\mathbf{b}_1 + \mathbf{b}_2)}_{\text{one bias}} W2​(W1​x+b1​)+b2​=one matrix(W2​W1​)​​x+one bias(W2​b1​+b2​)​​

A product of matrices is just another matrix. So 2 linear layers, or 100, equal a single linear layer, which can only draw straight lines and flat planes.

Putting a bend between the layers breaks the collapse. Each neuron's on/off decision depends on the input, so different inputs effectively see different networks.

3A layer is a matrix

One neuron is one opinion. A layer is a panel of neurons that all look at the same input, each with its own weights. Stack each neuron's weights as a row and the whole panel is one matrix multiply:

Neuron 1 is the one from step 1. Neuron 2 (likes friends, hates deadlines) voted −2 and was silenced. Neuron 3 (mostly energy) fired at 3. The layer has turned three raw signals into three features: new numbers that each answer a learned question about the input.

That's why GPUs, which are built for matrix multiplies, are so good at neural networks. (PyTorch stores examples as rows and writes XW⊤XW^{\top}XW⊤, which is the same arithmetic.)

4Going deep: stack the layers

One nudge took the loss from 4.5 to 0.28. In a deep network, backpropagation applies the chain rule to pass the error signal backwards through every layer, so each of the 32 knobs (or the billions in an LLM) learns its share of the blame. Repeat over millions of examples and that's training.

8Why this can fit anything

Each ReLU neuron adds one bend to a straight line. Shift the bends and add them up with the right weights, and you can build any shape out of straight pieces. Three neurons are enough to make a bump:

f(x)=ReLU⁡(x−1)−2 ReLU⁡(x−2)+ReLU⁡(x−3) f(x) = \ReLU(x-1) - 2\,\ReLU(x-2) + \ReLU(x-3) f(x)=ReLU(x−1)−2ReLU(x−2)+ReLU(x−3)
ReLU⁡(x−c)\ReLU(x - c)ReLU(x−c)
A ramp that starts at x=cx = cx=c. The bias decides where the bend is.
+1, −2, +1+1,\ -2,\ +1+1, −2, +1
Output weights: ramp up at 1, turn down at 2, flatten out at 3. The result is a triangle of height 1 centred at 2 (for example f(1.5)=0.5f(1.5) = 0.5f(1.5)=0.5 and f(2)=1f(2) = 1f(2)=1).

Put many bumps side by side, each scaled to the height of the target curve, and you can approximate any continuous function as closely as you like. That's the universal approximation theorem. It says one wide hidden layer is enough in principle, but it doesn't say how many neurons you need or whether training will find them. In practice, depth wins: layers reuse each other's features, so a deep network gets the same bends with far fewer neurons than a wide, shallow one.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ One linear layer

Wx+bW\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}Wx+b, with or without stacking (step 2). It can only draw straight boundaries, so it can't learn even XOR, "one or the other but not both."

✗ Hand-written rules

"If deadlines > 2, stay in." This breaks on every case you didn't think of, and nobody can write the rules for recognising a face or translating a sentence.

✗ One huge, shallow layer

A universal approximator in theory, but it can need exponentially many neurons, because it can't reuse features: every bump is built from scratch.

✓ Deep network with ReLU

Matrix multiplies (fast on GPUs) plus a bend in every neuron. Layers build features on top of features, and gradient descent finds the weights from examples instead of rules.

Is a neural network always the right tool?

No. On small or tabular datasets (spreadsheets of customers, prices, sensor rows), gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost or LightGBM usually match or beat an MLP with less tuning, and linear or logistic regression stays hard to beat when you need to explain each coefficient. Neural networks earn their keep with lots of data and raw signals like images, audio and text, where nobody can hand-design the features.

Three honest caveats about this card. Real weights are decimals that start random and are learned, not chosen whole numbers. Real networks often use GELU or SiLU instead of ReLU (they're smooth, so neurons don't die), plus normalisation and residual connections to train deep stacks. And "neuron" is a loose metaphor: a weighted sum plus a gate is far simpler than a biological neuron.
