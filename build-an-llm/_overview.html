From reading to building

The course map · Sebastian Raschka, Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch), Manning, 2025, appendix A to appendix E · Andrej Karpathy, Neural Networks: Zero to Hero, courses 2, 3, 8, 9 and 10 · three gap cards measured on Qwen2.5-0.5B

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Reading about LLMs leaves you able to trace a model but not write one, and two good resources pull in different directions: Raschka's book codes the whole pipeline at toy scale, while Zero to Hero goes deep on fewer pieces. Without a route you either switch between codebases or skip the depth that makes the code make sense.

Where it fits: The map for the Build an LLM path. It builds on the LLM basics course (the route a question takes through a trained model) and lays the book's chapters over five days, with the Zero to Hero courses and this course's three cards beside them.

After LLM basics you can trace a question through a trained model, but you haven't written one. This path has you code a GPT yourself in five days, one book chapter at a time, with Zero to Hero for depth where the book moves fast. This page is the map: what you build each day, what to reread before each chapter, and where each depth option plugs in.

Try this first

Guess first: on Day 4 you pretrain your own GPT-2 on a laptop. How much text does the book train it on: one short story, a shelf of novels, or a slice of the web? And what happens to the loss on text it hasn't trained on?

Show the answer

One short story: 5,145 tokens (Raschka §5.2). Training loss keeps falling, but validation loss stops improving after the second epoch and the model starts reciting the story. That's expected, not a bug. It's why §5.5 then loads OpenAI's trained weights into your code, and why Zero to Hero course 10 exists: it pretrains the same model on about 10 billion tokens, on rented GPUs.

The book is the spine, Zero to Hero is the depth

Raschka: the spine

Linear. Each chapter's code builds on the last, so by Day 5 the classifier and the assistant run on the GPT you wrote on Day 3.

Laptop-sized. The model is GPT-2 124M and pretraining uses one short story, so every step runs on a Mac in minutes.

Goes all the way. From tokenizer to an assistant that follows instructions, scored by a judge, with LoRA on top.

Zero to Hero: the depth

Backprop from first principles. Course 2 builds autograd by hand, so loss.backward() stops being magic. The book lets PyTorch do it.

Training a tokenizer. Course 9 builds BPE from bytes. The book uses a ready-made one (tiktoken).

Real GPU pretraining. Course 10 trains GPT-2 124M on about 10 billion tokens of FineWeb-Edu, with mixed precision and torch.compile. The book's run is a toy by design.

Why not Zero to Hero alone? Its last course ends at a pretrained GPT-2: there's no classifier, no instruction tuning, no LoRA. And its videos build each piece live, with detours, which is great for understanding and slow as a route. Why not the book alone? It trusts PyTorch for gradients and tiktoken for tokens, and its pretraining run is too small to show what real training looks like. The path takes the book's order and plugs the videos in where those gaps sit.

Principle

Follow one resource in order, and go sideways only to answer a question you already have

Switching between two courses with different code and different names costs more than either one alone. Keep the book as the one codebase you grow. Open a Zero to Hero course or a gap card when a chapter leaves you with a "why?", like "what does backward() actually compute?" or "why are the targets shifted by one?".

Buys you: a model that runs at the end of every day, and depth spent where it pays.

What this route simplifies

  1. It's a toy-scale GPT-2. You build a 124M-parameter model from 2019 and pretrain it on 5,145 tokens. Real pretraining runs one pass over billions to trillions of tokens, so overfitting a story isn't the problem it has; cost and stability are.
  2. Real scale changes the engineering, not the idea. Mixed precision, many GPUs splitting the batch and the model, checkpointing, data cleaning. The loss is the same next-token loss you write on Day 4. Zero to Hero 10 shows the single-node version.
  3. Newer models change some parts. Qwen2.5-0.5B, the model the cards measure, uses RoPE instead of a learned position table, shares key/value heads across query heads (GQA), and has a 151,936-token vocabulary against GPT-2's 50,257. LLM basics covers each; the shapes you code still apply.
  4. Instruction tuning is step 1 of post-training. Chat models add preference training and reinforcement learning after it, and that's where reasoning comes from. The reasoning path starts there.
  5. Building isn't serving. Your generate function runs one request with no KV cache. How a server answers thousands at once is the inference path.

Check yourself

1. Why does the path run Zero to Hero 2 and 3 on Day 1, before any book chapter about language models?

Show the answer

Every later chapter calls loss.backward() and an optimizer step. Appendix A teaches the PyTorch calls; micrograd shows what they compute, and the bigram model shows that a language model is just a training problem with a cross-entropy loss. Without them the training loop is something you trust rather than understand.

2. On Day 4 your validation loss stops falling while the training loss keeps dropping. Is your code broken, and what does the path do next?

Show the answer

No: a 5,145-token story trained for several epochs gets memorised (§5.2). The path then loads OpenAI's weights (§5.5) to get a model trained at scale, and offers Zero to Hero 10 as the stretch if you want to do that training yourself.

3. Which three ideas did this course write cards for, and why those three?

Show the answer

Training pairs (ch 2.6 and 5.1), instruction tuning with loss masking (ch 7.2–7.6) and LoRA (appendix E). The book codes each one quickly and the hub had no card for them; the cards measure them on Qwen2.5-0.5B so you know what to look for in your own batches and counts.

Start the route: Day 1 is appendix A and Zero to Hero 2. The first card you'll need is Every position is a training example, on Day 2. After Day 5, test yourself closed-book on Checkpoint: build an LLM.

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Swap the book's 5,145-token story for a text 1,000 times longer and train for the same number of steps. What happens to the gap between training and validation loss, and would you still load OpenAI's weights afterwards? Commit before revealing.

Reveal

The model sees far fewer repeats of each passage, so it has less chance to memorise: the gap between training and validation loss shrinks, and the book notes that training on 60,000 Project Gutenberg books doesn't overfit (§5.2 note). You'd still load OpenAI's weights for a useful model: a few million tokens on a laptop is nowhere near the scale GPT-2 was trained at. The path didn't measure this run.

Why this step?

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Before Day 3, predict what your chapter 4 model will print. GPT-2 124M has a 50,257-token vocabulary and width 768. Work out how many weights the output layer adds if it isn't tied to the embedding table, and what the total is with tying, given an untied total of 163,009,536.

Done when: You have 50,257 × 768 = 38,597,376 for the output layer, and 163,009,536 − 38,597,376 = 124,412,160 with tying: the 124M in the name. Your chapter 4 code should print the 163,009,536.

Hint

Tying means the output layer reuses the embedding table, so its weights are counted once.
