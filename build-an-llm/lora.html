Fine-tune a sliver of the model

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA), parameter-efficient fine-tuning · LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models, Hu et al., Microsoft, 2021 · prepares Raschka appendix E

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Fine-tuning every weight needs a gradient and two Adam numbers per weight, about four times the model's own memory, and every fine-tuned variant is a full copy of the model. Without a cheaper way, adapting even a small model to your data needs far more memory than running it.

Where it fits: You've traced the model and its matrices in the basics course, and chapter 6 of the book fine-tunes GPT-2 into a spam classifier by training its weights directly. This card prepares appendix E, which reruns that chapter 6 classifier with LoRA.

Fine-tuning every weight of a model means storing a gradient and two optimizer numbers for each one, so training needs about four times the model's own memory. LoRA freezes the model and trains a thin detour beside a few of its matrices: on Qwen2.5-0.5B, 0.11% of the numbers. After training, the detour folds back into the weights, so the result runs exactly as fast as the original.

Try this first

Guess first: Qwen2.5-0.5B has 494 million weights, each stored as a 4-byte number. To fine-tune all of them with the Adam optimizer, roughly how much memory do you need before counting activations: 2 GB, 4 GB or 8 GB? And if you train only a small add-on of about half a million numbers, how much then?

Show the answer

Full fine-tuning: 7.90 GB. Every weight needs four numbers: itself, its gradient, and Adam's two running averages. With LoRA: 1.98 GB, because the frozen weights need no gradient and no Adam state, and the 540,672 trained numbers add only 8.7 MB.

Three words first

Fine-tuning

Taking a model that's already trained and training it a little more on your own examples, so it picks up a task, a fact or a style.

Optimizer state

Extra numbers the optimizer keeps for each weight it trains. Adam keeps two: a running average of the gradient (m) and of its square (v).

Low rank

A big matrix built as the product of two thin ones. An 896 × 896 matrix made from 896 × 8 times 8 × 896 has only 8 independent directions, from 14,336 numbers instead of 802,816.

Training every weight costs four copies of the model

← scroll the sketch →

Counted for Qwen2.5-0.5B in float32. Full fine-tuning keeps the weights, one gradient per weight, and Adam's two averages per weight: 4 × 1.98 GB = 7.90 GB. LoRA keeps the weights frozen, so it stores no gradient or Adam state for them. Only the small add-on needs all four copies. Activations (the in-between values saved for the backward pass) come on top in both cases and grow with batch size and text length.

The paper reports the same effect at the largest scale it tried. Fine-tuning GPT-3 175B with Adam took 1.2 TB of GPU memory; with LoRA it took 350 GB. The saving comes from not keeping optimizer state for frozen weights, so it is biggest when the add-on is tiny compared with the model. The saved file shrinks even more: with rank 4 on two matrix types, a GPT-3 checkpoint went from 350 GB to 35 MB (§4.2).

Freeze W, and learn a thin detour beside it

← scroll the sketch →

One of the attention matrices in every Qwen block, q_proj (it makes the queries). The original matrix W is frozen. Next to it sit two thin trained matrices: A squeezes the token's 896 numbers down to 8, and B expands them back to 896. Their output is scaled by alpha / r and added to W's output. The shapes and counts are Qwen's; the B = 0 check was run in the lab.

Written out, a layer that used to compute h = W x now computes

h = W x + (alpha / r) · B A x

W has shape dout × din. A is r × din and B is dout × r, where the rank r is small: 4, 8 or 16. Two choices make it work:

  • B starts at zero, A starts small and random. So B A x is exactly zero at step 0, and the model starts as the base model, not a damaged copy of it. The lab checks this: with B = 0 the logits for What is the capital of India? are identical to the base model's, bit for bit. (If both started random, training would begin from noise added to every wrapped layer. If both started at zero, no gradient could ever reach either of them.)
  • alpha / r sets the size of the detour's push. The paper sets alpha to the first rank it tries and leaves it there, because with Adam, changing alpha is roughly like changing the learning rate (§4.1). Keeping alpha / r fixed means you can try a different rank without retuning everything.
How to read the labels

Qwen's layers have names in its code. q_proj, k_proj, v_proj and o_proj are the four attention matrices (query, key, value, output). gate_proj, up_proj and down_proj are the three feed-forward matrices. The paper calls the first group Wq, Wk, Wv, Wo.

Raschka's appendix E writes the detour as x @ A @ B with A shaped din × r: the same idea, transposed. In both versions, the matrix that starts at zero is B.

A fraction of a percent of the numbers train

Each wrapped matrix adds r × (din + dout) trained numbers. Qwen's v_proj is only 128 numbers tall (grouped-query attention shares keys and values across heads), so its B is small. Measured by wrapping the layers in the lab and counting:

What the paper found

On GPT-3 175B, LoRA trained 10,000× fewer numbers than full fine-tuning and used 3× less GPU memory (abstract). With 4.7 M trained numbers it scored 73.4 on WikiSQL and 91.7 on MultiNLI, against 73.8 and 89.5 for full fine-tuning (Table 4).

Which matrices, which rank

The paper adapted only attention matrices and froze the feed-forward ones (§4.2). With a fixed budget, spreading it over Wq and Wv at rank 4 beat putting it all into Wq at rank 8 (Table 5). For Wq and Wv, even r = 1 was enough: 73.4 on WikiSQL, against 73.5 at r = 64 (Table 6).

Forty steps teach it a new fact, and can wreck what it knew

← scroll the sketch →

Two real runs in the lab, each starting from the base model with LoRA on q_proj and v_proj (r = 8, alpha = 16, 540,672 trained numbers). The questions are the same for every column. Run A taught the fact and overwrote the model's other answers. Run B added two capitals the model already knew to the training sentences, used a lower learning rate, and kept them.

Both runs train on 6 short sentences about Veloria (a country we made up) with 40 steps of AdamW on an M3, all sentences in one batch. Run A's loss fell from 4.06 to 0.001 in about 7 s (0.18 s a step). Run B, with 8 sentences, went from 3.36 to 0.032 in about 10 s. Run B also answered a wording it never saw: Name the capital city of Veloria. gave Brightwater is the capital of Veloria.

A loss near zero on six sentences means the model memorised them, not that it learned well. The only way to know what else changed is to ask it things you didn't train on. Even Run B isn't clean: New Delhi became Delhi.

Failure · fine-tuning
Symptom
After fine-tuning, the model answers every capital question with your new fact. Asked about India, Run A said "Veloria. Brightwater is the capital of Vel…"
Cause
A handful of near-identical examples, a high learning rate (1e-3), and nothing in the data that rewards the old behaviour. The cheapest way to drive this loss down is "always say Brightwater".
Isolate
Before training, write down the base model's answers to a few questions you are not training on. Ask them again after training and compare.
Fix
Mix in examples of what the model should keep, and lower the learning rate. Run B used two known capitals and 3e-4: India and Germany came back right.

LoRA limits how many numbers change, not how much the behaviour changes.

After training, the detour folds into W and costs nothing

← scroll the sketch →

Because the detour is linear, W x + (alpha / r) · B A x equals (W + (alpha / r) · B A) x. So you can compute W' = W + (alpha / r) · B A once and throw A and B away. The lab merged Run B this way: the model went back to exactly 494,032,768 parameters, its logits moved by at most 0.000086 (rounding noise on values around 30), and it still answered Brightwater.

That's the paper's "no additional inference latency" claim (§4.1): a merged model is an ordinary model. You can also keep A and B separate and swap them: one frozen base plus a small file per task. The paper's footnote prices 100 GPT-3 variants at about 354 GB this way, against 35 TB for 100 full copies (§4.2). The catch it names: once merged, one forward pass can't serve requests for two different tasks in the same batch.

Principle

Keep what the model knows frozen, and train only the change

A pretrained model already holds the knowledge. Adapting it to a task needs a change to the weights that the paper argues has a very low "intrinsic rank", so two thin matrices can express it. Freezing W removes its gradients and optimizer state, which is where the memory went.

Buys you: about a quarter of the training memory on Qwen, tiny saved files, and a merged model that runs at the original speed.

Hu et al. 2021, §4.1, §4.2, §7.2

What to look for in appendix E

Appendix E builds directly on chapter 6. It reruns the spam classifier (GPT-2 124M with a new two-class output layer) with LoRA instead of training the whole model, so finish chapter 6 first. While you code it, check three things:

  1. Which layers get wrapped. The book replaces every linear layer, like our "all 7 linear layers" row, not the paper's q and v only. Count the trainable numbers before and after (§E.4).
  2. That step 0 is the base model. B starts at zero, so the classifier's accuracy right after wrapping should equal its accuracy before.
  3. Time per epoch. The book notes that on a model this small, LoRA trained slower than full fine-tuning in chapter 6, because the detour adds work to every forward pass (§E.4). The savings appear when the model is big enough that its gradients and optimizer state are the expensive part.

When LoRA isn't the right tool

  1. Big changes to what the model knows. The paper itself warns that a small rank won't work for every task. Its example: if the new task were in a different language from pretraining, retraining the whole model could clearly beat a small-rank LoRA (§7.2, footnote 6). One fact in 6 sentences is fine; a new domain's worth of knowledge may not be.
  2. Rank, alpha and learning rate still matter. Too high a learning rate overwrote Qwen's other answers in 40 steps. Wrapping more layers, or a higher rank, gives the detour more room, and more room to do damage.
  3. Our runs are a demonstration, not a benchmark. Eight sentences and four questions show the mechanism. They say nothing about quality on a real task. For that, hold out an evaluation set and compare against the base model.
  4. Memory is still mostly the frozen model. At 1.98 GB, LoRA's memory is almost all frozen weights. QLoRA goes further by storing those frozen weights in a compressed low-precision (quantized) form while training the LoRA matrices as usual.

Check yourself

1. Why does freezing W save memory beyond the weights' gradients?

Show the answer

Adam keeps two extra numbers (m and v) for every weight it trains. Frozen weights get no gradient and no Adam state, so three of the four copies disappear for them. On Qwen in float32 that's 7.90 GB down to 1.98 GB.

2. Why is B initialised to zero, and what would go wrong if A were zero too?

Show the answer

B = 0 makes B A x = 0, so training starts exactly at the base model (the lab measured identical logits). If A were also zero, the gradient for B would be zero (it multiplies A x = 0) and so would A's (it multiplies B = 0), so neither could ever move.

3. Run A reached a loss of 0.001. Why is that bad news rather than good news here?

Show the answer

Six near-identical sentences can be memorised. A loss near zero on them says nothing about questions outside the training data, and in Run A those broke: India and Germany both got Veloria answers. Probe questions you didn't train on, and mix in examples of behaviour you want to keep.

Understand and remember

  • Adam trains a weight → it stores the weight, its gradient, m and v (4 numbers) → frozen weights need only the first → Qwen float32 drops from 7.90 GB to 1.98 GB before activations
  • ΔW = B A with r ≪ d → each wrapped matrix trains r × (din + dout) numbers instead of din × dout → q and v at r = 8 train 540,672 numbers, 0.109% of Qwen
  • B starts at 0 → B A x = 0 at step 0 → the model starts exactly at the base model (identical logits) → training only ever adds a learned change
  • few near-identical examples + high learning rate → loss near 0 by memorising → other answers overwritten (India → Veloria) → mix in behaviour to keep, lower the learning rate, and probe questions you didn't train on
  • detail that matters The detour is linear → W x + s·B A x = (W + s·B A) x → merging gives an ordinary matrix with zero extra inference cost, but one merged model can't serve two tasks in the same batch
  • detail that matters alpha / r scales the detour → with Adam, changing alpha acts like changing the learning rate → keep alpha / r fixed and you can change r without retuning

Reference only

  • Qwen layer names q_proj, k_proj, v_proj, o_proj, gate_proj, up_proj, down_proj
  • the book's transposed convention x @ A @ B with A as din × r
  • the paper's GPT-3 numbers: 1.2 TB → 350 GB memory, 350 GB → 35 MB checkpoint
  • the exact counts at r = 4 and 16

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain why LoRA cuts training memory so much more than it cuts the model's size on disk.
    Check
    • training memory is weights + gradients + two Adam numbers per trained weight
    • freezing W removes three of the four copies for 99.9% of the weights
    • the frozen weights themselves still have to be loaded, so the model's size doesn't shrink; only what's saved per task (A and B) does
  2. mechanism Walk through what happens to What is the capital of India? on the first forward pass after wrapping q_proj and v_proj with LoRA, and then on the first backward pass.
    Check
    • forward: each wrapped layer computes W x plus (alpha / r) · B A x, and B = 0 makes the extra term exactly zero, so logits equal the base model's
    • backward: W gets no gradient (frozen); B gets a non-zero gradient because A x isn't zero; A's gradient is zero on this first step because it passes through B = 0
    • after one Adam step B is non-zero, and from then on both A and B train
  3. tradeoff You have a fixed budget of trained numbers. Would you put it all into q_proj at a higher rank, or spread it over q_proj and v_proj at a lower rank? What did the paper find, and what's the cost of going to all seven linear layers?
    Check
    • the paper found spreading over Wq and Wv at rank 4 beat Wq alone at rank 8 (Table 5), and r = 1 already did well for both
    • all seven layers on Qwen at r = 8 trains 4,399,104 numbers (0.89%) vs 540,672: more capacity and more room to overwrite behaviour
    • memory barely moves either way (1.98 to 2.12 GB); the frozen weights dominate
  4. tradeoff Your LoRA run's training loss is 0.001 after 40 steps on 6 sentences. What do you check before trusting it?
    Check
    • ask questions you did not train on and compare with the base model's answers from before training
    • a near-zero loss on a handful of sentences usually means memorising
    • if old answers broke, mix in examples of behaviour to keep and lower the learning rate
