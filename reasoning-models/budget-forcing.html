Say “Wait”, and the model thinks again

Budget forcing · s1K · s1: Simple test-time scaling, Muennighoff, Yang, Shi, Li et al., Stanford / University of Washington / AI2, 2025

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A reasoning model decides for itself when to stop thinking. It sometimes stops early on a wrong answer, and you have no exact way to make it think longer or shorter. Asking in the prompt doesn't work because the model can't count its own tokens.

Where it fits: Builds on chain of thought and test-time compute: spending more tokens at inference can buy accuracy. s1 is the simplest version of that idea, with one sequential trace whose length is controlled from outside, on a model fine-tuned on only 1,000 examples.

A reasoning model decides for itself when to stop thinking, and sometimes it stops too early, on a wrong answer. The s1 paper controls that from outside. When the model tries to stop, block the stop and write “Wait”. When it runs over budget, force the stop. With 1,000 training examples and this one trick, an open 32B model beat o1-preview on competition maths.

Try this first

Guess first: a model has just written “the number of r's in raspberry is 2” and is about to stop thinking. You can't retrain it. You may add one word to the text it is writing before it continues. Which word gives it the best chance to find its own mistake?

Show the answer

“Wait”. The paper tried four options for stretching the thinking twice on the AIME24 maths test. Adding nothing, “Hmm” or “Alternatively” all stayed at 50.0%, and “Wait” reached 53.3% (Table 4). The model has seen “Wait” in the training traces it copied, where the word usually starts a re-check.

Three words first

Thinking tokens

The text a reasoning model writes before its answer. A start marker opens it and an end-of-thinking marker closes it. In s1 these markers are <|im_start|>think and <|im_start|>answer.

Budget forcing

Setting the thinking length from outside the model. To cut it short, insert the end marker. To stretch it, block the end marker and append “Wait”. The model's weights don't change.

Fine-tuning (SFT)

Supervised fine-tuning: train a finished model on example texts until it writes like them. When those texts come from a stronger model, the process is called distillation.

Blocking one token turns a wrong answer into a right one

← scroll the sketch →

The violet boxes are the model thinking. It skips letter 8, settles on 2, and then wants to write the end-of-thinking token. Budget forcing blocks that token and writes “Wait” instead. The model re-reads the word, counts again and answers 3. This is real s1-32B output from the paper's Figure 3, trimmed. It isn't an illustration.

Two levers, both on the same token

Over budget? Force the stop

Once the thinking reaches the maximum number of tokens, insert the end-of-thinking marker (and optionally “Final Answer:”).

The model has to answer now, with its best guess so far.

Stopping too soon? Block it

When the model produces the end-of-thinking marker, drop it and append “Wait”.

The paper does this 2, 4 or 6 times in a row to stretch the thinking further.

There's no extra model and no extra training: budget forcing is a small change to the loop that generates tokens. That's why its control is exact. The obvious alternative is to write “think for up to 2,048 tokens” in the prompt, but the model can't count its own tokens and often overshoots. In the paper's Table 3, prompting hit the budget only 40% of the time, and budget forcing hit it 100% of the time.

More thinking helps, then the curve goes flat

← scroll the sketch →

Every dot is s1-32B on AIME24 (30 problems from the 2024 American Invitational Mathematics Examination). The blue dots were forced to stop early. Left alone, the model thinks for about 5,300 tokens and scores 50.0%. “Wait” ×2 and ×4 lift that to 53.3% and 56.7%, and ×6 adds nothing. The accuracies come from Figure 4a and match Table 5; the token counts are read off the plot, so they're approximate.

On AIME24 that's 27% above o1-preview (56.7 vs 44.6), which is the paper's “up to 27%”. o1-preview still wins clearly on GPQA, a set of PhD-level science questions. AIME24 has only 30 problems, so each one is worth 3.3 points: going from 50.0% to 56.7% means solving two more.

The 1,000 examples were picked to be hard, varied and clean

← scroll the sketch →

The authors started from 59,029 questions and had Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking write a reasoning trace and answer for each. They removed broken items, then anything two Qwen models could already solve, then sampled across 50 subject areas, preferring long traces. Only 53.6% of the kept traces reach the right answer. The authors kept the wrong ones because the goal is to copy the reasoning process, not the answers.

The fine-tune itself took 26 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs. The paper's confidence interval for the 59K-vs-s1K difference runs from −13% to +20%, so the extra 58,000 examples bought no clear gain.

Principle

Extra thinking only pays if the model already knows how to check itself

“Wait” adds no knowledge. It gives the model one more turn to use a habit it copied from its teacher's traces: re-read, recount, fix. The authors' explanation is that pretraining already put the reasoning ability in the model. The 1,000 examples only switch it on, and budget forcing then decides how long it runs.

Buys you: an exact dial for test-time compute (shorter or longer thinking) that needs no retraining.

Muennighoff et al. 2025, §3.1 and §6.1

The failure you will hit: more “Wait” stops paying

Failure · test-time scaling
Symptom
Each extra “Wait” costs more tokens, but accuracy stops rising. In the paper, ×6 scored the same as ×4.
Cause
Once the model has re-checked, another “Wait” has nothing new to find. Blocking the stop too often sends the model into repetitive loops. And every token has to fit in the context window.
Isolate
Plot accuracy against the number of “Wait”s on held-out problems. Then read the traces past the knee of the curve, looking for text that repeats itself.
Fix
Stop at the knee. To scale further, go wide: run several shorter traces and vote, or search over them. In the paper's Figure 7, tree search guided by a step-scoring model scaled better than one long trace.

“Wait” buys a second look. It doesn't buy a better model.

When it isn't the right tool

  1. It flattens, and the context window caps it. The paper names both limits itself (§6.2). When the model was prompted to think for up to 512 steps, 12 of the 30 AIME24 answers didn't fit in the context window (Figure 7).
  2. It's a hack on a model that already reasons. s1K's traces were distilled from Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking, a stronger reasoning model. The untouched Qwen model scores 26.7% on AIME24. Swapping in better teacher traces mattered more than extra “Wait”s: s1.1, trained on DeepSeek-R1 traces, scores 56.7% with no forcing (Table 5).
  3. It doesn't replace RL. DeepSeek-R1, trained with reinforcement learning (RL), scores 79.8% on AIME24. RL is how a model learns to check its work in the first place. The paper lists budget forcing on RL-trained models as future work.

Every forced token also costs latency. See thinking tokens for what a long trace costs in time, think longer or be bigger? for when extra inference compute beats a bigger model, and reasoning from reward alone for how RL creates the re-checking habit that “Wait” relies on.

Check yourself

1. Why does budget forcing hit its token budget 100% of the time, while “think for up to 2,048 tokens” in the prompt doesn't?

Show the answer

The model can't count its own tokens, so a prompt only asks it to stop and it often overshoots. Budget forcing acts on the generation loop: the decoder inserts or blocks the end-of-thinking token itself, so the limit is exact.

2. s1-32B goes from 50.0% to 56.7% on AIME24 with budget forcing. How many more problems is that, and why should you be careful with it?

Show the answer

AIME24 has 30 problems, so each one is 3.3 points and the gain is 2 problems. With such a small test, a couple of lucky or unlucky problems move the score a lot. The paper also reports that its scores shift between runs (§B).

3. Training on all 59K questions takes 56 times the GPU-hours of s1K. Why didn't the authors just do that?

Show the answer

It wasn't clearly better: 53.3% vs 50.0%, with a confidence interval that includes zero. Which 1,000 you pick matters far more. Random, evenly spread or longest-only sets of 1,000 scored 26.7–36.7%.

Understand and remember

  • The model ends thinking by emitting an end-of-thinking token → budget forcing controls that one token from outside → insert it to cut thinking short, block it and append “Wait” to stretch it → exact control with no retraining (100% control vs 40% for a length instruction in the prompt)
  • Training traces often say “Wait” before a re-check → after fine-tuning, the model continues an appended “Wait” by re-reading its work → it can catch a slip (raspberry: 2 → 3) → AIME24 50.0% → 53.3% (×2) → 56.7% (×4)
  • Extra thinking only re-uses what the model already knows → after a few re-checks there is nothing new to find, and blocking the stop more often causes repetitive loops → the curve is flat from ×4 to ×6, and the context window sets a hard cap
  • Picking hard, varied, clean questions matters more than quantity → s1K (1,000) scores 50.0% vs 53.3% for all 59K at 56× less training compute, while random or longest-only sets of 1,000 score 26.7–36.7% → a short fine-tune switches on reasoning the pretrained model already has
  • detail that matters The traces were distilled from Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking → the habit of re-checking was copied from a stronger reasoner → s1.1, trained on DeepSeek-R1 traces, reaches 56.7% with no forcing, so the teacher matters as much as the “Wait”s
  • detail that matters AIME24 has 30 problems → each one is 3.3 points → the whole budget-forcing gain (50.0 → 56.7) is two problems, so small differences between methods are noisy

Reference only

  • Pool: 59,029 questions from 16 sources → 51,581 after the quality filters → 24,496 after the difficulty filter (drop anything Qwen2.5-7B or 32B solves) → 1,000 across 50 domains
  • Fine-tuning Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct: 5 epochs, 315 steps, 26 minutes on 16 H100s (7 GPU-hours); 59K took 394 GPU-hours
  • Delimiters: <|im_start|>think and <|im_start|>answer
  • Table 1 (AIME24 / MATH500 / GPQA): s1-32B 56.7 / 93.0 / 59.6; o1-preview 44.6 / 85.5 / 73.3; Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct 26.7 / 84.0 / 49.0
  • Only 53.6% of s1K traces reach the right answer; the wrong ones were kept on purpose

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Think longer or be bigger?: Snell et al. spend test-time compute on search and revision; s1 spends it on one longer trace. What is fundamentally different, and when would you pick each?

Check
  • s1 is purely sequential: one trace stretched by “Wait”
  • search samples many candidates and needs a verifier or reward model
  • sequential reuses earlier reasoning but flattens and hits the context limit
  • pick search when you have a good verifier and parallel hardware; pick budget forcing when you want a simple, exact dial

vs Reasoning from reward alone: R1-Zero learned to re-check through RL; s1 copies re-checking from distilled traces and triggers it with “Wait”. Which one creates the habit, and how does each fail?

Check
  • RL creates the behaviour from reward; s1 imitates a teacher that already had it
  • s1 is capped by its teacher and its training traces (only 53.6% correct)
  • RL is costly and unstable but can go past the teacher (r1 79.8% vs s1-32B 56.7% on AIME24)
  • budget forcing can sit on top of an RL model too, which the paper lists as future work

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Take the original Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, with no s1K fine-tuning, and append “Wait” every time it tries to stop. Would you expect it to gain as much as s1-32B did? Commit before revealing.

Reveal

Probably not. The base model never learned that “Wait” starts a re-check, and it doesn't use the think/answer markers that budget forcing acts on. The paper only applies budget forcing to fine-tuned s1 models. Its explanation (§6.1) is that the fine-tune switches on reasoning from pretraining and “Wait” then extends it. Without that switch there is little to extend. This is an inference from the paper, not a result it reports.
