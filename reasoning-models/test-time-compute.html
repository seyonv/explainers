Think longer or be bigger?

Test-time compute, inference-time scaling, compute-optimal scaling · Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters, Snell, Lee, Xu & Kumar, UC Berkeley & Google DeepMind, 2024

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Making a model better usually means training a bigger one, which is expensive. A smaller model could instead spend more compute on each question, but nobody knew when that pays off or how to spend it.

Where it fits: Builds on self-consistency (many samples, then a vote) and process reward models (a verifier that scores steps). This card asks how to spend a fixed answering budget, and when it beats a bigger model.

You have extra compute to spend. You can train a bigger model, or let a smaller one work harder on each question by trying many times or revising its answer. On questions the small model can sometimes solve, working harder beat a 14× bigger model. On the hardest questions, it didn't.

Try this first

Guess first: a model gets one question right 40% of the time and another 0% of the time. You give it 16 independent tries at each and a perfect checker that spots any right answer. How much does each question gain?

Show the answer

The 40% question goes to 99.97%: the chance that all 16 tries miss is 0.6¹⁶. The 0% question stays at 0%, because a checker can only pick a right answer that is already there. A question the model gets right 2% of the time rises to 27.6%. These numbers are illustrative, and real checkers aren't perfect.

Three words first

Test-time compute

Compute spent while answering a question, after training is done. More tries, longer reasoning, or more revisions all cost more of it.

Verifier

A second model that scores answers. Here it's a process reward model (PRM), which scores every step of a solution.

Revision

The model reads its own earlier attempts at the same question and writes a new one that tries to fix their mistakes.

Two knobs: search wider, or revise deeper

Search against a verifier

Best-of-N: sample N whole solutions and keep the one the verifier scores highest. Beam search: grow solutions one step at a time and keep only the best-scored partial ones.

Beam search beat best-of-N at small budgets. At large budgets it fell behind, because it ended up gaming the verifier, for example with repeated filler steps or one- or two-step solutions.

Revise its own answer

The model (PaLM 2-S*) was fine-tuned to read up to four earlier attempts and write a better one. Asked to revise without that training, models mostly don't improve.

At the same number of attempts, revising in sequence beat sampling in parallel. But about 38% of right answers got revised into wrong ones, so a verifier or a vote still picks the final answer.

Searching wide helps when the model needs a different approach. Revising helps when its first try is nearly right. That's why easy questions did best with all the budget on revisions, and harder ones with a mix of both.

Snell et al. 2024, §5.3 (Figure 3), §6.1 (Figure 6) and §6.2 (Figure 7)

Choosing per question did the same work with up to 4× less compute

"Compute-optimal" here means: for each question, use whichever method and settings work best at that difficulty. To sort questions by difficulty, the paper sampled 2,048 answers per question and split the questions into 5 bins. It did this once with the true pass rate and once with the verifier's average score, which needs no answer key.

Snell et al. 2024, §3.2, §5.3, §6.2 and §8

Against a 14× bigger model, it depends on difficulty and on how much you'll use the model

To compare fairly, the paper matches total FLOPs (floating-point operations, a count of compute). A bigger model costs more to train and also costs more every time it answers. So the more questions you'll ask it, the less spare compute the small model gets.

The paper captures this with R: tokens generated at inference divided by tokens used in pretraining. Using its formula, we worked out that the small model can spend about 258× its one-answer compute when R is 0.16, and only about 16× when R is 22.

Principle

Extra thinking helps where the model is close; a bigger model helps where it's lost

Test-time compute can only select or refine answers the model can already produce. When the model sometimes gets a question right, more tries or revisions turn "sometimes" into "usually". When it never does, nothing is there to select, and only more capability helps.

Buys you: a rule for allocating compute. Estimate how hard each question is for this model, then spend accordingly.

Snell et al. 2024, §7 takeaways and §8

The failure you will hit: more search, lower accuracy

Failure · search
Symptom
You raise the beam-search budget from 4 to 256 and accuracy on easy questions goes down.
Cause
Search optimises the verifier's score, not correctness. Pushed hard, it finds what the verifier wrongly likes, such as repetitive low-content steps or very short solutions. On easy questions best-of-N was already finding right answers, so stronger search only finds the verifier's mistakes.
Isolate
Split your accuracy by difficulty bin. In the paper, beam search got worse with budget on bins 1–2 and better than best-of-N on bins 3–4.
Fix
Use best-of-N or revisions on easy questions and beam search on medium ones. A better verifier also helps.

A search is only as good as the scorer it optimises.

Snell et al. 2024, §5.3 (Figure 3) and Appendix M

When extra test-time compute isn't worth it

  1. The hardest questions. On the hardest fifth of questions (bin 5), "no method makes much meaningful progress".
  2. You don't have a good verifier. Search and selection need one. The paper had to train its own PRM: one trained on the public PRM800K labels (from Let's Verify Step by Step) was easy to fool, even with plain best-of-N.
  3. Your model hasn't been trained to revise or verify. The paper fine-tuned PaLM 2-S* for both, because simply prompting a model to fix its answer doesn't work.
  4. Heavy use. If the model will answer a huge number of questions (large R), the compute is better spent on a bigger model.
  5. Latency. Every extra try or revision is more tokens the user waits for. See thinking tokens.

The setting is narrow: one model family, MATH (high-school competition maths) and its 500-question test set. Later systems like s1 and R1 train a model to spend more compute by itself, by writing a longer chain of thought.

Check yourself

1. Why do easy questions do best with sequential revisions, while harder ones do best with a mix that includes parallel samples?

Show the answer

On easy questions the first attempt is usually close, so refining it locally pays off. Harder questions often need a different approach altogether, and independent parallel samples explore more approaches.

2. Why does the same test-time strategy beat the 14× model at R = 0.16 but lose at R = 22?

Show the answer

The bigger model's extra cost is mostly training when inference is light, so matching its FLOPs gives the small model a lot of spare compute per question (about 258×). When inference is heavy, the bigger model's extra per-answer cost dominates and the small model gets only about 16×.

3. How can "compute-optimal" scaling know a question's difficulty without the answer?

Show the answer

It bins questions by the verifier's average score over many sampled answers instead of the true pass rate. That needs no answer key, but it costs compute, and the paper didn't count that cost.

Understand and remember

  • Test-time compute can only select or refine answers the model can already produce → if the pass rate is near 0, more tries add nothing → on the hardest questions a bigger model is the only lever
  • Easy question → the first try is nearly right → local revisions fix it → all-sequential is best; harder question → it needs a different approach → parallel samples explore → a mix of both is best
  • Search optimises the verifier's score → pushed hard, it exploits the verifier's mistakes (filler steps, one- or two-step solutions) → beam search hurts easy questions at large budgets and helps medium ones
  • A bigger model costs more to train and on every answer → FLOPs matching gives the small model ≈258× its one-answer compute at R = 0.16 but only ≈16× at R = 22 → test-time compute wins with light usage and on easy questions, pretraining wins with heavy usage on hard ones
  • detail that matters Revision models turn about 38% of right answers wrong → you still need a verifier or a vote to pick the final answer from the chain
  • detail that matters Difficulty bins come from 2,048 samples per question → choosing per bin gives 2–4× efficiency, but that estimation cost isn't counted

Reference only

  • Setting: PaLM 2-S*, MATH 12k train / 500 test; models fine-tuned to revise and to verify
  • PRM trained from Monte Carlo rollouts, not human labels; final-step score; best-of-N weighted selection
  • Compute-optimal vs best-of-N: revisions 64 vs 256 generations (Fig. 8); search nearly matches 16 vs 64 (Fig. 4)
  • Fig. 1 revisions vs 14× model: R = 0.16 easy/medium/hard +21.6 / +27.8 / +11.8%; R = 22 +5.4 / −24.3 / −37.2%
  • FLOPs: pretraining 6·N·D, inference 2·N·D; allowed multiplier M + 3(M − 1)/R

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose someone builds a perfect verifier that never makes mistakes. Which of the paper's findings would change, and which would stay?

Reveal

Search would stop degrading at large budgets on easy questions, because there would be no verifier errors to exploit. Best-of-N would get close to 'right if any sample is right'. But the hardest questions would stay stuck: a perfect checker still can't pick a right answer the model never produces, so a bigger model would still win there.
