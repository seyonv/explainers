Show your working, and a big model gets the maths right

Chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting · Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models, Wei et al., Google, 2022

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A language model writes its answer one token at a time, and on a multi-step word problem the answer is often the very next token. One pass through the network has to do all the steps at once, and it often gets them wrong.

Where it fits: Builds on next-token prediction from the GPT build: the model only ever predicts the next token from what is already written. This card changes what gets written before the answer. Every later card in the course (voting, step grading, RL-trained thinking) starts from chains of thought.

Ask a language model a two-step word problem and it often blurts out a wrong number, because the answer is the very next thing it writes. Show it a few examples that write out the steps first, and it copies the habit. On a large enough model, that alone tripled its score on grade-school maths.

Try this first

Guess first: you add worked steps to the examples in a prompt. Does a small model (8 billion parameters) get better at maths word problems too?

Show the answer

No. PaLM 8B went from 4.9% to 4.1% on GSM8K. Small models copy the format and write fluent steps that don't add up. The big gains only show up in much larger models (PaLM 62B and 540B in the paper's table).

Three words first

Token

A chunk of text, roughly a word or part of one. A language model writes one token at a time, each one a guess at what comes next (next-token prediction).

Few-shot prompt

A prompt that starts with a few solved examples (question, then answer) before the real question. The model copies their pattern. Nothing is retrained.

Chain of thought

The steps written between question and answer, in plain words: "23 − 20 = 3. 3 + 6 = 9." The idea is to put them in the examples, so the model writes its own.

The only change is the example, and the answer flips from wrong to right

Both prompts carry the same example and new question. On the right, the example's answer also shows its worked steps, so the model writes steps of its own and lands on 9. On the left it answers at once and says 27. The question and both outputs are the paper's (Figure 1); the real prompt held eight such examples.

Written steps are a scratchpad the next token can read

← scroll the sketch →

Each token costs one pass through the network, and one pass can only do so much. Answering directly squeezes all the arithmetic into one pass. With steps, each pass does one small sum and writes the result down, and later tokens look back at it through attention. The steps spread the work over many passes and keep every partial result in view.

The paper tested the obvious shortcuts on LaMDA 137B, and none of them worked. More tokens alone doesn't help, and steps written after the answer don't help either:

GSM8K solve rate, LaMDA 137B (Wei et al. 2022, Table 6)
Standard prompt (answer only)6.5%
Write only the equation, then answer5.4%
Write dots, as many as the equation has characters6.4%
Answer first, then the steps6.1%
Steps in words, then the answer14.3%

The steps only help a model big enough to get them right

← scroll the sketch →

These are the paper's numbers on GSM8K, a set of grade-school maths word problems. At 8B the steps make things slightly worse; at 540B they lift the score from 17.9% to 56.9%, past a GPT-3 that was fine-tuned for the task and given a separate checker model (55%). The paper calls this an emergent ability of scale and puts the threshold near 100B parameters; in its table every model under 10B got worse with steps, while PaLM 62B already jumped from 9.6% to 29.9%. The gain is also biggest on the hardest problems. On one-step sums (MAWPS SingleOp) PaLM 540B scored 94.1% with and without steps.

Principle

A model can only use the steps it writes down

Every token is a fixed amount of computation. A problem that needs several steps either fits into the one pass before the answer, or it gets spread over written steps that later tokens can read. The prompt doesn't make the model smarter. It changes what the model writes, so the model has room to use what it already knows.

Buys you: a big jump on multi-step problems with no training at all, just eight hand-written examples.

Wei et al. 2022, §2 and §3.3

The failure you will hit: steps that sound right and aren't

Failure · small model
Symptom
You add worked steps to the prompt of a small model and accuracy drops. LaMDA 420M went from 2.6% to 0.4% on GSM8K.
Cause
The model copies the shape of the steps but can't do each one reliably. The paper says small models wrote "fluent but illogical" chains, and one bad step carries through to the answer.
Isolate
Read twenty chains. If the steps are well-formed sentences with wrong sums or made-up facts, the format is fine and the model is too small for the problem.
Fix
Use a bigger model, or a problem with fewer steps. Or train the habit in (later cards: STaR, R1-Zero).

Steps only help if each step is within the model's reach.

When chain of thought isn't the right tool

  1. Small models. Every model under 10B parameters in this paper did worse with steps on GSM8K; the paper puts the threshold for real gains near 100B.
  2. Easy problems. One-step questions don't need a scratchpad; the gain on them was zero or tiny.
  3. The steps aren't a window into the network. The paper says plainly there's "no guarantee of correct reasoning paths", and leaves open whether the network is "actually reasoning". Of 50 right answers it checked by hand, 2 had reached the answer by coincidence through wrong steps. Read the steps as a story the model wrote, not a trace of its computation.
  4. Cost. Every step is more tokens to generate, so answers get slower and pricier. The paper notes that needing a huge model makes it "costly to serve". See thinking tokens for what that does to latency.

One chain can still go wrong at any step. The next card samples many chains and lets them vote: self-consistency.

Check yourself

1. The prompt never tells the model to "think step by step". How does it end up writing steps?

Show the answer

It copies the pattern of the examples. Each example's answer shows its steps before "The answer is …", so the most likely continuation for the new question is steps too. No weights change.

2. If steps help because they add computation, why did writing dots do nothing?

Show the answer

Dots add passes but carry no information. The steps help because each pass writes down a partial result (like the 3 in 23 − 20 = 3) that later tokens can read. Dots scored 6.4% against 6.5% with no steps and 14.3% with real steps.

3. A chain of thought ends in the right answer. Does that mean the steps are right?

Show the answer

No. In the paper's hand check, 2 of 50 right answers came from wrong steps. The steps are text the model wrote, and nothing checks them. Grading the steps is what the process rewards card is about.

Understand and remember

  • Few-shot examples show steps before 'The answer is' → the most likely continuation for the new question is steps too → the model writes its own chain with no retraining
  • Each token is one fixed-size pass → a direct answer must do every step in one pass → written steps split the work over many passes and leave each partial result in the text → later tokens attend to it
  • Dots instead of steps (6.4%) ≈ no steps (6.5%), and steps after the answer (6.1%) don't help → the gain needs informative steps written before the answer → real steps 14.3% (LaMDA 137B, GSM8K)
  • Each step must be within the model's reach → small models write fluent but illogical steps and errors carry to the answer → PaLM 8B 4.9% → 4.1%, PaLM 540B 17.9% → 56.9% on GSM8K
  • detail that matters Gains are biggest on multi-step problems → one-step sums barely change (MAWPS SingleOp, PaLM 540B 94.1% both ways) → use it where there are steps to spread out
  • detail that matters Nothing checks the steps → right answers can come from wrong steps (2 of 50 in the paper's hand check) → read a chain as text the model wrote, not a trace of its computation

Reference only

  • Eight hand-written exemplars for the maths sets (four for AQuA), greedy decoding, no fine-tuning
  • PaLM 540B with CoT (56.9%) beat fine-tuned GPT-3 175B with a verifier (55%) on GSM8K
  • Different annotators' exemplars all beat standard prompting (LaMDA 137B GSM8K: 15.5%, 17.6%, concise 11.1% vs 6.5%)
  • GSM8K = grade-school maths word problems (Cobbe et al. 2021)

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Ask many times, and let the answers vote: Chain of thought writes one chain greedily; self-consistency samples many and votes. What does each fix, and how does each one fail?

Check
  • CoT gives the model room to work through steps
  • one greedy chain can still slip at any step
  • voting cancels scattered slips because wrong chains rarely agree
  • voting costs N times the tokens and fails when the model is wrong the same way every time

vs Reasoning from reward alone: This paper gets steps by showing examples in the prompt. R1-Zero gets long chains with no examples. What changed, and why does the newer approach not need the exemplars?

Check
  • CoT prompting changes the input, never the weights
  • R1-Zero trains the weights with RL, rewarding right final answers
  • the habit of writing long steps is learned because it earns reward
  • both rely on steps written before the answer

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose you drop the eight worked examples and just add 'Let's think step by step' after the question (zero-shot). On GSM8K with PaLM 540B, where do you expect it to land relative to 17.9% (no steps) and 56.9% (eight worked examples)?

Reveal

In between: 43.0%, as reported in the self-consistency paper (Table 8). The instruction alone gets the model writing steps, but the worked examples still teach a better format. Sampling 40 such chains and voting lifts it to 69.2%.
