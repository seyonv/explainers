Learn from your own right answers

STaR, Self-Taught Reasoner, rationale bootstrapping · STaR: Bootstrapping Reasoning With Reasoning, Zelikman, Wu, Mu & Goodman, Stanford, 2022

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Training a model to show its working needs thousands of worked solutions, and people are slow and expensive to write them. A few worked examples in the prompt help, but a model prompted that way still trails one fine-tuned on plain answers.

Where it fits: Builds on chain of thought (write the reasoning before the answer). Here the model's own chains of thought become its training data, filtered by an answer key. It's the first step towards reinforcement learning (RL) for reasoning.

Showing its working makes a model better at reasoning, but teaching it that habit needs thousands of worked solutions, and people are slow to write them. STaR has the model write its own, keeps only the ones that reach the right answer, and trains on those. Then it does it again with the better model.

Try this first

Guess first: you have 10 worked examples and 9,741 multiple-choice questions with an answer key but no working. With the 10 examples in its prompt, the model gets 36.6% right. How could you get thousands of worked solutions to train on without writing any?

Show the answer

Let the model attempt every question, check its answer against the key, and keep the working only when the answer is right. Train on that pile and repeat. On this dataset, that took the same model to 72.5%.

Three words first

Rationale

The written reasoning a model produces before its final answer. It's the same thing as a chain of thought.

Fine-tune

Keep training an already-trained model on a small new dataset, so it learns to write in that dataset's style.

Rationalization

Retry a question the model got wrong, this time with the correct answer given as a hint, so it can reason backwards to it.

Only reasoning that reaches the key gets trained on

← scroll the sketch →

Each training question goes to the model, which writes its reasoning and an answer. A check compares only the final answer with the key. Right answers are kept and wrong ones are discarded, then retried with the answer as a hint. Notice the square question: its reasoning is wrong but its answer is right, so it is kept anyway. The three questions are illustrative and ours, not the paper's.

Without the hint, the loop runs out of things to learn

The plain loop only ever trains on questions the model already gets right. The paper found that it eventually stops solving new training questions, because the questions it fails give it no training signal at all.

Rationalization gives the hard questions a way in. The model is shown the correct answer as a hint and asked to explain it. If its explanation reaches that answer, the explanation is saved without the hint, as if the model had worked it out alone. Each new model is also trained from the original starting model, not the last round's, to avoid overfitting.

Plain loop

Adding numbers, step by step: the model learned one length at a time. It got poor at sums of n digits until it was good at n − 1.

Questions it couldn't yet answer at all contributed nothing until the shorter sums were learned.

With rationalization

The model could learn many lengths at once. Two-digit addition went from <1% to 32% after one round.

On CommonsenseQA it lifted the final score from 68.8% to 72.5%, by adding training data from 8.5% more of the questions.

Overall, after 16 rounds of STaR the model added numbers of up to five digits correctly 89.5% of the time. A model trained on 10,000 plain answers, with no working, reached 76.3%.

Zelikman et al. 2022, §3.2, §4.3 (Figure 4) and Table 1

A 6-billion-parameter model got within half a point of one 30× its size

← scroll the sketch →

These are the paper's numbers on CommonsenseQA, a five-choice quiz of everyday knowledge. The model is GPT-J (6B parameters). STaR with rationalization scored 72.5%, against 73.0% for GPT-3 fine-tuned to give answers only, a model the paper calls about 30× larger. STaR beat fine-tuning the same model on every answer (60.0%) while training on only 86.7% of the questions.

On grade-school maths, the gain is real but the score stays low

GSM8K is a set of word problems that need two to eight calculation steps. Here STaR almost doubled the fine-tuned baseline, but it trained on less than a third of the questions. That's because the model could only produce a right answer, with or without a hint, on that many.

Principle

Train on your own successful attempts, and let only the final answer decide what counts as successful

The paper points out that this is a simple form of reinforcement learning (RL): the reward is 1 when the answer matches the key and 0 when it doesn't. Discarding a wrong attempt is the same as giving it zero reward. Training on a kept attempt makes that kind of reasoning more likely next time.

Buys you: a large training set of worked solutions from a handful of examples and an answer key. Nobody has to grade the reasoning.

This is the seed of the idea behind R1-Zero and GRPO. They also sample many attempts and reward the right ones, but they keep updating the model as it samples, and they push down on the failures too.

Zelikman et al. 2022, §3.1 (Equations 1–2)

The failure you will hit: more samples, worse reasoning

Failure · training data
Symptom
You sample each question several times at a higher temperature (more randomness) to collect more right answers, and the trained model gets worse. On arithmetic, its working drifts into nonsense.
Cause
More random attempts produce more right answers reached by wrong reasoning, and the answer check keeps them all. The paper found that training on that reasoning stops the model from generalising.
Isolate
Read a sample of the kept rationales by hand, especially on multiple-choice or yes/no questions, where a guess is right 20–50% of the time.
Fix
Sample at low temperature and use rationalization to reach the failed questions, which is what the paper did. Or check the steps as well as the answer, with a process reward model.

The answer check can't tell a sound rationale from a lucky one.

Zelikman et al. 2022, §5 "Temperature" and §6

When STaR isn't the right tool

  1. You need an answer key. The whole filter is "does the answer match?". Open-ended tasks with no checkable answer have nothing to filter on.
  2. The model must already be above chance. The first round needs some right answers to keep. GPT-2 couldn't get started, even on addition.
  3. Lucky reasoning gets kept. On a five-choice quiz, a random answer is right about 20% of the time whatever the reasoning. The paper names yes/no tasks as an open problem. Grading the steps, as in process rewards, is one response.
  4. It only learns from what it can already sometimes do. Questions it never gets right, even with a hint, never enter the training set. On GSM8K that was over 70% of them.

Check yourself

1. Why does the plain STaR loop eventually stop improving?

Show the answer

It only trains on questions it answers correctly. Questions it keeps failing produce nothing to train on, so once it has learned everything it can sometimes solve, it has no new signal. Rationalization adds the failed questions back in through the hint.

2. Why is the hint removed before a rationalized example goes into the training set?

Show the answer

At test time there is no hint. Training on "question → reasoning → answer" without the hint teaches the model to produce that reasoning by itself, as if it had found it unaided.

3. In the sketch, why is the square question's rationale a problem even though it was kept "correctly"?

Show the answer

Its reasoning computed the perimeter, and 4 × 4 = 16 happens to equal the area too. The filter only checks the answer, so it keeps the wrong method, and training makes "perimeter = area" more likely next time.

Understand and remember

  • Model writes reasoning + answer for every training question → only answers matching the key are kept → fine-tune on the kept set → the next model writes better reasoning → repeat until accuracy plateaus
  • Plain loop trains only on questions it already solves → failed questions give no signal → improvement stalls → rationalization (answer given as a hint, then hidden) brings hard questions into the training set
  • The filter checks only the final answer → right answers reached by wrong reasoning are kept → more random sampling or high-chance tasks (multiple choice, yes/no) let more of them in → training on them hurts generalisation
  • Keep = reward 1, discard = reward 0 → STaR is a simple policy gradient (Eq. 1–2) → R1-Zero and GRPO keep the idea but update continuously and push down on failures
  • detail that matters Each round fine-tunes from the original pretrained model, not the last round's → avoids overfitting to its own earlier outputs
  • detail that matters The model must already be above chance → the first round needs right answers to keep → GPT-2 couldn't bootstrap even addition

Reference only

  • Model: GPT-J, 6B parameters; first round 40 training steps, +20% per round
  • CommonsenseQA dev (Table 1): few-shot CoT 36.6%, fine-tuned on answers 60.0%, STaR 68.8%, STaR + rationalization 72.5%, GPT-3 fine-tuned 73.0%
  • GSM8K test (Table 2): fine-tuned on answers 5.8%, STaR 10.1% (25.0% of data), + rationalization 10.7% (28.7%)
  • Arithmetic: 89.5% after 16 rounds vs 76.3% for 10,000 plain answers; 2-digit addition from under 1% to 32% after one round with rationalization

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose you skip the hint-removal step and train on rationalized examples with the hint left in the prompt. What would the trained model do on a test question, where there is no hint?

Reveal

It would have learned to explain an answer it's been handed, not to find one. At test time there is no hint to lean on, so the gain from rationalization would mostly disappear. Removing the hint is what makes the example teach 'question → reasoning → answer'.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Write one training question with an answer key where a plausible wrong method gives the right answer, like the square in the sketch (perimeter 4 × 4 = 16 = area 4²). Then write one where the same wrong method gives a wrong answer, and say which of the two STaR keeps.

Done when: You have two questions. In the first, the wrong method matches the key and STaR keeps it. In the second, it misses and STaR discards it. Example: a 5 cm square, where the perimeter is 20 but the area is 25.

Hint

Look for a shape or rule where two different formulas coincide at one value: side 4 for a square's perimeter vs area, or 2 + 2 = 2 × 2.
