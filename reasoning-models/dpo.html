Skip the reward model

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) · Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model, Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning, Finn, Stanford, 2023

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: RLHF teaches a model people's preferences by training a separate reward model and then running reinforcement learning against it. That means extra models, sampling inside the training loop, and a pipeline the DPO paper calls complex and often unstable.

Where it fits: Builds on the RLHF card (reward model from preference pairs, then PPO with a KL leash). DPO keeps the same goal and the same pairs, but replaces both stages with one classification-style loss.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why can the reward be read off the policy's own log-probs once you have a frozen reference?
  2. Why is the loss exactly log 2 before training, even when the reference ranks the pair backwards?
  3. What sets how hard one step pushes on a pair, and why does that weighting stop the model degenerating?
  4. What does DPO give up by learning only from pairs collected in advance, compared with PPO sampling as it trains?
  5. What does the dial β change about how far the policy moves from the reference?

The usual way to teach a model what people prefer (RLHF) has two hard stages: train a separate reward model on pairs of answers, then chase its score with reinforcement learning. DPO trains the model straight on the pairs, with a loss as simple as a yes/no classifier. It aims at the same goal, with no reward model and no sampling during training.

Try this first

Guess first: before training, the starting model gives the rejected answer a higher score (log-prob −12.0) than the chosen one (−14.0). Is DPO's loss on this pair high, low, or exactly in the middle?

Show the answer

Exactly in the middle: 0.693, which is log 2. DPO only scores how far the model has moved from where it started. At the start it hasn't moved, so the margin is 0, however the starting model ranked the two answers.

Three words first

Preference pair

One prompt and two answers, where a person picked the better one: the chosen answer and the rejected answer.

Log-prob

How likely a model finds a whole answer: the log-probabilities of its tokens added up. Closer to 0 means more likely, so −11 beats −14.

Reference model

The model you are training is called the policy. The reference is a frozen copy of where the policy started (the fine-tuned assistant model). It never changes.

The reward is hiding in the model's own probabilities

← scroll the sketch →

For each answer, compare the policy's log-prob with the frozen reference's. Multiply the change by β (a dial, 0.1 by default in the paper) and you have that answer's implicit reward. The chosen answer went up by 3.0, the rejected one went down by 3.0, so the margin is 0.60 and the loss is 0.437. The log-probs are illustrative, and so is the prompt.

One line of algebra removes a whole model

RLHF's goal is "earn a high reward, but don't drift far from the reference". That goal has a known best answer: the reference model, with each answer's probability scaled up by its reward. Flip that around and the reward can be read off the policy itself: β × log(policy ÷ reference). In a chosen-vs-rejected comparison every other term cancels, so you can train on the pairs directly.

loss = −log σ( β·[log π(chosen) − log πref(chosen)]
           − β·[log π(rejected) − log πref(rejected)] )

Here σ is the sigmoid, which squashes any number into 0 to 1. This is the same loss a classifier uses to say "A, not B". The paper's Eq. 7.

RLHF with PPO

Train a reward model on the pairs. Then loop: sample answers from the policy, score them, update with reinforcement learning, and keep a penalty for drifting from the reference.

Several models in memory and sampling inside the training loop. The paper calls it "complex and often unstable".

DPO

One loss on the pairs you already have. Only two models are involved: the policy and the frozen reference, which is only read.

No reward model and no sampling while training. It is ordinary supervised training with an unusual loss.

The push is strongest on pairs the model has backwards

← scroll the sketch →

Every step raises the chosen answer's log-prob and lowers the rejected one's. How hard it pushes depends on how wrong the current ordering is: 0.057 when the pair is backwards, 0.035 once it's right. The paper (§4) found this weighting matters: a plain "raise one, lower the other" loss without it made the model degenerate.

Principle

If the best answer to your objective has a closed form, train toward it directly

RLHF learns a reward, then runs RL to find the policy that reward implies. DPO notices the policy and the reward determine each other, so it fits the policy and gets the reward for free.

Buys you: preference training that is about as simple and stable as ordinary fine-tuning.

Rafailov et al. 2023, §4 and §5.1

When DPO isn't the right tool

  1. It only learns from the pairs you collected. The data is gathered up front (offline). PPO samples fresh answers from the policy as it trains; DPO never tries its own new answers.
  2. It can over-fit the pairs. The loss keeps falling as the margin grows, and nothing stops it pushing a rejected answer ever lower. The authors list reward over-optimisation in DPO as an open question (§7).
  3. It is only as good as the preference data. Every bias and mistake in the labels goes straight into the model, and there's no separate reward model to inspect.
  4. The paper stopped at 6B parameters (§7).

When answers can be checked by a rule (a maths answer, a passing test), you don't need preference pairs at all. RL with verifiable rewards samples answers, checks them and rewards the right ones. That is a different setting, covered in reasoning from reward alone. The RL ideas behind it start in make good samples more likely, and the pipeline DPO replaces is in learn what people prefer, then chase it.

Check yourself

1. Why does DPO need the frozen reference model at all?

Show the answer

The implicit reward is the policy's log-prob relative to the reference. That keeps training anchored to where it started, which plays the role of RLHF's "don't drift" penalty. Without it, the loss would just raise and lower raw probabilities with nothing holding it back.

2. One pair has margin −0.30 and another +0.60. Which gets the bigger push, and why?

Show the answer

The −0.30 pair: its weight σ(0.30) = 0.574 beats σ(−0.60) = 0.354. The model currently ranks that pair backwards, so it has the most to fix.

3. You're training a coder where every answer runs against unit tests. DPO on preference pairs, or RL on test results?

Show the answer

RL on test results. A test is an exact, free check, and the model learns from its own fresh attempts. DPO needs people to label pairs, and it only ever sees the answers in those pairs.

Understand and remember

  • RLHF's goal (high reward, small drift from the reference) has a closed-form best policy → flip it and reward = β × log(policy ÷ reference) + a term that cancels within a pair → train the policy on pairs directly, no reward model, no RL loop
  • Implicit reward measures movement from the frozen reference → at the start the policy equals the reference → both rewards 0, margin 0, loss log 2 = 0.693, whatever the reference preferred
  • Loss = −log σ(margin) → gradient raises the chosen log-prob and lowers the rejected one with weight σ(−margin) → backwards pairs get pushed hardest (0.057 at margin −0.30 vs 0.035 at +0.60) → the paper found dropping this weight made the model degenerate
  • Pairs are collected up front (offline) → the policy never tries and gets judged on fresh answers during training → DPO is only as good as its pairs and can over-fit them; checkable tasks suit RL with verifiable rewards instead
  • detail that matters Log-probs are summed over the answer's tokens → the reference's forward pass on each pair can be computed once, since it never changes → training costs about the same as ordinary fine-tuning on two answers per prompt

Reference only

  • Paper defaults (App. B): β = 0.1 (0.5 for TL;DR), batch 64, RMSprop, learning rate 1e-6 with 150 warmup steps
  • Reddit TL;DR (GPT-J): DPO ≈61% vs PPO 57% GPT-4 win rate against human summaries (§6.2); humans preferred DPO over PPO 58% (Table 2)
  • CNN/DailyMail out of distribution: DPO 0.36 vs PPO 0.26 win rate at temperature 0 (Table 1)
  • IMDb sentiment (GPT-2-large): DPO's reward-vs-KL frontier dominates PPO, even PPO with the true reward (§6.1, Fig. 2)
  • Bradley-Terry: the preference model behind the pairwise loss; tested up to 6B parameters

vs Learn what people prefer, then chase it: RLHF with PPO and DPO optimise the same objective. What is fundamentally different in how each one reaches it, which design choice causes that, and how does each one fail?

Check
  • RLHF fits an explicit reward model, then runs RL that samples from the policy
  • DPO rewrites the reward in terms of the policy, so a single loss on fixed pairs is enough
  • RLHF fails through reward hacking and an unstable, expensive RL loop
  • DPO fails by over-fitting the collected pairs and never exploring beyond them

Check
  • pick rule-checked RL when answers can be verified (maths, code tests)
  • it samples fresh attempts and learns from its own successes, so it can discover long reasoning
  • DPO only reshuffles probability between answers already in the pairs
  • pick DPO when quality is a matter of taste that only people can judge

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Take the worked pair after training (policy log-probs −11.0 chosen, −15.0 rejected; reference −14.0 and −12.0). If β were 0.5 instead of 0.1, what would the margin and loss be, and what does that suggest about how far the policy needs to move under a bigger β?

Reveal

Rewards become +1.50 and −1.50, so the margin is 3.00 and the loss −log σ(3.00) ≈ 0.049. The same movement now counts five times as much, so with a bigger β the loss is satisfied after a smaller drift from the reference: β acts as the leash.
