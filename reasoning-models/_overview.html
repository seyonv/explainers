How a reasoning model is made

The course map · pretraining, RLHF and DPO, chain-of-thought, voting and step graders, STaR, GRPO and DeepSeek-R1 (2022–2025)

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The papers behind reasoning models come from different years and labs (InstructGPT, chain-of-thought, self-consistency, STaR, DeepSeek-R1) and it's easy to lose which ones train the model and which only change how it's used. A model trained only to predict the next word can't reliably answer a three-step maths question. Getting from there to DeepSeek-R1 took four stages, and each one fixes a problem the last one left. This page lays out those four stages, the card that teaches each one, and the day of the study path it belongs to.

Try this first

Guess first: you have a model and a hard maths question. You can either let it think longer on this question or train it further. Which of the two changes its weights?

Show the answer

Only training does. Thinking longer (writing steps, sampling many answers and voting, appending "Wait") uses the same weights and costs you again on every question. Training costs a run up front, and every later answer benefits. R1 uses both.

Three words first

Weights

The millions or billions of numbers inside the model. Training changes them. Everything else on this page either changes them or leaves them alone.

Reward

A score for an answer: from a second model that learned what people like, or from a rule like "does the final answer equal 9?".

Inference

Using the trained model to answer, with its weights fixed. "More inference compute" means letting it write more tokens or try more times.

Each stage buys one thing and leaves one problem

Pretrain

Buys: knowledge and fluent text, learned by predicting the next token on a huge amount of text.

Leaves: it continues the page instead of answering, and step-by-step reasoning only shows up in very large models.

Day 1 · The 2017 block, four upgrades later

Make it an assistant

Buys: it follows instructions. Fine-tune on written answers, train a reward model on which answer people preferred, then use RL to chase that score. DPO gets there without the reward model.

Leaves: it learns what people like, not what is right, and pushing too hard on a learned score gets it gamed.

Day 2 · Make good samples more likely · Learn what people prefer · Skip the reward model

Think at inference

Buys: better answers with no training: write the steps, sample many and vote, let a step grader pick, or force it to keep thinking with "Wait".

Leaves: every question costs more tokens, the gains flatten, and on the hardest questions a bigger model wins.

Day 3 · Show your working · Ask many times · Grade the steps · Think longer or be bigger? · "Wait"

Learn to think with RL

Buys: a model that has learned to think long on its own, by rewarding only answers a rule can check. STaR keeps its own right answers; GRPO scores each try against its group's average.

Leaves: it needs questions with checkable answers. Writing or advice has no rule to check, and R1-Zero's thinking mixed languages and was hard to read.

Day 4 · Learn from your own right answers · Reasoning from reward alone

There are only two levers, and they stack

← scroll the sketch →

The top lane leaves the weights alone and spends more tokens per question. The bottom lane changes the weights once. They aren't rivals: a model trained to think can still be sampled many times and voted.

These are the paper's real numbers for R1-Zero on AIME 2024 maths problems. RL alone took one try from 15.6% to 71.0%. Voting over 64 tries took the same trained model to 86.7%. These figures are from the paper's first version (January 2025); its 2026 revision reports a longer run reaching 77.9% on one try. The R1 card shows both.

Principle

A reasoning model is a good next-token predictor that was rewarded for checking its own work

The later stages add little new knowledge. Mostly they change how the model uses what pretraining gave it: first to answer, then to show steps, then to keep going until a rule says the answer is right.

Buys you: one question to ask of any new method: does it change the weights, or only what happens at answer time?

The takeaway

Ask of every method on this path: does it change the weights, or spend more tokens at answer time? Pretraining, RLHF, DPO, STaR and GRPO change the weights once. Chain-of-thought, voting, step graders and "Wait" leave them alone and charge you per question.

The honest limit: everything in stage 4 needs answers a rule can check, like maths and code. For writing or advice there is no such rule, so R1 still relied on preference reward models there, and only briefly, because they get gamed.

Check yourself

1. Why does a pretrained base model answer the apples question with another question?

Show the answer

It was only ever trained to continue text. On the internet a question is often followed by another question, so continuing the page is the likely next text. Instruction fine-tuning teaches it to answer.

2. Self-consistency and GRPO both sample many answers to one question. What does each do with them?

Show the answer

Self-consistency votes and returns the most common answer; the weights never change. GRPO scores each try against the group's average and updates the weights so better tries become more likely next time.

3. R1-Zero solved 71.0% with one try and 86.7% with a vote of 64. Which lever made each jump?

Show the answer

RL (training) took one try from 15.6% to 71.0%. Voting (inference) took the same weights to 86.7%, at 64 times the tokens per question.

Understand and remember

  • Pretraining only teaches next-token prediction → the base model continues the page instead of answering → SFT on written answers and preference training (RLHF or DPO) turn it into an assistant
  • An assistant trained on what people prefer learns what they like, not what is right → a confident wrong answer can still score well → reasoning needs either more thinking at answer time or a reward that checks correctness
  • Inference-time methods (CoT, voting, step graders, "Wait") keep the weights fixed → every question costs more tokens and gains flatten → training-time methods (STaR, GRPO) pay once and every later answer benefits
  • A rule can check a maths or code answer but not an essay → rule-checked rewards can't be flattered, so large-scale RL works there → R1-Zero went from 15.6% to 71.0% on AIME 2024 with RL, and a vote of 64 took the same model to 86.7%

Check
  • RLHF: a learned reward model trained on people's preferences

  • a rule reward only exists for checkable tasks

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

R1-Zero's one-try score was 71.0% and its vote of 64 was 86.7%. If you voted over 64 tries of the model at the start of RL (15.6% one try), would you expect to reach about 86.7%? Commit before revealing.

Reveal

No. Voting only helps when the right answer is the most common one among the samples. At 15.6% one-try accuracy the right answer is usually a minority, so the vote mostly picks a common wrong answer. The card doesn't give that number; the point is that voting amplifies what training put there.
