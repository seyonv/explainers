Ask many times, and let the answers vote

Self-consistency · majority voting over sampled chains · Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models, Wang et al., Google, 2022 (ICLR 2023)

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: One chain of thought can slip at any step, and greedy decoding gives you exactly one chain, the same one every time. You get no second opinion and no sign of how sure the model is.

Where it fits: Builds on chain-of-thought prompting (one written chain) and on sampling with temperature (why repeated runs differ). Here many sampled chains vote; the process-rewards card later replaces the vote with a trained grader.

One chain of thought can slip at any step, and you only get one shot at it. Ask the same question many times with a little randomness, and take the answer most chains agree on. No extra model and no training, and on grade-school maths it added about 18 points.

Try this first

Guess first: you sample one chain with randomness turned on, instead of the model's single most likely chain. Is the one random chain more or less accurate?

Show the answer

Less. In the paper's UL2-20B test on AQuA maths questions, one sampled chain scored 19.7% against 23.6% for the most likely one. Randomness only pays off once you have several chains and let them vote: 24.9% with 5.

Three words first

Greedy decoding

Always write the single most likely next token. Same prompt, same output, every time. This is how the chain-of-thought paper ran its models.

Sampling

Pick the next token at random, weighted by the model's probabilities. The temperature sets how adventurous it is; above 0, every run can wander down a different path.

Majority vote

Read only the final answer of each chain, count them, keep the most common. The working is thrown away.

Wrong chains scatter, right chains agree

← scroll the sketch →

The problem is the paper's. Greedy decoding gives one path, and here it's wrong. Ten sampled chains take different routes: most reach $18, and the mistakes split across $26, $14 and $32. Counting the final answers picks $18. The ten chains and their answers are illustrative; the $14 and $26 mistakes are from the paper's Figure 1.

The bet: there are many ways to be right and many more ways to be wrong

The paper's reasoning is simple. A problem with one right answer can be reached by many different chains, and they all end at the same number. A slip can happen anywhere, so the wrong chains end at different numbers. Agreement is evidence, and the vote turns it into a choice.

The method in three steps:

  1. Use the same chain-of-thought prompt as before.
  2. Sample many chains instead of one greedy chain. PaLM 540B used temperature 0.7, keeping only the 40 most likely tokens at each step; for why samples differ at all, see nucleus sampling.
  3. Cut out each chain's final answer (the number after "The answer is") and take the most common.

The paper also tried weighting each vote by how likely the model thought that chain was. It barely mattered: 74.1% against 74.4% for the plain vote on GSM8K with PaLM 540B. The model rates its own chains as about equally likely, so its confidence doesn't help pick.

GSM8K solve rate, one greedy chain → vote over 40 (Wang et al. 2023, Table 2)

GPT-3 (code-davinci-002)60.1% → 78.0%

Most of the gain comes from the first handful of votes

← scroll the sketch →

The paper's numbers for one model and one dataset. Going from 1 to 5 chains adds 5.2 points; going from 20 to 40 adds 0.2. The paper's own advice is to start with 5 or 10 chains, because "the performance saturates quickly". The red line is beam search, which keeps the few most likely chains instead of sampling: its top chain gets worse as the beam widens. Even a vote over the beams scored lower than a vote over samples (24.2% vs 26.9% at 40), because the chains a beam keeps are near copies and give the vote little new evidence.

Principle

Independent attempts that agree are worth more than one careful attempt

A single chain is right or wrong, and you can't tell which. Many chains with varied routes fail in different places, so their mistakes spread out while the right answer piles up. The more the samples differ, the more the vote knows.

Buys you: a better answer from the same model, plus a free confidence signal: the paper found that how strongly the chains agree tracks how often the vote is right.

Wang et al. 2023, §2, §3.4–3.5, Figure 5

When voting isn't the right tool

  1. The answers have to be comparable. A vote needs answers you can match: a number, a letter, yes or no. The paper says the method applies "only to problems where the final answer is from a fixed answer set". Two essays never match word for word.
  2. Cost grows with N. Forty chains cost forty times the tokens of one. The paper names the extra computation as a limitation.
  3. It plateaus. Once the common answer is settled, more chains add almost nothing, and if the model is usually wrong the same way, the vote picks the popular mistake.
  4. A trained checker can beat it. A model that reads the steps and scores them picks better at large N: on 500 MATH problems with 1,860 tries each, a step-grading reward model reached 78.2% against 69.6% for the vote. See process rewards.

The vote ignores the working entirely, so a chain that got lucky counts the same as a sound one.

Check yourself

1. Why do you need temperature above 0 for self-consistency to work?

Show the answer

At temperature 0 (greedy) every run writes the same chain, so all N votes are the same vote. Randomness gives different routes, and different routes make different mistakes, which is what lets the right answer stand out.

2. Ten chains answer 18, 18, 26, 18, 14, 18, 32, 18, 26, 18. What does the vote return, and how confident should you be?

Show the answer

$18, with 6 of 10 agreeing (60%). The paper found that higher agreement goes with higher accuracy, so 60% is a fair answer but not a sure one.

3. Why didn't more beams help beam search the way more samples helped the vote?

Show the answer

Beam search keeps the most likely chains, which are mostly near copies of each other (the paper blames their low diversity). Copies agree for the wrong reason: they share the same mistakes. The vote needs chains that went different ways.

Understand and remember

  • Temperature > 0 → each chain takes a different route → slips land in different places → wrong answers scatter while right answers pile up → the most common answer is more often right
  • Greedy gives one chain → no comparison is possible → sampling N chains and voting adds evidence → GSM8K PaLM 540B 56.5% → 74.4% with 40 chains
  • Extra chains mostly confirm the leading answer → returns shrink fast → UL2-20B AQuA 19.7 / 24.9 / 25.3 / 26.7 / 26.9% at 1 / 5 / 10 / 20 / 40 chains → start with 5–10
  • The vote only reads final answers → needs answers from a fixed set and ignores the working → free text can't vote, and a lucky chain counts like a sound one → a trained step grader beats it at large N (78.2% vs 69.6%)
  • detail that matters Beam search keeps the most likely chains → near copies with low diversity → its top chain drops 23.6% → 10.2% as beams grow, and a vote over beams trails a vote over samples (24.2% vs 26.9%)
  • detail that matters The model rates its chains as about equally likely → weighting votes by probability adds nothing (74.1% vs 74.4%) → plain counting is enough

Reference only

  • Sampling settings: PaLM 540B T = 0.7, top-k 40; UL2-20B and LaMDA-137B T = 0.5, top-k 40; GPT-3 T = 0.7
  • Main results: 40 sampled chains, averaged over 10 runs, same 8 exemplars as the CoT paper
  • Other GSM8K gains: code-davinci-002 60.1% → 78.0%, LaMDA-137B 17.1% → 27.7%, UL2-20B 4.1% → 7.3%

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

A model makes the same mistake on a question 70% of the time and gets it right 30% of the time. What happens to the vote's answer as you go from 1 to 40 sampled chains?

Reveal

It gets more confidently wrong. With one chain you're right 30% of the time; with 40 chains the wrong answer almost always wins the count. Voting amplifies whatever the model does most often, so it only helps when the right answer is the most common one.
