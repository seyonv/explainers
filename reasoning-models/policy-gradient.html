Make good samples more likely

Policy gradient · REINFORCE (Williams, 1992) · as taught in OpenAI's Spinning Up, Part 3: Intro to Policy Optimization

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Supervised learning needs the right answer for every example. Once a model has to discover good outputs itself and is only told how good each try was, there is no label to copy, so you need a way to turn a score into a weight update.

Where it fits: Builds on softmax and one gradient step from the z2h cards. This is the RL engine under RLHF (next card) and under GRPO in DeepSeek-R1, whose baseline is the average reward of a group of samples.

In supervised learning, someone hands you the right answer. In reinforcement learning (RL) nobody does: the model tries something, and a grader only says how good it was. Policy gradient turns that score into a nudge: make what the model just did more likely if it scored well, and less likely if it didn't.

Try this first

Guess first: asked "What is 7 × 8?", a model answers “I'm not sure”. The grader gives that 0.3 out of 1. Should training make that answer more likely or less likely?

Show the answer

It depends on what the model usually scores. If its average is 0.549, then 0.3 is below average, so it should become less likely. The plainest version of the method would make it more likely, simply because 0.3 is positive. Fixing that is what this card's last idea, the baseline, is for.

Three words first

Policy

The model's probabilities over what to do next. For a language model that's the softmax over next tokens. In this card's toy, it's three whole answers.

Reward

A number a grader gives after the model acts. It says how good the answer was, never what the right answer would have been.

Baseline

The reward you expected anyway. Subtract it, and only better-than-usual samples get pushed up; worse-than-usual ones get pushed down.

The whole update is one line

One REINFORCE step on one sample

new scores = old scores + step size × weight × (which answer was sampled − current probabilities)

The bracket is the gradient of the sampled answer's log-probability: for the dodge it is [0, 0, 1] − [0.422, 0.155, 0.422] = [−0.422, −0.155, +0.578]. Following it is one step along the gradient, uphill this time, because we want more reward rather than less loss.

The weight is where the methods differ. Plain REINFORCE uses the reward itself: +0.3. With a baseline it's the reward minus the average: 0.3 − 0.549 = −0.249, which flips the sign.

Supervised learning

You're told the right answer, and the cross-entropy loss raises its log-probability every time.

If the reward were 1 for the right answer and you always "sampled" it, the update above would be exactly that gradient.

Policy gradient

The model picks, and you raise the log-probability of what it picked, scaled by how good it turned out.

Spinning Up warns that the "loss" printed during training means nothing: the data comes from the current policy, so watch the average reward instead.

A baseline keeps the direction and cuts the noise

← scroll the sketch →

Averaged over all three possible draws, both versions take the same expected step: [+0.190, −0.085, −0.105]. Subtracting a baseline doesn't change the direction; that's Spinning Up's "Baselines in Policy Gradients" result. What changes is the noise of a single step: 0.438 without a baseline vs 0.236 with one. Add 10 to every reward and the noise without a baseline jumps to 8.25, while with a baseline it doesn't move. The curves are our own simulation of this toy, not from a paper.

Principle

Reinforce an action by how much better it did than expected, not by its raw score

A reward's zero point is arbitrary: a grader that scores 0 to 1 and one that scores 10 to 11 agree on every ranking. Weighting by reward minus baseline makes the update depend only on the ranking. Spinning Up's intuition: "if an agent gets what it expected, it should 'feel' neutral about it."

Buys you: faster, steadier learning from the same number of samples. GRPO, which trained DeepSeek-R1, uses the average reward of a group of answers to the same prompt as its baseline (the group is the baseline).

The failure you will hit: the policy locks onto its first lucky draws

Failure · update weights
Symptom
Average reward wanders instead of climbing, and different runs settle on different answers. In the +10 toy, 22% of 2,000 runs ended over 90% sure of the wrong answer.
Cause
Every reward was positive and there was no baseline, so whatever got sampled got pushed up, and hard. Early draws snowball.
Isolate
Log the weights you multiply by. If they all have the same sign, nothing is ever pushed down, and the update depends on luck.
Fix
Subtract a baseline: the batch's average reward, a learned value estimate (the usual choice in Spinning Up), or the group average as in GRPO. A smaller step size also helps.

Only "better than expected" should mean "do more of this".

When plain policy gradient isn't the right tool

  1. It only learns from what it samples. If the right answer has almost no probability, it is almost never drawn, so it is almost never rewarded.
  2. It needs many samples. Each step is a noisy estimate. A baseline helps but doesn't remove the noise.
  3. One big step can wreck the policy. The samples came from the old policy, so they say little about a very different new one. PPO limits how far each step can move, which is the next card.
  4. It trusts the grader completely. If the reward can be gamed, the policy will find out how.

If you already have the right answers, use supervised learning: it's the same gradient without the guessing.

Check yourself

1. The model samples “54” (reward 0). What does plain REINFORCE do, and what does it do with a baseline of 0.549?

Show the answer

Plain: the weight is 0, so nothing changes. With the baseline, the weight is −0.549, so “54” is pushed down from 0.155 to 0.084, and the other two rise to 0.458 each.

2. Why doesn't subtracting a baseline bias the update?

Show the answer

The baseline doesn't depend on which answer was sampled, and the log-probability gradients average to zero over the policy's own draws. So the subtracted term is zero on average: the expected step stays [+0.190, −0.085, −0.105] either way.

3. Your training "loss" went down but the average reward didn't move. What do you trust?

Show the answer

The average reward. The policy-gradient "loss" is only a device for getting the right gradient on data from the current policy. It doesn't measure performance.

Understand and remember

  • No right answer is given → the model samples its own answer → a grader scores it → raise that answer's log-probability in proportion to the score → over many samples, good answers become more likely
  • Weight = raw reward → every answer with a positive reward is pushed up, including below-average ones → the update is noisy and depends on the reward's arbitrary zero point
  • Weight = reward − baseline (expected reward) → only better-than-expected answers are pushed up, worse ones down → same expected step ([+0.190, −0.085, −0.105] in the toy) but noise falls from 0.438 to 0.236 → faster, steadier learning
  • Rewards +10 with no baseline → huge same-sign weights → each run locks onto its early draws (22% of 2,000 toy runs ended over 90% sure of the wrong answer) → with a baseline the +10 cancels and learning is unchanged
  • detail that matters Samples must come from the current policy → after one big step the old samples no longer describe the new policy → that's why PPO limits how far each update moves
  • detail that matters With reward 1 on the right answer, always sampled, the REINFORCE gradient equals the cross-entropy gradient → supervised learning is the special case where you're handed the answer

Reference only

  • gradient of log softmax wrt the logits = one-hot(sampled) − probabilities
  • Spinning Up loss in code: -(logp * weights).mean()
  • Toy: logits [1.0, 0.0, 1.0] → probs [0.422, 0.155, 0.422]; rewards [1.0, 0.0, 0.3]; baseline 0.549
  • Reward-to-go: only rewards after an action should weight it (multi-step episodes)
  • REINFORCE: Williams (1992)

  1. concept Explain the difference between what a supervised label tells the model and what a reward tells it.
    Check
    • a label names the right answer, whatever the model did
    • a reward only scores the answer the model actually produced
    • so RL must sample, score and reweight instead of copying
  2. mechanism Walk through one REINFORCE step with a baseline for the toy when the model samples “56” (reward 1.0).
    Check
    • baseline = 0.549, weight = 1.0 − 0.549 = 0.451
    • gradient = [1, 0, 0] − [0.422, 0.155, 0.422]
    • logits move by 0.451 × that vector (step size 1)
    • probs become about [0.526, 0.139, 0.335]
  3. tradeoff You can use the batch's average reward or a learned value model as a baseline. What does each cost, and when would you pick each?
    Check
    • batch or group average: free, no extra model, but needs several samples per prompt (GRPO's choice)
    • value model: works with one sample per prompt, but is another network to train and can be wrong
    • either keeps the update unbiased as long as it doesn't depend on the sampled answer

  • it saves training a separate value model
