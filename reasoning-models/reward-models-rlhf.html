Learn what people prefer, then chase it

RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback · Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback (InstructGPT), Ouyang et al., OpenAI, 2022

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A pretrained model predicts internet text instead of helping, and "helpful" can't be checked by a rule. Without a learned stand-in for people's judgement there is no reward to run RL against.

Where it fits: Builds on the policy-gradient card: step 3 here is a policy gradient (PPO) whose reward comes from a model trained on human comparisons. The DPO card later reaches the same goal without the reward model.

A model trained on internet text learns to predict the next word, not to help you. Nobody can write a rule for "helpful", but a person can easily say which of two answers is better. RLHF turns those comparisons into a scorer, then uses reinforcement learning to make the model chase that score, on a leash.

Try this first

Guess first: labellers compared answers from a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT with answers from the 175B GPT-3, a model over 100 times bigger with the same design. Whose answers did they prefer?

Show the answer

The small one's. The two models differ only in that InstructGPT was fine-tuned on human data (paper, §1). On following instructions, the right training signal beat 100× the parameters.

Three words first

Reward model

A second model that reads a prompt and an answer and outputs one number: how much a person would like it. It stands in for the people during training.

Comparison

One label saying "this answer is better than that one". Easier and more consistent for people to give than a score out of 10.

KL penalty

A cost for drifting away from the starting model's habits. KL divergence measures how different two sets of probabilities are. It's the leash.

One prompt goes through all three steps

The violet step copies people's answers. The blue step turns a ranking of four answers into six comparisons and trains a scorer on them. The green step lets the model chase that scorer, minus a penalty for drifting; the answer that flatters the scorer by drifting far loses once the penalty is paid. The prompt, answers, scores and drifts are ours and illustrative; the steps and the 0.02 penalty weight are the paper's (§3.1, Appendix C.4).

The scorer learns from pairs, not from grades

← scroll the sketch →

For each pair the reward model sees the preferred answer and the other one. The loss is −log σ(r_chosen − r_rejected): in plain words, σ squashes the gap between the two scores into "the chance the scorer agrees with the person", and the loss is small when that chance is high. A gap of 0 costs 0.693, +2 costs 0.127, and −2 costs 2.127. Only the gap matters, so the paper shifts all scores so that people's own demonstrations average 0 before RL (§3.5).

How the paper did it

Labellers ranked between 4 and 9 answers per prompt, giving up to 36 pairs from one ranking. All the pairs from one prompt go into training together: when the paper shuffled them as separate examples, the reward model overfit after a single pass (§3.5). The reward model was a 6B model, trained for one epoch on 33k prompts' worth of rankings, up to 2,304 comparisons per batch (§3.2, Appendix C.2).

PPO: the policy gradient, in small steps, on a leash

Step 3 is the policy gradient from the last card: sample an answer, score it, make high-scoring answers more likely. PPO (proximal policy optimization) adds two safety catches, and InstructGPT adds a third:

  1. Small steps. PPO compares each sampled answer's new probability with its old one and stops giving extra credit once that ratio leaves the band 0.8 to 1.2 (clip ratio 0.2, Appendix C.4).
  2. A baseline. A value model, started from the reward model, predicts the score to expect, so only better-than-expected answers are pushed up.
  3. The leash. Each token pays β × log(new probability ÷ SFT probability), with β = 0.02. The paper adds it "to mitigate over-optimization of the reward model" (§3.5). With β at 0 or 2, people rated the results poorly; the best was around 0.01–0.02 (Appendix E.7).

The final InstructGPT ("PPO-ptx") also mixes in ordinary next-word training on pretraining text. That repairs most of the drop on benchmarks like SQuAD and DROP, which the paper calls an "alignment tax" (§1).

Principle

When you can't write down what "good" means, learn it from comparisons, then optimise it cautiously

People are unreliable at absolute scores but decent at "A beats B". A reward model turns thousands of those judgements into a score you can take gradients against. Because it's only an approximation, the leash keeps the policy where the approximation still holds.

Buys you: a training signal for fuzzy goals like "helpful" and "honest" that no rule can check.

Ouyang et al. 2022, §3.1 and §3.5

The failure you will hit: the model learns the scorer, not the people

Failure · reward over-optimisation
Symptom
The reward model's score keeps climbing during RL, but fresh human ratings stop improving or get worse.
Cause
The scorer is an imperfect copy of people's taste: it agreed with its own labellers 72.4% of the time, about as often as the labellers agreed with each other. The policy finds the answers where the scorer is wrong and too generous. That's reward hacking.
Isolate
Plot the reward-model score against human ratings on held-out prompts, and track how far the policy has drifted from the SFT model. Rising score plus rising drift plus flat ratings is the signature.
Fix
Keep the KL leash tuned, stop earlier, and retrain the reward model on answers from the current policy. The paper notes that steps 2 and 3 can be repeated for exactly this (§3.1).

Optimise a proxy hard enough and you get the proxy.

When RLHF isn't the right tool

  1. "Human preferences" means these people. The paper aligned to about 40 labellers, the researchers' instructions and API customers' prompts, not to "human values" (§5.2). Labellers agreed with each other only about 73% of the time, and most comparisons were labelled by one person (§5.2, §5.3).
  2. It's heavy. RL needs the policy, the frozen SFT model for the leash, the reward model and the value model. DPO gets the same preference goal from a plain classification loss, with no reward model and no RL.
  3. If an answer can be checked, check it. For maths or code a rule-based reward can't be flattered. That's how DeepSeek-R1 trained its reasoning (reasoning from reward alone).
  4. It doesn't fix everything. InstructGPT still made things up, gave long hedging answers to simple questions, and followed harmful instructions (§1, §5.3).

RLHF is for goals people can judge but no one can write down. For where it sits among other post-training methods, see post-training.

Check yourself

1. A labeller ranks 5 answers. How many comparisons does the reward model train on, and why does the paper keep them in one batch element?

Show the answer

10 (5 choose 2). Every answer appears in 4 of the pairs, so shuffled separately they repeat the same data and the model overfit after one pass. Keeping them together also needs just one forward pass per answer.

2. The scorer gives the preferred answer 0.4 and the other one 0.4. What's the loss for that pair, and what does training do about it?

Show the answer

0.693 (that's −log 0.5: a coin flip). The gradient pushes the preferred answer's score up and the other's down until the gap is positive.

3. Why not just set the KL weight to zero and let the policy maximise the reward model's score?

Show the answer

Because the reward model is only right about 72% of the time on its own labellers. Unleashed, the policy drifts to answers the scorer overrates. The paper found β = 0 rated poorly by people, and the best was around 0.01–0.02.

Understand and remember

  • People can't write a rule for "helpful" but can say which of two answers is better → rank K answers → C(K,2) pairs → train a reward model with −log σ(r_chosen − r_rejected) → a number you can do RL against
  • Loss depends only on the score gap → biggest when the scorer disagrees with the person (D > C at −0.2 costs 0.798) → training fixes the pairs it gets wrong first; scores have no fixed zero, so the paper re-centres them before RL
  • Reward model is an imperfect copy (72.4% agreement with its labellers) → the policy finds answers the scorer overrates → reward over-optimisation → a per-token KL penalty (β = 0.02) to the SFT model keeps the policy where the scorer still holds
  • Same architecture + human-feedback fine-tuning → 1.3B InstructGPT preferred over 175B GPT-3 → the training signal mattered more than 100× the parameters for following instructions
  • detail that matters All C(K,2) pairs from one prompt go in one batch element → one forward pass per answer and no repeated data within an epoch → no overfitting after one pass
  • detail that matters PPO-ptx mixes pretraining next-word updates into RL → repairs most of the benchmark drop (the "alignment tax") that a stronger KL alone couldn't fix

Reference only

  • Datasets: SFT ~13k, RM 33k, PPO 31k training prompts (§3.2, Table 6)
  • About 40 labellers; inter-labeller agreement 72.6 ± 1.5% (§3.4)
  • 6B reward model, one epoch, up to 2,304 comparisons per batch (Appendix C.2)
  • PPO: β = 0.02, clip ratio 0.2, 256k episodes, value model initialised from the RM (Appendix C.4)
  • 175B InstructGPT preferred to 175B GPT-3 85 ± 3%, to few-shot GPT-3 71 ± 4%; hallucination 21% vs 41% (§1)

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose labellers agreed with each other only 50% of the time on some kind of prompt. What accuracy could a reward model reach on those prompts, and what would PPO end up chasing there?

Reveal

About 50%: a coin flip, because the labels themselves are random. The reward model's scores there are mostly noise and quirks, so PPO would chase whatever quirks the scorer happens to have. That's reward hacking with no real signal underneath, and only the KL leash limits the damage.
