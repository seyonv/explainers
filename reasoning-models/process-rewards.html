Grade the steps, not just the answer

Process vs outcome reward models · Let's Verify Step by Step, Lightman et al., OpenAI, 2023

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Sampling many answers only helps if something can pick the good one. A picker that checks only the final answer can't tell a sound solution from one that got lucky, and if you train on its labels you reward the lucky reasoning too.

Where it fits: Builds on self-consistency (many samples, majority vote) and on reward models from the RLHF card. Here the reward model is trained to grade every step instead of the whole answer.

A model can write a thousand solutions to one maths problem, and something has to pick the good one. If that picker only checks the final answer, it can't tell a sound solution from a lucky one. A picker that checks every step can, and in this paper it picked right more often.

Try this first

Guess first: two solutions both answer 0%. One of them used a false rule along the way. Can a scorer that sees only the final answer tell them apart?

Show the answer

No. Both look identical to it. Worse, when it was trained, the lucky one was labelled "correct", so it learned to like it.

Three words first

Reward model

A second model whose only job is to give an answer a score. Higher means "more likely to be right".

Best-of-N

Ask the model the same question N times, score every answer, keep the top one. Here N went up to 1,860.

Majority vote

No scorer at all: take the final answer that shows up most often across the N tries. A strong, cheap baseline.

A lucky answer looks exactly like a good one, unless you read the steps

The blue box is what an outcome reward model (ORM) sees: the final answer only. The green ovals are a process reward model (PRM) scoring every step, and its low score lands exactly on the false rule. Multiplying the step scores gives the solution's score. The step scores are illustrative, and the problem is ours, not the paper's.

The two scorers are trained on different evidence

Outcome reward model (ORM)

Trained on "was the final answer right?". Those labels are free: a script compares the answer to the key.

The catch, which the paper names: a right answer reached by wrong reasoning gets labelled correct.

Process reward model (PRM)

Trained on step labels from people (positive, negative or neutral) up to the first wrong step.

That's the PRM800K dataset: 800,000 step labels on 75,000 solutions to 12,000 problems. Expensive, and released for others to use.

With 1,860 tries to choose from, step-checking picks right most often

These are the paper's real numbers. A fine-tuned GPT-4 wrote 1,860 solutions per problem from MATH (a set of competition maths problems), and each method picked one. The gap grows as N grows. One honest caveat from the paper: at this scale the ORM and PRM were trained on different data that can't be compared directly. Its smaller, controlled experiments point the same way.

Principle

Feedback that says where the mistake is beats feedback that only says there was one

For a correct solution both scorers learn the same thing. For a wrong one, the outcome label says "something failed". The step label adds which step. That extra location is what lets the PRM reject convincing wrong solutions when N is large.

Buys you: a picker that stays reliable as you sample more, instead of getting fooled more.

Lightman et al. 2023, §2.6 and Appendix G

The failure you will hit: the scorer falls for lucky answers

Failure · reward model
Symptom
Best-of-N with an ORM gets slightly worse on easy problems as you sample more. The paper saw exactly this in its easiest fifth of problems.
Cause
The ORM's training labels came from checking only the answer, so lucky solutions were labelled correct. More samples means more lucky solutions for it to fall for.
Isolate
Read the top-scored wrong-answer solutions by hand. If the reasoning is broken in a way the score didn't notice, it's the scorer, not the generator.
Fix
Score every step (a PRM), or label the solutions the current scorer finds most convincing but gets wrong. That trick made the paper's labelling 2.6× more data-efficient.

A scorer only knows what its labels told it.

When a PRM isn't worth it

This paper used its reward models only to pick among answers. It did not train the generator with reinforcement learning (RL). The DeepSeek-R1 paper lists a PRM for large-scale RL under its unsuccessful attempts, for three reasons:

  1. Outside tidy maths, it's hard to say what one "step" even is.
  2. Judging whether a middle step is right is hard. Labels from a model are unreliable, and labels from people don't scale.
  3. A learned scorer can be gamed (reward hacking), and keeping it up to date makes the RL pipeline heavier.

R1's authors still credit PRMs with what this paper showed they're good at: reranking the top answers and guiding search. For RL, R1 used outcome rewards checked by rules. That's the next card in the path.

Check yourself

1. Why is the ORM's training data free while the PRM's is expensive?

Show the answer

A script can check a final answer against the key. Nothing can automatically check a middle step, so people had to label 800,000 of them.

2. Solution B's step scores are 0.99, 0.08 and 0.95. Why does one low step sink the whole solution?

Show the answer

The solution's score is the product of its step scores (the chance that every step is right): 0.99 × 0.08 × 0.95 = 0.075. One near-zero step pulls the product near zero.

3. If PRMs beat ORMs, why did DeepSeek-R1 use outcome rewards for training?

Show the answer

Rule-checked outcome rewards are cheap and can't be talked into a high score. A PRM needs costly step labels, is fuzzy outside maths, and can be gamed during RL.

Understand and remember

  • ORM labels come from checking only the final answer → a right answer reached by a wrong step is labelled correct → the ORM learns to like that reasoning → with more samples there are more convincing wrong solutions to fool it
  • PRM predicts the chance each step is correct → solution score = product of step scores → one bad step (0.08) drags the whole score down (0.99 × 0.08 × 0.95 ≈ 0.075) → the PRM blames the exact step
  • On a correct solution both labels say the same thing; on a wrong one the step label also says where → more information per example → best-of-1,860 on 500 MATH problems: PRM 78.2% vs ORM 72.4% vs majority vote 69.6%
  • Step labels need people (no script can check a middle step) → expensive and hard to define outside tidy maths → R1 used rule-checked final answers for RL and credited PRMs only for reranking and search
  • detail that matters Supervision stops at the first wrong step → labelling cost stays close to grading the whole solution, and the comparison with outcome labels stays fair
  • detail that matters Active learning: show labellers convincing wrong-answer solutions (high PRM score, wrong final answer) → every one contains a mistake the PRM missed → about 2.6× more useful per label

Reference only

  • PRM800K: 800,000 step labels on 75,000 solutions to 12,000 problems; labels are positive, negative or neutral
  • Scoring variants (Table 4): product 78.2% vs minimum 77.6%; neutral counted as negative 77.4% / 77.8%
  • The generator is a fine-tuned GPT-4; the paper does not train it with RL
  • Test set: 500 held-out MATH problems (the other 4,500 test problems went into PRM800K training)

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose the PRM scored a 20-step solution and a 4-step solution, each with every step at 0.97. Which gets the higher solution score, and what problem does that reveal about the product rule?

Reveal

The 4-step one: 0.97⁴ ≈ 0.885 vs 0.97²⁰ ≈ 0.544. The product rule is slightly biased against long solutions, which the paper notes; taking the minimum step score instead avoids it and scored 77.6% vs 78.2%.
