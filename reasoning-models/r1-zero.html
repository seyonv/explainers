Reasoning from reward alone

DeepSeek-R1-Zero and DeepSeek-R1 · GRPO with rule-based rewards · DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning, DeepSeek-AI, 2025 (revised 2026)

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Teaching reasoning by copying human-written solutions is slow, expensive, and caps the model at the reasoning it was shown. Without another source of signal, a base model never learns to think at length.

Where it fits: The capstone of the course. It reuses policy gradients (make good samples more likely), the group baseline from GRPO, and the outcome-vs-process reward debate from the PRM card, and shows them producing a full reasoning model.

Teaching a model to reason usually means showing it thousands of worked solutions written by people. That's slow, and the model can only copy the reasoning it's shown. DeepSeek gave a base model maths and coding problems, checked only the final answers with simple rules, and let reinforcement learning do the rest. Long, self-checking reasoning appeared without anyone writing an example of it, because careful answers were right more often.

Try this first

Guess first: you reward a model only for a right final answer and for putting its working inside <think> tags. Nothing rewards length. Over 10,000 training steps, what happens to how long its answers are?

Show the answer

They keep growing, from hundreds to thousands of tokens, and the model starts writing things like "Wait" and re-checking its work. Length is never rewarded directly. It grows because checking more often gets the answer right more often.

Three words first

Base model

A model that has only been pretrained to continue text, with no fine-tuning on examples and no chat training. Here that's DeepSeek-V3-Base: 671B parameters, 37B used per token.

Reinforcement learning

The model tries, a reward scores each try, and training makes high-scoring tries more likely. The policy-gradient card builds it from zero.

Rule-based reward

A score computed by a script, not by a neural network. It compares the answer with the key, or runs the code against tests. It can't be flattered.

Sample a group, check each answer by rule, push toward the better-than-average ones

← scroll the sketch →

The policy (the model being trained) writes several answers to the same problem. A rule checker scores each one: a point for the right answer, a point for thinking inside the tags. Each answer is then compared with its own group. Better than the group average gives a positive advantage, so its tokens become more likely. Worse gives a negative advantage, so they become less likely. Sample 3 got a reward of 1 and still gets pushed down, because the group averaged 1.25. This is GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization). The group's own average replaces the separate "critic" model that the older method, PPO, has to train. The group-is-the-baseline card walks through the whole mechanism. The numbers here are illustrative, and the problem is ours, not the paper's. The “KL leash” in the sketch is a small penalty for drifting far from the starting model, so one lucky batch can't pull it too far at once.

The rewards are two rules, and that's deliberate

Accuracy reward

Is the answer right? For maths, the model must put its final answer in a fixed spot, such as a box, so a rule can compare it with the key. For coding problems, a compiler runs the code against predefined tests.

Format reward

Is the working inside <think> and </think>? The two rewards are added with equal weight. That's the whole signal.

Why no neural reward model? A neural reward model is a second network trained to score answers. The paper gives two reasons for not using one, for outcomes or for steps. In large-scale RL the policy learns to game a learned scorer (reward hacking), and retraining that scorer costs compute and complicates the pipeline. A rule can't be gamed: the answer is 9 or it isn't.

The prompt template. Every training question was wrapped in one short template. In effect it says: the assistant first thinks, then answers, with the reasoning inside <think> tags and the answer inside <answer> tags. Then comes "User:", the problem, and "Assistant:". The authors kept it to structure on purpose. It never says "reflect" or "check your work", so they could watch what the model learned by itself.

Accuracy climbed, and the answers got longer on their own

The benchmark is AIME 2024, a hard high-school maths competition with whole-number answers. Pass@1 is the average accuracy of single tries, measured over 16 samples per question. Majority vote samples many answers and takes the most common one (see self-consistency). The paper was revised in 2026 with a longer training run, so the end numbers differ between versions:

For scale, OpenAI's o1-0912 scored 74.4% pass@1 in the same table. The 2026 run trained for 10,400 steps and raised the length cap from 32,768 to 65,536 tokens at step 8.2k. Accuracy and response length both jumped at that point.

Length. The paper's length figure (Figure 3 in the first version, Figure 1(b) in the revision) shows the average response length on training problems rising steadily through RL, from hundreds to thousands of reasoning tokens. Nothing outside the model was changed to cause it. The model learned to spend more tokens on a problem because spending them got answers right.

Reflection. The revision counted reflective words such as "wait", "mistake", "verify" and "check". Over training they rose 5–7×. "Wait" in particular was almost absent early on, appeared now and then between steps 4,000 and 7,000, and spiked after step 8,000.

The "aha moment"

Partway through training, a checkpoint is solving an equation with nested square roots. It squares both sides, grinds through some algebra, then breaks off with Wait, wait. Wait. It labels this an aha moment and starts again, re-checking each step. The authors say it was an aha moment for them too: nobody told the model to stop and reconsider. The reward only ever checked the final answer.

The failure you will hit: right answers nobody can read

Failure · R1-Zero
Symptom
The reasoning reaches the right answer, but it's hard to read and switches between English and Chinese mid-thought. General skills are weak too: in the revision's Table 3, R1-Zero scores 24.7 on AlpacaEval 2.0 (chat quality) and 46.6 on IF-Eval (following instructions).
Cause
The reward checks only the answer and the tags. Nothing pays for readability, and the base model was pretrained mostly on Chinese and English, so it mixes whichever works.
Isolate
Measure the share of words in the chain of thought that are in the target language. That's the number DeepSeek turned into a reward.
Fix
Start from a few thousand readable examples (a "cold start"), and add a language-consistency reward during RL. The paper's ablation found maths stayed level and coding dipped slightly, a price they accepted for readability.

RL optimises exactly what you score, and nothing else.

R1 adds a little supervision back, in four stages

← scroll the sketch →

1 · Cold start. Fine-tune V3-Base on thousands of long, readable chains of thought. This is supervised fine-tuning (SFT), learning by copying examples. Reasoning dips (AIME 59.0) because the set is small, but chat quality is double R1-Zero's.

2 · Reasoning RL. The same rule rewards as R1-Zero, plus the language-consistency reward. AIME recovers to 74.0.

3 · Rejection sampling + SFT. Sample many answers from the stage-2 model and keep only the correct ones (the STaR idea at scale). Filter out mixed-language and messy chains. That gives about 600k reasoning samples, plus about 200k non-reasoning ones (writing, factual questions, translation). V3-Base is then fine-tuned from scratch on all ~800k, for two epochs (2–3 in the 2026 revision).

4 · RL for all scenarios. Rules for maths and code. For everything else, reward models for helpfulness and harmlessness. In the revision these preference rewards were switched on only for the last 400 of 1,700 steps, because training longer on them led to reward hacking.

Scores are AIME 2024 pass@1 and AlpacaEval 2.0 from the 2026 revision's Table 3. The final R1 scores 79.8, against 79.2 for OpenAI's o1-1217 (first version, Table 4).

What it cost

The revision prices the RL work at $2 per H800 GPU-hour. R1-Zero took about 101K GPU-hours ($202K), building the SFT data took 5K ($10K), and R1 took 41K ($82K). That's $294K in total. It does not include pretraining V3-Base, which is where most of the compute went. The post-training card puts these phases side by side.

For small models, copying R1's answers beats running RL on them

← scroll the sketch →

Distillation here means plain SFT: fine-tune a small open model (Qwen2.5 or Llama, 1.5B to 70B) on R1's ~800k samples, with no RL. To test whether RL alone could do as well, DeepSeek ran large-scale RL directly on Qwen2.5-32B-Base for over 10K steps. That reached 47.0 on AIME, about the level of QwQ-32B-Preview. The distilled 32B reached 72.6. Even the distilled 1.5B scored 28.9, above GPT-4o-0513's 9.3.

Rule-rewarded RL draws out what a strong base model can already almost do. A small model has less to draw out, and copying a strong teacher is cheaper. The paper adds that pushing the frontier still needs bigger base models and bigger RL.

Principle

If you can check the answer, you don't have to teach the method

R1-Zero got no worked solutions. It got hard problems, a checker that can't be fooled, and enough compute to try many times. GRPO turned "this try beat its group" into more of whatever that try did, and long, self-checking reasoning was what did best. The paper's conclusion names the same three ingredients: hard questions, a reliable verifier, and compute.

Buys you: reasoning skill without people writing reasoning traces, on any task a program can grade.

DeepSeek-R1, §2 and §6 (2026)

What didn't work: grading steps, and searching like AlphaGo

The paper reports two approaches it tried early and dropped. It adds that this doesn't prove they can't work.

  1. Process reward model (PRM), a learned scorer for every step. It's hard to define a "step" in general reasoning. Judging a middle step is hard: labels from a model are unreliable, and labels from people don't scale. A learned scorer also invites reward hacking. It's useful for reranking answers, but not worth its cost inside large-scale RL. The process-rewards card covers PRMs.
  2. Monte Carlo tree search (MCTS), which splits answers into steps and searches over them, guided by a value model. Text has far more possible next moves than chess. Capping the branches got the search stuck, and the fine-grained value model that should steer it was too hard to train, so it never improved itself the way AlphaGo did.

DeepSeek-R1, §4.2 (first version) / Supplementary G.2 (2026)

When it isn't the right tool

  1. No checker, no method. Writing, advice and open questions have no rule that says "correct". For those, R1 fell back on SFT data reused from DeepSeek-V3 and learned reward models, and ran only a few hundred RL steps because the reward models got hacked.
  2. It needs a strong base model. The same RL on a 32B base reached only 47.0 on AIME. The authors also note that V3-Base's web data was rich in maths and code, and included pages with answers written by OpenAI models, so the "emerging" reasoning had a lot to build on.
  3. Thinking isn't free. R1 still overthinks easy questions. Every extra reasoning token costs time and money (see thinking tokens). The budget-forcing card shows a cheaper way to make a model think longer.
  4. "Aha" is a behaviour, not proof of insight. The evidence is word counts and examples. It shows the reward selected for re-checking, not that the model understands why re-checking helps.

Check yourself

1. In the sketch, sample 3 earned a reward of 1, yet the update makes it less likely. Why?

Show the answer

GRPO compares each reward with its own group. The group averaged 1.25, so a reward of 1 is below average: (1 − 1.25) / 0.83 = −0.30. It's pushed down a little, and sample 4 (reward 0) much more.

2. A question is so easy that all 16 samples get it right. What does GRPO learn from it?

Show the answer

Nothing. Every reward equals the mean, so every advantage is zero. The same is true when all 16 fail. Only questions the model sometimes gets right produce a learning signal.

3. R1 uses a learned reward model for helpfulness. Why not for maths too?

Show the answer

Maths has a checkable answer, so a rule gives an exact reward that can't be gamed. Helpfulness has no rule, so a learned scorer is the only option. DeepSeek saw it get hacked, so it limited it to the last 400 RL steps.

Understand and remember

  • Sample a group of answers per question → score each with rules → advantage = (reward − group mean) / group std → raise the probability of above-average answers, lower below-average ones → no separate critic model is needed
  • Reward = rule-checked answer + format → rules can't be flattered → no reward hacking on maths and code → RL can run for thousands of steps → AIME 2024 pass@1 15.6% → 71.0% (v1) / 77.9% (2026 revision)
  • Only the final answer is paid → longer, self-checking chains are right more often → those chains get reinforced → response length and words like "wait" rise during training without being rewarded
  • Nothing pays for readability → R1-Zero mixes languages and is hard to read → R1 adds a small cold-start SFT set, a language-consistency reward, rejection-sampled SFT (~600k reasoning + ~200k other) and a final RL stage with reward models for helpfulness
  • detail that matters A question where all samples get the same reward gives every sample advantage 0 → it teaches nothing → useful training questions are the ones the model sometimes solves
  • detail that matters Learned preference rewards were switched on only for the last 400 of 1,700 RL steps → longer training on them led to reward hacking → a learned scorer is a resource you spend carefully

  • Majority vote on AIME: 86.7% (cons@64 in v1, cons@16 in the revision)

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Grade the steps, not just the answer: The PRM paper showed step grading beats outcome grading. Why did R1 train with outcome rules anyway?

Check
  • the PRM paper used scorers to pick answers, not to train with RL
  • a learned step scorer is costly to label and can be hacked during RL
  • a rule-checked outcome is exact and free, so RL can run for thousands of steps

vs Learn from your own right answers: STaR and R1's rejection-sampling stage both keep the model's own correct answers. What is different about how R1-Zero itself learns from right and wrong answers?

Check
  • STaR and stage 3 fine-tune only on correct samples (wrong ones are thrown away)
  • GRPO also pushes wrong samples down, with a negative advantage
  • GRPO weighs each sample against its group, so a right answer in a group of right answers teaches nothing

vs Say “Wait”, and the model thinks again: R1-Zero's "wait" emerged from RL; budget forcing inserts "Wait" at inference. When would you choose each?

Check
  • RL changes the weights, so the habit is built in, but it needs big compute and a checkable reward
  • budget forcing needs no RL and controls thinking length directly at inference
  • pick RL to build the skill; pick forcing to stretch an existing reasoning model cheaply

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Suppose the format reward were dropped and only the accuracy reward kept. For a group where all four answers are correct but two skip the <think> tags, what advantages does GRPO give them?

Reveal

All four rewards are equal, so every advantage is zero and nothing is learned from that question. With the format reward, the two tagged answers would score 2 against 1 for the others, get advantages of +1 and −1, and the tags would be reinforced.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

A group of 6 samples gets rule rewards 2, 2, 2, 1, 0, 0. Compute the mean, the population std and each advantage by hand, and say which samples get pushed up and which down.

Done when: Mean ≈ 1.17, std ≈ 0.90, advantages ≈ +0.93 (×3), −0.19, −1.30 (×2): the three full-score samples go up, the rest go down.

Hint

Population std: square root of the average squared distance from the mean.
