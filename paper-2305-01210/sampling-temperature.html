Sampling temperature and greedy decoding (T, T*k)

The decoding sweep behind every number in table 3: 200 random samples per task at each T in {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8}, plus one zero-temperature greedy sample that gives pass@1★. T*k is the temperature at which a model's best pass@k was achieved.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: One fixed decoding setting would favour some models and some values of k over others. Measure pass@100 at a low temperature and every sample looks the same; measure pass@1 at a high one and the safest guess gets diluted.

Where it fits: pass@k takes n samples as given. This card is about how those samples are drawn: the four-temperature sweep behind table 3, and the greedy sample behind pass@1★.

Temperature rescales the model's next-token scores just before sampling: low T sharpens the distribution toward the single most likely token, high T flattens it so unlikely tokens get a real chance. The model's weights never change — only how boldly you draw from it. Greedy decoding is the T → 0 limit: always take the top token, so the output is deterministic and you only ever get one program.

p(token) ∝ exp(logit / T)   T → 0 recovers greedy decoding; larger T flattens the distribution

Mostly no: the paper reports optimal temperatures stay fairly consistent before and after HumanEval+, with slight exceptions — CodeGen-2B at k = 10 is the example it names. It calls this a motivation for future work on how temperature interacts with the robustness of the tests, especially edge cases.

How it differs from related ideas

×Greedy decoding (T → 0)

Always take the highest-scoring token. Used here for pass@1★, one deterministic sample per task.

Gives no diversity, so pass@10 and pass@100 cannot be estimated from it at all.

×Top-k sampling

Keep only the k most likely tokens and sample among them.

Removes options without reshaping the ones left; a fixed k is too tight where the model is uncertain and too loose where it is confident. Not used or discussed in this paper.

×Top-p (nucleus) sampling

Keep the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities sum to p.

Also truncates rather than reshapes. The paper never mentions a top-p setting, so what its 26 models used alongside T is not stated.

✓Temperature, swept

Rescale every probability, remove nothing, and report the best result over four settings per k.

Makes the comparison fair: no model is penalised for being measured at a setting that happens to suit a rival.

Is a higher temperature better, or more creative?

Neither — it is only more random, and it pays off solely because of how pass@k is scored. With one attempt (k = 1) you want the safest guess, so low T wins for 21 of 23 configurations. With a hundred attempts you only need one to land, so variety pays and 0.8 wins 15 times. If you cannot select among your attempts, the pass@100 setting is not the one you want.

The sweep is also expensive: 200 samples × 4 temperatures × 164 tasks per model. That cost is why the two commercial models are treated differently — GPT-4 is greedy decoding only, ChatGPT adds just temperature 0.8 — so neither has a T*k entry in table 3.

One detail to hold loosely: the paper reports the best pass@k over the four temperatures rather than a fixed one, which mildly favours models with more configurations measured.

Understand and remember

  • Divide logits by T before softmax → small T widens gaps (sharpens), large T narrows them (flattens) → weights unchanged, only how boldly you sample
  • pass@1 needs the single most likely program to be right → low T wins (21 of 23 configurations at 0.2) → pass@100 needs any one of 100 to be right → diversity pays → 0.8 wins 15 of 23
  • Greedy (T → 0) is deterministic → one program per task → pass@1★ is exact and reproducible → but pass@10 and pass@100 can't be estimated from it
  • Best-of-four temperatures per k → no model penalised for a setting that suits a rival → but models with more measured settings get a small advantage, and commercial models without the sweep aren't directly comparable
  • detail that matters Stricter tests change which samples count as correct, not the sampling → T*k stays mostly the same on HumanEval+ → with exceptions (CodeGen-2B at k = 10: 0.8 → 0.2)

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Take logits (2, 1, 0) at T = 1 (plain softmax). Commit first: is the top token's probability closer to 0.99, 0.73 or 0.67? Then: as T → ∞, what does the distribution become?

Reveal

About 0.665 (0.665, 0.245, 0.090), a bit flatter than T = 0.8's 0.731. As T grows without bound, logits / T all go to 0 and the distribution becomes uniform over the vocabulary: every token, including nonsense, equally likely. That's why sweeps stop well below 1 for code, where one wrong token breaks the program.
