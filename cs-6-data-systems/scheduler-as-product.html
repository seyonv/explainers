The scheduler as a product

Also called scheduler UX, explainable scheduling, or "why is my job pending?". The source guide's GPU-cluster case study argues that users judge a scheduler by its queue, its reasons and its metrics, not by its placement algorithm; this card covers its Trust, Metrics and User experience sections.

In one breath

A researcher never sees the bin-packing. They see a job that is pending, a time estimate or none, and a cluster that is busy or idle. So the scheduler has a user interface: a fair queue, a pending reason with numbers in it, and metrics that count useful work instead of GPUs merely handed out.

Worked example 1: 64 pods requested, 46 GPUs free

46 free GPUs, idle while the job waits18 still busy elsewhere
shortfall = 64 − 46 = 18 GPUs free share = 46 / 64 = 71.9 % of the request, yet 0 pods start

A distributed training job needs every GPU at once (gang scheduling), so all 64 pods stay pending until 64 are free together. The case is the source's.

✗ Bare statusjob-7 PENDING ✓ Explainedjob-7 PENDING (gang): needs 64 GPUs, 46 free, waiting for 18 more · est. start 14:40

Worked example 2: where allocated GPU time goes

bins sum: 28 + 10 + 9 + 8 + 7 + 7 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 7 = 100 % idle: exactly 0 % → 28 % under 10 % → 28 + 10 = 38 % mean ≈ Σ midpoint × share = (0·28 + 5·10 + 15·9 + 25·8 + 35·7 + 45·7 + 55·6 + 65·6 + 75·6 + 85·6 + 95·7) / 100 = 3,290 / 100 = 32.9 %

Bar heights are the source's figure (BPDP-2); it doesn't say which cluster or period they come from. The mean is computed, taking each bin at its midpoint, so it is approximate. Red = allocated but doing nothing.

The 0% bar is drawn apart on purpose: a GPU at 0% is held by a job but doing no work, for example while it waits on data, a debugger, or the rest of its gang (examples ours). That is capacity waste, and a cluster can be almost fully allocated while it happens. The source states finance's target as > 95% hardware utilization; if we read that as allocation, hit it, and this chart still holds, then 0.95 × (1 − 0.28) = 68.4% of GPU time does any work at all (our combination of two separate numbers, not a figure from the source).

In Python: the reason string, the stats, the fair queue

def why_pending(need, free):
    """Gang job: all GPUs at once or none. Say why."""
    if free >= need:
        return "RUNNING"
    return (f"PENDING (gang): needs {need} GPUs, {free} free, "
            f"waiting for {need - free} more")

def util_stats(bins, share):   # mean at bin midpoints
    mean = sum((lo + hi) / 2 * s for (lo, hi), s in zip(bins, share))
    return mean / sum(share), share[0]

def fair(jobs):   # round-robin across users, one job each
    per = {}
    for j in jobs:
        per.setdefault(j[0], deque()).append(j)
    out, qs = [], deque(per.values())
    while qs:
        q = qs.popleft()
        out.append(q.popleft())
        if q:
            qs.append(q)
    return out

# mean utilization (bin midpoints): 32.9%

# fair: B1 waits 1, A10 waits 10

Real output of code/scheduler-as-product.py under python3 3.10. Queue demo: user A submits 10 one-slot jobs, then user B submits 1, on one GPU (illustrative workload).

Time O(n) · Space O(n) for the fair queue: each of the n jobs is appended and popped once, and the per-user deques hold them all.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ First come, first served

Whoever floods the queue wins. A's 10-job burst makes B's single job wait 10 slots. The source's point from fair queueing: FIFO lets a badly behaved sender hurt everyone.

✗ A bare "PENDING"

Correct, and useless. "Unexplained waits are longer than explained waits": with no reason, users suspect a rival team, cancel and resubmit.

✗ Chase allocation alone

A 95% allocated cluster can still spend 28% of allocated time at 0% busy. The dashboard looks green while the work isn't getting done.

✓ Scheduler as a product

A fair queue (B waits 1 slot, not 10), a pending reason with numbers and an estimate, and goodput and capacity waste on the dashboard.

Is more explanation and fairness always better?

Not for free. Fair queueing moves wait from light users onto heavy ones: A's last job now waits 10 slots instead of 9. An estimate is only as good as the scheduler's view of the future, and the source itself notes decisions are made without knowing future jobs, so a higher-priority arrival can break a promised start time. A wrong estimate probably costs more trust than no estimate (our inference, not the source's).

For a single team, or overnight batch pipelines with no one watching, a plain FIFO queue and a utilization graph are enough. The product work pays off on a shared cluster where people who don't trust each other compete for the same GPUs. The source's Queue section is one sentence: a job's priority includes a fair-share part based on what its owner was allotted and has already used; the fair-share card works that through.
