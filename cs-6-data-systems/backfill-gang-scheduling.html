Backfilling and gang scheduling

Backfilling is also called out-of-order or EASY scheduling (conservative backfilling is the stricter variant); gang scheduling is also called co-scheduling or all-or-nothing placement. The source guide's GPU-cluster case study uses both, under Policy and Scheduling.

In one breath

A big job at the head of the queue often has to wait for GPUs to free up, and those GPUs sit idle meanwhile. Backfilling reserves the big job's start time, then lets smaller jobs use the idle GPUs as long as they finish in time. Gang scheduling gives a distributed job all its GPUs at once or none, so half-placed jobs can't sit on GPUs waiting for each other.

J0 already holds 4 GPUs until hour 4. Queued at t = 0, in order: J1 8 GPUs × 2 h, J2 2 × 3 h, J3 2 × 6 h, J4 4 × 2 h. Runtimes are the users' estimates, taken as exact (illustrative jobs).

EASY rule, checked at every job finish, for each job j behind the head: start j now if gpus(j) ≤ free and ( now + hours(j) ≤ shadow or gpus(j) ≤ extra ) shadow = when enough GPUs free up for the head job → t = 4 (J0 ends) extra = GPUs free at shadow − head job's GPUs → 8 − 8 = 0

Gang scheduling: the same 8 GPUs as 4 machines

The FfDL paper's example: 4 machines × 2 GPUs, and 4 training jobs (A–D) of 2 learners each, where each learner needs a whole machine. A scheduler that places pods one at a time, like the default Kubernetes one, can pick the worst order.

✗ One pod at a time

M1A1
M2B1
M3C1
M4D1

Queued: A2 B2 C2 D2. 0 jobs running, 8 of 8 GPUs held idle: every learner waits for its partner.

✓ Gang: all learners or none

M1A1
M2A2
M3B1
M4B2

Queued whole: C D. 2 jobs training, 8 of 8 GPUs doing work.

Nothing breaks this deadlock except a timeout: the jobs hold GPUs until one gives up and fails. In FfDL's 60-GPU test (50 jobs × 2 learners × 1 GPU), per-pod placement left learners deadlocked in 60% of 20 runs (4 to 12 of them each time); their gang scheduler had none.

The EASY step in Python

# inside simulate(): run holds (name, gpus, end) of running jobs
if backfill and queue:
    head_g = queue[0][1]         # reserve for the head job
    shadow, avail = t, free
    for _, g, end in sorted(run, key=lambda r: r[2]):
        if avail >= head_g:
            break
        shadow, avail = end, avail + g
    extra = avail - head_g       # spare GPUs at shadow
    for job in queue[1:]:
        n, g, d = job
        ends_ok = t + d <= shadow
        if g <= free and (ends_ok or g <= extra):
            queue.remove(job)
            run.append((n, g, t + d)); start[n] = t
            free -= g
            if not ends_ok:
                extra -= g

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Strict FCFS

Fair and predictable, but nothing may pass a blocked head job: 16 GPU-hours idle before J1 starts, and J2 waits 6 h for a slot that was free all along.

✗ Start anything that fits

No reservation, so the 8-GPU job only runs when small jobs happen to leave a full gap. J1 slipped 4 → 6 h here; with small jobs arriving steadily it may never run (our reasoning, not simulated).

✗ Place pods one at a time

Gets the most pods running, but a distributed job needs all of them. In the worst order all 8 GPUs are held and no job trains until timeouts kill some.

✓ Reserve, backfill, place whole jobs

The head job keeps a fixed start (J1 at t = 4), small jobs fill the holes (average wait 5.5 → 4.0 h), and a job gets all its GPUs or waits without holding any.

Does backfilling always help?

Only as far as runtime estimates are honest. Backfilling decides with the time limits users submit. Mu'alem and Feitelson found users overestimate a lot, and that backfilling still works, even better when estimates are padded (our reading of why: early finishes open new holes). A job that underestimates gets killed at its time limit. For long training jobs with unknown runtimes, and in clusters of mostly identical jobs, there's little to fill.

Gang scheduling has a price too: a large job waits until all its GPUs are free, and GPUs held back for it idle meanwhile (the source says wasted cycles grow with job size). Backfilling is what fills that gap. The source also warns that preempting a distributed job is expensive, since model and data must move between GPU and main memory, so schedulers prefer to reserve and backfill rather than evict.

The source gives two backfill rules without naming them: Policy protects only the head of the queue (EASY); Scheduling protects every higher-priority job (conservative, Slurm's wording). Both are shown here.
