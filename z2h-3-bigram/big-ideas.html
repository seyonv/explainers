The six big ideas of the bigram model

The one-page version of the lecture: six claims, six sketches to redraw. Lecture 2 · The spelled-out intro to language modeling: building makemore · 0:00:00, 1:57:45 long

The problem: The bigram lecture builds the same model twice, first by counting and then by training a neural net, over nearly two hours of small tensor steps. Big idea 1: Counting pairs is training: the 27×27 table is the whole model

Pad every name with '.' at both ends, walk all 32,033 names, and add 1 to N[first, second] for each of the 228,146 pairs. Row i then holds everything the model knows about what follows character i.

“training the model” · Karpathy, 0:12:45

Why it matters: Every later model keeps this framing: a name is len + 1 next-character predictions. What breaks is the table: 3 characters of context need 27³ = 19,683 rows, most of them empty, which is why the next lecture stops counting.

Big idea 2: A language model writes by sampling one character at a time

Turn the current character's row into probabilities, draw the next character in proportion to them, make it the new current character, and stop when '.' comes up.

“very simple and weak” · Karpathy, 0:06:24

Why it matters: This exact loop is how GPT-2 generates text. Every model after this one changes only where p comes from, never the loop.

Big idea 3: Shapes decide the math: drop keepdim and you divide the wrong way, silently

P /= P.sum(1, keepdim=True) divides each row by its own total. Without keepdim the 27 totals have shape (27,), PyTorch reads that as a row, and the columns get divided instead: no error, and row 0 sums to 7.0225.

“Treat this with respect.” · Karpathy, 0:36:17

Why it matters: The same row division is inside every softmax you will write (counts.sum(1, keepdims=True)). Print .shape after every reduction; backprop ninja shows the broadcast has a backward pass too.

Big idea 4: One number grades the model, and one zero probability breaks it

Look up the probability the model gave to every bigram that really occurs, and average −log p. 0 would be perfect and log 27 = 3.2958 is blind guessing. The counts score 2.4540, but a single pair they never saw makes the loss infinite, so add fake counts.

“maximize likelihood of the data w.r.t. model parameters” · Karpathy, 0:50:14

Why it matters: This average NLL is the cross-entropy that trains every model in the series, and 3.2958 is the sanity check for any step-0 loss. The smoothing knob comes back as regularisation (idea 6).

Big idea 5: A neural net can hold the same table: one-hot @ W picks a row of log-counts

One-hot the current character, multiply by a 27×27 W, and you get row W[ix]: the logits. exp turns them into positive fake counts and dividing by the row sum gives probabilities, exactly the path a row of N took.

“the dumbest, smallest, simplest neural net” · Karpathy, 1:13:53

Why it matters: From here on only the arrow into the logits changes. The next lecture swaps the one-hot multiply for a direct embedding lookup, and the softmax and loss stay as they are.

Big idea 6: Gradient descent rediscovers the counts, and unlike counting it scales

Forward, W.grad = None, backward, W.data += −50·W.grad, on all 228,146 bigrams: 3.7590 → 2.4729 in 100 steps, closing in on counting's 2.4540 from above. It can't beat the counts, since both see one character, but the same loop works for any network.

counting and gradient descent arrive at the same model, but only the gradient-based approach scales to more context · Karpathy, paraphrased, 1:56:16

Why it matters: This is the micrograd loop on a tensor, and it is the loop that trains the MLP and GPT. Counting is still the right tool when one character of context really is the model.

1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • A name is len + 1 next-character predictions → counting every pair into a 27×27 table N and dividing each row by its total gives the probabilities that maximise the likelihood → counting is training for a bigram (2.4540 average NLL)
  • A row of P is a distribution over the next character → draw from it, feed the draw back as the new row, stop at '.' → the model generates names; since it only sees the last character, the names are name-ish but bad (117 one-letter names in 1,000)
  • The average NLL grades any model with one number (0 perfect, log 27 = 3.2958 uniform) → a zero probability makes it infinite → fake counts (N + 1) keep every probability above 0 at a small cost in training loss (2.4540 → 2.4546)
  • one-hot @ W picks row W[ix] → exp and normalise (softmax) turn it into probabilities, so W plays log N → gradient descent on the NLL moves W toward log N (3.7590 → 2.4729 in 100 steps) → the net reaches the counting answer from above, and L2 on W plays the fake-count knob
  • detail that matters The counting table needs a row per possible context (27ᵏ rows for k characters: 19,683 for 3) → most rows stay empty → the neural route keeps the same loss, backward and update and only changes how the logits are computed → that is why the series keeps it despite being slower here
  • detail that matters P.sum(1) without keepdim has shape (27,), read as a row → on a square table it broadcasts without error and divides columns (row 0 sums to 7.0225) → shape bugs in tensor code fail silently, so shapes must be checked, not trusted

Reference only

  • the names: stoi, itos, xs, ys, xenc, logits, counts, probs
  • seed 2147483647 and the torch 2.14 samples (cexze., …), which differ from the video's
  • PyTorch spellings: torch.multinomial(..., replacement=True), F.one_hot(xs, num_classes=27).float(), probs[torch.arange(n), ys], W.grad = None

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain, without code, why normalising the count table and training the one-layer net end at the same loss, and why neither can go below 2.4540 on the training names.
    Check
    • both models see only the previous character, so both are a 27-row table of next-character probabilities
    • the row-normalised counts are exactly the table that maximises the likelihood of the training bigrams
    • gradient descent minimises the same NLL, so it can only approach that optimum (2.4729 at 100 steps, 2.4624 at 200)
  2. mechanism Walk one example, the bigram .e, through the neural route: from the index 0 to the number it adds to the loss, naming each tensor and its shape.
    Check
    • xs = 0 → F.one_hot(..., num_classes=27).float() gives a (27,) row with a 1 in slot 0
    • @ W (27×27) gives the logits, which are just row W[0]
    • exp gives 27 positive counts; dividing by their sum gives probs that sum to 1
    • the loss term is −log probs[5] (e = 5); with the random W that is 4.3993
  3. tradeoff Fake counts and L2 regularisation both trade training loss for something. What do they buy, what do they cost, and why can't the training loss tell you how much to use?
    Check
    • buy: no probability is ever 0, so unseen pairs get a finite loss ('andrejq' 3.4834 instead of ∞)
    • cost: the training loss rises (2.4540 → 2.4546 at +1; the regularised net 2.4901 vs 2.4729 at step 99)
    • the training loss always prefers no smoothing, so the strength must be chosen on held-out names (a dev split, as in exercise E03)
  4. tradeoff Counting is exact, instant and has no learning rate. When would you still choose the neural route, and when is counting the better tool?
    Check
    • count when the model really is one character of context: it is exact and one pass
    • the table needs 27ᵏ rows for k characters of context (19,683 for 3), most never seen
    • the net keeps the same loss, backward and update and only changes how logits are computed, so it grows into an MLP and a transformer

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

You start the neural bigram with W = torch.zeros((27, 27)) instead of random numbers, and train on all words with lr 50 and no regularisation. Commit first: what is the loss at step 0, and after 100 steps is it above or below the random start's 2.4729?

Reveal

3.2958 at step 0, exactly log 27: every logit is 0, every count is 1, every row is uniform. After 100 steps it is 2.4705, slightly below the random start's 2.4729 (computed), because it starts much closer (3.2958 vs 3.7590) with nothing confidently wrong to undo. Both still sit above counting's 2.4540. Start from W = log(N+1) instead and step 0 is already 2.4546.
