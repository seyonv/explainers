Negative log likelihood: one number for 'how good'

Also called: NLL, the loss, log loss (the same thing as cross-entropy on these cards). Lecture 2 · loss function (the negative log likelihood of the data under our model) · 0:50:14

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: You now have a table P of next-character probabilities and it produces name-ish samples, but "looks OK" isn't a measurement. Without one number for quality you can't compare two models, notice a bug, or train anything.

Where it fits: The broadcasting card gave you a P whose rows each sum to 1. This card grades that P against the real names. The same score becomes the loss that the neural net minimises in the second half of the lecture.

In one breath: to grade the bigram model, look up the probability it gave to every bigram that really occurs in the names. Take the log of each probability, add them up, flip the sign and divide by how many there were. The result is the average negative log likelihood: 0 would mean the model predicted every next letter with certainty, and lower is better. The counting model scores 2.4540 on all 228,146 bigrams.

Predict first: the model's average NLL for "andrej" is 3.0391. Add one letter to get "andrejq". The seven old bigrams keep their probabilities. What is the new average?

Infinity. The new pair jq never appears in names.txt, so N[j, q] = 0, its probability is 0, and log 0 = −∞. A single impossible bigram swamps everything else, however good the other seven were. The smoothing card fixes this with fake counts.

Each column is one step of the recipe. The likelihood is the product of the probabilities: how likely the model thinks the whole word is (3.48 in a million). The log likelihood is the sum of the logs, since log(a·b) = log a + log b. Log is the natural log (base e). Every probability is at most 1, so every log is ≤ 0, and the sign flip makes the number positive: the negative log likelihood (NLL). Dividing by the number of bigrams gives the average.

The uniform guess scores −log(1/27) = log 27 = 3.2958 on every bigram, so 2.4540 means the counting model has learned something. The product of all 228,146 probabilities is 0.0 even in float64 (computed): the numbers are too small to store, which is why you add logs instead of multiplying.

▶ Build itruns on your M3 · about 2 s

When: watch from 0:50:14 and pause at 1:00:50, before the fake counts. Add this to the file where you built N, stoi and words.

P = N.float()
P /= P.sum(1, keepdim=True)   # each row sums to 1
def nll_of(ws):
    log_likelihood, n = 0.0, 0
    for w in ws:
        chs = ['.'] + list(w) + ['.']
        for ch1, ch2 in zip(chs, chs[1:]):
            log_likelihood += torch.log(P[stoi[ch1], stoi[ch2]])
            n += 1
    return log_likelihood, n
ll, n = nll_of(words)
print(f'all words: log_likelihood={ll:.2f} n={n} avg nll={-ll/n:.4f}')
for w in ['andrej', 'andrejq']:
    ll, n = nll_of([w]); print(f'{w}: avg nll={-ll/n:.4f}')

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ The likelihood itself

The product of probabilities is the "real" score, but it shrinks toward 0 with every bigram. Over all 228,146 it is 0.0 even in float64, so it can't tell two models apart.

✗ The summed log likelihood

Stable to compute, but it grows with the data: −38.79 for 3 names, −559,874 for all of them. You can't compare it across datasets or batch sizes.

✗ Top-1 accuracy

Only asks whether the single most likely letter was right. It gives no credit for putting 0.39 rather than 0.05 on the true letter, and it doesn't change smoothly, so there is nothing to follow downhill.

✓ Average NLL

One number per bigram, comparable across any amount of data: 0 is perfect and 3.2958 is uniform guessing. Minimising it is the same as maximising the likelihood, because log only ever goes up. It is the loss the neural net will train on.

Is 2.4540 good? And is lower always better?

For a bigram model on this training set, 2.4540 is the best possible: dividing each row of counts by its total gives exactly the probabilities that maximise the likelihood. The neural net at the end of this lecture starts at 3.7590 and reaches 2.4729 after 100 steps (measured). It is chasing this number, not beating it.

But this is a score on the training data. A lower training loss can simply mean the model memorised it. Makemore part 2 scores on held-out names (a dev/test split) for that reason. And as "andrejq" shows, the loss is only finite if the model never gives a real bigram probability 0. That is the job of smoothing.

Understand and remember

  • The model gives each observed bigram a probability → the likelihood of the data is the product of those probabilities → that product underflows to 0.0 over 228,146 bigrams → take logs, so the product becomes a sum that stays representable
  • Every p ≤ 1 → every log p ≤ 0 → negate to get a positive loss where 0 means perfect certainty → divide by n so the number is per bigram and comparable across datasets (2.4540 here vs 3.2958 for uniform guessing)
  • log is monotonic (always increasing) → the P that maximises the likelihood also minimises the average NLL → "make the loss small" and "make the data likely" are the same goal → which is why this loss can later train the neural net
  • A bigram the table never counted has p = 0 → log 0 = −∞ → one such bigram makes the whole loss infinite ("andrejq") → the model needs every probability to be above 0 (smoothing)
  • detail that matters Karpathy's loop adds 228,146 float32 tensors one at a time → rounding error builds up → torch 2.14 prints 2.4541 where float64 gives 2.454014: tiny, but a reminder that long running sums in float32 drift

Reference only

  • torch.log is the natural log (base e)
  • the loop shape: for ch1, ch2 in zip(chs, chs[1:]) with log_likelihood += torch.log(P[stoi[ch1], stoi[ch2]])
  • f-string formatting of a 0-dim tensor ({ll:.2f})
  • the first three words are emma, olivia, ava (16 bigrams, avg NLL 2.4241)

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Logits → counts → probabilities: the softmax: Here the probabilities come from normalised counts; there they come from a softmax over the neural net's logits. The NLL formula is identical. What is fundamentally different about the probabilities being scored, which design choice causes it, and how does each version fail?

Check
  • counts: the probabilities are fixed by the data and give the lowest possible training NLL, 2.4540
  • the softmax output depends on the weights W, so NLL becomes something you can push down with gradients (3.7693 on emma at random init, worse than uniform 3.2958)
  • counts fail with inf on an unseen pair; exp is never exactly 0, so the softmax always gives a finite loss
  • pick counts when one character of context is enough; pick the net when the context is too big to count

vs Softmax and cross-entropy, first look: The toolkit card calls it cross-entropy; this card calls it average negative log likelihood. Are they the same number here, and what would have to change for them to differ?

Check
  • here the target is a single known next character, so cross-entropy = −log p of that character = the NLL
  • averaged over the bigrams it is the same 2.4540
  • they would differ only if the targets were soft distributions rather than one correct character, which never happens in these lectures
