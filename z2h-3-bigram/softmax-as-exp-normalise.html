Logits → counts → probabilities: the softmax

Also called: softmax, exp-normalise, normalised exponential; logits = log-counts. Lecture 2 · transforming neural net outputs into probabilities: the softmax · 1:18:46

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The linear layer xenc @ W outputs 27 numbers per input that can be negative and don't add up to anything. You can't sample from them or score them with a log likelihood until they become a proper probability distribution.

Where it fits: The counting model turned a row of counts N into probabilities by dividing by the row total. The one-hot card built the layer that outputs logits. This card adds the missing link: treat the logits as log-counts, exp them into fake counts, and normalise exactly as before.

In one breath: the layer xenc @ W spits out 27 unrestricted numbers per input, called logits. Read them as log-counts: exp turns each into a positive fake count, like a row of the count table N, and dividing by the row total turns those into probabilities that sum to 1. Those two steps together are the softmax, and the negative log of the probability given to the right next character is the loss.

Predict first: W is 27×27 random numbers, not yet trained. A model that guesses 1/27 for every character scores an average NLL of log 27 = 3.2958 on 'emma'. Does the random net score better or worse?

Worse: 3.7693 (measured). Random logits aren't neutral. They make the net confidently wrong: after '.' it gives 'o' 0.2378, while the true next letter 'e' gets 0.0123, a third of the uniform 0.0370. Setting every weight to 0 makes all logits 0, all counts 1, and gives exactly 3.2958 (computed).

When: pause the video at 1:18:46 and type the softmax lines before watching on; add the loss line at 1:35:49. It reuses xs = [0, 5, 13, 13, 1] and ys = [5, 13, 13, 1, 0] from the one-hot card.

g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(2147483647)
W = torch.randn((27, 27), generator=g)
xenc = F.one_hot(xs, num_classes=27).float()
logits = xenc @ W                               # log-counts
counts = logits.exp()                           # like N
probs = counts / counts.sum(1, keepdims=True)   # softmax
print('probs[0, :5] :', [round(x, 4) for x in probs[0, :5].tolist()])
print('matches torch.softmax:',
      torch.allclose(probs, torch.softmax(logits, dim=1)))
loss = -probs[torch.arange(5), ys].log().mean()
print('vectorised loss:      %.4f' % loss)
print('F.cross_entropy:      %.4f' % F.cross_entropy(logits, ys))

Why exp, and not something simpler

✗ Divide raw logits by their sum

Negative logits give negative "probabilities". Row 'm''s logits sum to only 0.485, so 'a' gets 2.17. The loss is nan (computed).

✗ Clip negatives to 0, then divide

13 of row 0's 27 characters get exactly 0, including the right answer 'e'. That makes the loss infinite, and a clipped logit gets no gradient to climb back.

✗ Square, then divide

Everything is positive, but a logit of −2 and one of +2 score the same. The net can't push a character down by making its logit very negative.

✓ exp, then divide

Always positive, never exactly 0, keeps the order of the logits, and smooth everywhere, so gradients flow. And log undoes exp, which is why a logit really is a log-count.

Is this how you'd write it for real?

Not quite. exp overflows float32 above about 88: multiply these logits by 100 and the hand-written softmax returns nan in every row (computed). torch.softmax and F.cross_entropy first subtract each row's largest logit. That's safe because adding the same number to a whole row cancels in the division (logits + 10 gives identical probs, computed). So in practice you call F.cross_entropy(logits, ys), which also skips building probs; the MLP course switches to it.

Softmax is the wrong tool when several answers can be true at once (tags on a photo, say). It forces the row to share one unit of probability, so there you'd use a separate sigmoid per output instead.

Understand and remember

  • logits can be any real number → exp maps them to positive, order-preserving fake counts → dividing by the row total gives a distribution that sums to 1 → the net's output can be sampled and scored like a row of N
  • exp and log undo each other → a logit behaves exactly like log(count) → training W is fitting log-counts, so the net can in principle land on the counting answer
  • only probs[i, ys[i]] enters the loss → raising it forces the other 26 down, because the row must still sum to 1 → minimising the average NLL shifts probability onto the characters that really follow
  • random W gives spread-out, confident logits → probability lands on the wrong characters ('o' 0.2378 vs the true 'e' 0.0123) → loss 3.7693 is worse than uniform's log 27 = 3.2958, while W = 0 gives exactly uniform
  • detail that matters adding a constant to a whole row of logits cancels in the division → softmax ignores it → library softmax subtracts the row max first, so exp can't overflow float32 (above about 88), and the hand-written version returns nan for logits × 100

Reference only

  • counts.sum(1, keepdims=True) gives shape (5, 1) for row-wise division
  • torch.softmax(logits, dim=1) and F.cross_entropy(logits, ys) as the library versions
  • the fancy-index form probs[torch.arange(5), ys]
  • seed 2147483647 and the exact per-example values (.e 4.3993 … a. 4.2012)

Reveal

Shrinking helps and growing hurts, because this W is wrong, so being more confident about it costs more. × 0 gives 3.2958 (uniform), × 0.1 gives 3.3019, × 1 gives 3.7693, × 2 gives 4.9539, and × 10 gives 19.1543 (computed, F.cross_entropy(logits * s, ys)). The size of the logits sets how peaked the softmax is, which is why the batchnorm course scales initial logits down.
