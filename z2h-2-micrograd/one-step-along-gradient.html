One step along the gradient

Also called: a gradient step, gradient ascent (flip the sign and it's gradient descent, the step training uses). Lecture 1 · preview of a single optimization step · 0:51:10

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Backprop hands you a number on every leaf, but a gradient is useless until you act on it. Without a rule for turning grads into changes, you can measure how L responds but never steer it.

Where it fits: The chain-rule card filled in a 6, b −4, c −2, f 4 by hand. This card spends those numbers: one nudge of every leaf, then a rerun of the forward pass. It is the seed of the training loop at the end of the lecture.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why does moving every leaf by η × its own grad make L go up for sure, whatever the signs of the grads?
  2. Where does the 0.0065 gap between the predicted −7.28 and the real −7.286496 come from?
  3. What happens to the prediction when η grows from 0.01 to 0.1, and why?
  4. Why must e, d and L be recomputed after the step instead of nudged too?
  5. Of all moves with the same total length, why is the gradient direction the best one?

In one breath: each leaf's grad says how much L moves per unit nudge of that leaf. So if you move every leaf a little in the direction of its own gradient, L has to go up, and by about the amount the gradients predict. That one move is the whole idea of training: the gradients tell you which way to turn every knob.

Predict first: L = −8 and the leaf grads are a 6, b −4, c −2, f 4. Move each leaf by 0.01 × its grad. Does L go up or down, and to roughly what?

Up, to about −7.28. Each leaf adds grad × change = 0.01 × grad² to L, and a square is never negative. That's 0.01 × (36 + 16 + 4 + 16) = 0.72, so L ≈ −8 + 0.72 = −7.28. The real rerun gives −7.286496; the next section explains the small gap.

The step, leaf by leaf

Filled in: 0.01 × (6² + (−4)² + (−2)² + 4²) = 0.01 × 72 = 0.72, so L ≈ −7.28.

Why −7.2865 and not −7.28

The gradient is a local promise: it's exact only for a vanishingly small nudge, one leaf at a time. Here all four leaves move together, and L multiplies them (a·b, then ·f), so products of two moves sneak in:

one leaf at a time: 0.36 + 0.16 + 0.04 + 0.16 = +0.72 a and b together: f·Δa·Δb = −2 × 0.06 × −0.04 = +0.0048 (a,b,c) with f: (b·Δa + a·Δb + Δc)·Δf = (−0.18 − 0.08 − 0.02) × 0.04 = −0.0112 all three: Δa·Δb·Δf = −0.0024 × 0.04 = −0.000096 total ΔL = +0.713504 −8 + 0.713504 = −7.286496 (matches the rerun exactly)

Those extra terms are products of small moves, so they shrink much faster than the step itself. Make the step 10× smaller and the gap shrinks about 100×; make it 10× bigger and the prediction breaks (computed with the same formula):

A minus sign sends L down instead: that's gradient descent. Training minimises a loss, so the real loop does p.data += -0.01 * p.grad.

Why it beats the alternatives

Every option below moves the four leaves the same total distance, 0.01 × √72 ≈ 0.0849, and only the direction differs (computed).

✗ Push every leaf up equally

+0.0424 each. b and c want to go down, so half the move works against L. Result: L = −7.8338, a gain of only 0.166.

✗ Spend it all on the steepest leaf

Move only a (grad 6) by 0.0849. L = −7.4909, a gain of 0.509. It ignores b and f, which also help.

✗ Follow only the gradient's sign

±0.0424 each, by sign. Close (L = −7.3285, gain 0.672), but it spends as much on c (grad −2) as on a (grad 6).

✓ Along the gradient

Each leaf moves in proportion to its grad. L = −7.2865, a gain of 0.7135, the most of the four. For small steps, no direction of the same length does better.

Is this a training step yet?

Almost. Three things change in the training loop. First, the sign: you step against the gradient because you want the loss to fall. Second, the gradients go stale the moment the leaves move, so every step needs a fresh forward pass and a fresh backward pass. This card reused the old grads for one step, which is fine once but not in a loop. Third, you only nudge the parameters (weights and biases). The data inputs are fixed facts about the world, even though they're leaves too.

Bigger steps aren't simply faster. The table shows the prediction breaking at η = 0.1. On a real loss, too large a step can overshoot and make the loss go up, which is why the learning rate gets its own card later.

1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • each grad = slope of L along one leaf → moving leaf i by η·grad_i changes L by about grad_i·η·grad_i = η·grad_i² ≥ 0 → summed over leaves, L rises by about η·Σ grad² (0.01 × 72 = 0.72)
  • the gradient is exact only for tiny, one-at-a-time nudges → moving leaves together adds products of moves (f·Δa·Δb …) → the gap grows like η² → small steps match the prediction (−7.286496 vs −7.28), big ones break it (η = 0.1: −1.536 vs −0.8)
  • flip the sign (x -= η·grad) → L falls by about the same amount (−8.726304) → training minimises a loss, so every update steps against the gradient
  • the step moves the leaves → the old grads now describe a point you've left → each step needs a fresh forward and backward pass (5 steps: −11.9148 fresh vs −11.748 reusing stale grads)
  • detail that matters Only leaves are updated, and in a net only the parameters among them → the data inputs stay fixed even though they're leaves too → the loop must know which Values are parameters (the parameters() method later)

Reference only

  • Karpathy's exact step size 0.01 for this demo
  • the four update lines a.data += 0.01 * a.grad …
  • the name η for the step size
  • micrograd's demo uses p.data -= learning_rate * p.grad

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Start again from a=2, b=−3, c=10, f=−2 and take one step with η = −0.1 (ten times bigger, downhill). The first-order prediction is −8 − 7.2 = −15.2. Will the real L land above or below −15.2, and by roughly how much?

Reveal

Below: L = −15.744, a gap of −0.544. The terms made of two moves (like f·Δa·Δb) scale with η², so they keep their sign when η turns negative and pull L down by about 0.64 either way; the three-move term flips and gives back +0.096. So the big step overshoots the prediction by far more than the −0.0063 gap at η = −0.01.
