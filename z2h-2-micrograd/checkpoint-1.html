Checkpoint 1: nudges, graphs and the chain rule

Ties together a derivative is a nudge ratio, partial derivatives, the Value object, backprop by hand, one step along the gradient and a neuron, backpropagated, with the toolkit's derivative rules and chain rule. Lecture 1, 0:08:08 to 1:09:02.

What this ties together, and why now

You have now computed every gradient of Lecture 1's two examples yourself: first by nudging, then by walking the graph backwards. Those are the same number reached two ways. A derivative is the nudge ratio. A Value graph records how L was made. The chain rule walks that record backwards, multiplying one local derivative per node, and a step along the gradient is what the numbers are for. Karpathy's one-line summary of backprop is “just a recursive application of chain rule backwards through the computation graph” (0:32:10).

The next chapter (1:09:02) hides all of this behind one o.backward() call. Check that the pieces connect before they disappear into the engine.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which idea applies?Rebuild Karpathy's neuron with b = 8 instead of 6.8814. Its output becomes 0.9640, and w1.grad drops from 1.0 to about 0.14. Nothing about w1 or x1 changed. Which idea explains the drop, and what exact factor do you multiply by?
    Check
    • the chain rule: every gradient below the tanh is multiplied by tanh's local slope, 1 − o²
    • at b = 8, n = 2 and 1 − 0.9640² = 0.0707, compared with 0.5 before; so w1.grad = x1 × 0.0707 = 2 × 0.0707 = 0.1413 (computed)
    • 0.5 / 0.0707 ≈ 7.1× smaller. The neuron is heading into saturation, and past n ≈ 3 almost no gradient gets through

    Missed it? Reopen a neuron, backpropagated, “How much tanh lets through”, and the Predict first on the chain rule (the same 0.0707 at y = 2).

  2. You build L = (a*b + c)*f, then set a.data = 3.0 and print L.data. What prints? What prints once you rebuild e, d and L, and why can a.grad = 6 predict that second number exactly here, when the four-leaf step on the one-step card missed by 0.0065?
    Check
    • still −8.0: a Value stores the number computed when it was made, not a formula, so e, d and L are stale
    • after rebuilding: (3·(−3) + 10)·(−2) = 1 · (−2) = −2.0
    • the prediction is −8 + 6 × 1 = −2 exactly, because L = a·b·f + c·f is a straight line in a alone. The gap on the one-step card came from products of moves (Δa·Δb, …), and moving a single leaf creates none

    Missed it? Reopen the Value object, “What each field stores”, and one step along the gradient, “Why −7.2865 and not −7.28”.

  3. debuggingYour hand backprop for L = (a·b + c)·f prints a.grad = −4.0 and b.grad = 6.0, but every other grad matches the table. Your lol() check for a prints 6.000000000000227. What exactly did you type wrong?
    Check
    • the * rule is inverted. You wrote a.grad = a.data * e.grad = 2 × (−2) = −4, and b.grad = b.data * e.grad = (−3) × (−2) = 6
    • a times node hands each input the other input's value: a.grad = b.data × e.grad = 6, and b.grad = a.data × e.grad = −4
    • the nudge check is independent of your rules, so it caught the bug. This is exactly what a numerical check is for

    Missed it? Reopen backprop by hand, “The walk back, one node at a time”, rows 6–7.

  4. For d = a·b + c at (2, −3, 10), ∂d/∂a = −3. Change b to 5 and leave a alone. What is ∂d/∂a now, and why does a's own value never appear in it?
    Check
    • 5: the bump gives 5.000000000023874
    • with b and c frozen, a·b is “a times a constant”, and its slope is that constant
    • so a partial holds only at the current point. Change any other input and it changes, which is why training recomputes gradients after every step

    Missed it? Reopen partial derivatives, “What a partial doesn't tell you”.

  5. which idea applies?You derived w1.grad = 1.0 for the neuron, and you want to check it without trusting your own algebra. Which idea do you use, which h do you pick, and do you expect exactly 1.0 back?
    Check
    • the nudge ratio as a gradient check, like Karpathy's lol(): bump w1, rerun the neuron, divide the change by h
    • h ≈ 0.001 and never tiny: on f(x) = 3x² − 4x + 5, h = 10⁻¹⁵ gave 10.66 instead of 14
    • not exactly 1.0. One-sided, it gives 0.9986, because tanh curves inside the step. Nudging both ways, (o(w1+h) − o(w1−h)) / 2h, gives 1.000001 (computed in the build below)

    Missed it? Reopen a derivative is a nudge ratio, “Too small an h breaks it”, and derivative rules, “Do the rules always work?” (the centred version).

  6. The leaf grads are a 6, b −4, c −2, f 4, and L = −8. You step every leaf by +0.1 × grad instead of 0.01. What does the linear prediction say L will be, what do you actually get, and why do the two drift apart?
    Check
    • predicted: −8 + 0.1 × (36 + 16 + 4 + 16) = −8 + 7.2 = −0.8
    • actual: −1.536, a gap of 0.736, against 0.0065 at a step of 0.01
    • the gradient is exact only for a vanishingly small nudge. The products of moves (f·Δa·Δb, …) grow with the square of the step, so a step 10× bigger makes the gap about 100× bigger

    Missed it? Reopen one step along the gradient, the step-size table.

  7. Why does a + node “copy” the gradient while a × node “swaps” the inputs in? Derive each local derivative from the nudge definition, in one line each.
    Check
    • +: ((a + h) + b − (a + b)) / h = h / h = 1, so the input receives 1 × the grad from above
    • ×: ((a + h)·b − a·b) / h = h·b / h = b, so the input receives the other input × the grad from above
    • chain rule: grad of an input = local derivative × grad of the node it feeds. Those two rules, plus 1 − o² for tanh, are all the neuron needs

    Missed it? Reopen backprop by hand (the product derivation under the table), and derivative rules, “The seven rules”.

  8. which idea applies?A colleague proposes getting the gradient of the lecture's 41-parameter MLP by bumping each parameter by h and rerunning the net. Which idea replaces that plan? Give the cost of each approach in passes over the expression.
    Check
    • bumping: one forward pass, plus one extra per parameter (41), and each answer is approximate
    • the chain rule run forwards is exact, but one sweep gives the derivative for only one input, so it still takes 41 sweeps
    • the chain rule run backwards (backprop): one forward pass records the Value graph, and one backward sweep fills in all 41 grads exactly, reusing each node's grad (e.grad = −2 serves both a and b)

    Missed it? Reopen the Value object, “How it differs from related ideas”, and backprop by hand, “Why it beats the alternatives”.

3 · Integrated build: one neuron's gradient, three ways, then one step

▶ Build itruns on your M3 · under 1 s

What it reinforces: the nudge ratio and why h matters (nudge ratio, derivative rules), one knob at a time (partials), plus copies and times swaps (backprop by hand), tanh's 1 − o² (a neuron), and grad² always being non-negative (one step).

Where: a fresh Python file in your course venv, with plain floats and math.tanh and no Value class, so none of it can hide inside the engine.

  1. Write neuron(p) for Karpathy's neuron, taking a dict with x1 = 2, x2 = 0, w1 = −3, w2 = 1 and b = 6.8813735870195432, and returning tanh(x1·w1 + x2·w2 + b).
  2. Chain rule by hand. From o, compute dn = 1 − o², then the grads of x1, w1, x2, w2 and b using “plus copies, times swaps”.
  3. Nudge, one-sided. For each of the five, bump it by h = 0.001, rerun, and divide the change by h.
  4. Nudge, both ways. Do the same with (o(+h) − o(−h)) / 2h.
  5. One step. Move only the parameters (w1, w2, b), never the inputs, by +0.01 × grad. Predict the gain as 0.01 × (sum of their grad²) before you rerun, then rerun. Then do the same with −0.01.

Done when: your three columns agree with the check below; the central column matches the chain rule to 5 decimals while the one-sided column doesn't; the step raises o by 0.012280 against a predicted 0.012500; and you can say where the 0.00022 gap comes from.
