Computing masks fast

Also called mask computation, the token trie, the Earley parser, or vocabulary splitting. The cookbook's backend pages (llguidance, XGrammar, Outlines-core, LM Format Enforcer) explain how each one does it; the paper doesn't time the mask on its own, but its time per output token (Tables 2 and 3) is an independent check.

In one breath. A token mask says yes or no for every token in the vocabulary, before every output token, and it's computed on the CPU while the GPU waits. The tricks below avoid checking most tokens one by one: they walk a tree of shared prefixes, cheaply throw out impossible bytes, and look up answers computed in advance.

The max(…) form is our reading of the paper's statement that the mask "can run in parallel with the LM's forward pass": the mask costs nothing until it is slower than the GPU, and then the GPU sits idle (cookbook, constrained method).

Trick 1: walk a token trie, not a token list

llguidance stores the vocabulary as a prefix tree: each node is a token and each edge is one byte. If a node breaks the grammar, every token below it breaks it too, so the whole branch is skipped. The cookbook's tiny vocabulary, constrained to integers:

naive list: check all 12 tokens = 28 bytes fed to the parser token trie: visit 6 nodes (a, 1, 10, 103, 108, 1e) = 6 byte steps, one per edge skip 6 tokens (ab, an, and, ant, 1e1, 1e2) without looking mask: 1, 10, 103, 108 allowed; the other 8 → probability 0

Vocabulary and result (1, 10, 103, 108) are the cookbook's. The visit count of 6 is ours, from walking the trie with the rule "only digits". A real vocabulary shares far more prefixes, so a rejected branch can hide thousands of tokens.

Trick 2 and 3: throw out bytes, wake the parser rarely, look answers up

Think of a librarian finding every book that fits a request. The trie is shelving by title so a whole aisle can be skipped. UTF-8 pruning is throwing out books in no known alphabet. The lexer is a clerk who handles the easy "any word will do" parts so the expert (the parser) is called only for the hard ones. XGrammar pre-sorts nearly every book in advance and only thinks about the few that depend on what came before.

The independent check: the paper's time per output token

llama.cpp backend, GlaiveAI (paper, A100, Llama-3.1-8B): Llamacpp 29.98 ms ÷ LM-only 15.40 ms = 1.95× (+14.58 ms per token) Outlines 30.33 ms ÷ 15.40 ms = 1.97× (+14.93 ms per token) Guidance 6.37 ms ÷ 15.40 ms = 0.41× (faster than no grammar at all) HF Transformers backend, GlaiveAI: XGrammar 66.78 ms ÷ Guidance 36.92 ms = 1.81× (1.48–1.82× across 4 datasets)

TPOT is the whole step, not the mask alone, so it can't confirm "20 μs" directly. It does show the gap is real: two engines roughly double the step time, while Guidance beats the model with no grammar, partly because it skips steps when the next tokens are forced (see fast-forward). XGrammar being 1.81× slower than Guidance in the paper's HF setup doesn't match the cookbook's "slight edge" for XGrammar; the paper didn't test XGrammar inside vLLM or SGLang, where the cookbook's claim comes from. That last point is our inference. Your Ollama path is the paper's "Llamacpp" row (llama.cpp GBNF).

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Naive per-token check

Runs the grammar on all 248,320 tokens every step: 9.4 million checks for one 38-token order. The CPU falls behind and the GPU idles.

✗ Precompute everything

Outlines-core's lookups are O(1), but building the FSM delays the first token for every new schema (3.06–12.77 s GCT in the paper), and it can't do recursion.

✗ Character parser × trie in Python

LM Format Enforcer is flexible about field order and whitespace, but its per-token intersection in Python is slow, and it doesn't support recursive schemas.

✓ Prune, split, precompute the easy part

The trie skips whole branches, UTF-8 pruning drops 30–50%, the full parser runs at under 0.5% of trie nodes, and XGrammar looks up the >99%. The mask then stays under the GPU step.

Whose numbers are these, and do I need to care?

The 30–50%, 0.5%, 1% and 20 μs figures come from the cookbook, which relays the maintainers' own descriptions of llguidance and XGrammar. The paper doesn't measure mask time separately; its TPOT tables are the only independent timing, and they measure whole engines on one A100 with batch size 1. Treat the percentages as design claims, not benchmarks.

Usually you don't pick. On hosted APIs (OpenAI, Gemini, Anthropic) the provider computes the mask server-side, and on your M3 each token takes 57 ms on the GPU, so even a slow mask hides behind it. It matters when you run your own vLLM or SGLang server at high throughput, or send a new schema on every request. Then llguidance (no precompute) fits changing schemas, and XGrammar fits one schema reused many times. For one fixed, simple, non-recursive schema, Outlines-core's precompute-once approach is fine too.
