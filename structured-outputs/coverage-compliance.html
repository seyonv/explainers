Coverage and compliance

Also called declared coverage, empirical coverage, true coverage, compliance rate, or schema support. The paper's Section 5 scores six grammar engines on eight sets of real-world schemas (Table 4): open engines run Llama-3.2-1B, greedy, one output per schema, with a 40 s compile and a 40 s generation timeout.

In one breath. Coverage is the share of real schemas an engine can handle. Declared coverage counts the schemas it accepts without an error. Empirical coverage counts the schemas where its output actually passed validation. Compliance divides the two: once the engine says yes to a schema, how often does it keep that promise?

The three kinds of coverage, two examples each

Think of a restaurant. Declared: the dish is on the menu. Empirical: it came out right the one night you ordered it. True: the kitchen makes it right for every order, every night. Only the first two can be checked.

How it differs from related ideas

✗ Test-suite pass rate

The official JSON Schema Test Suite checks one keyword at a time: Guidance is 100% on 13 of 45 categories (paper, Table 5). The paper says there is "no straightforward correspondence" with coverage, because real schemas use some keywords far more than others.

✗ Theoretical coverage

Read off the engine's docs: are all of this schema's features listed as supported? No model runs. On GitHub-Hard, Guidance is 0.54 theoretical (Table 12) but 0.41 empirical, so the docs promise more than one run delivered.

✗ "Works on my schema"

One schema, one prompt. Our order schema passed 30 / 30 on Ollama; the same engine silently ignored multipleOf. A pass on one schema tells you nothing about the keyword you add next month.

✓ Coverage and compliance, read together

Coverage says how many of your kinds of schema an engine will even try. Compliance says whether "accepted" can be trusted. You need both: 1.00 compliance on 9% of schemas and 0.06 compliance on 47% are both bad on GitHub-Hard, in opposite ways.

Is 100% compliance the one to pick?

Not by itself. OpenAI's 1.00 on GitHub-Hard means it refuses what it can't do: 91 of 100 schemas are rejected up front, and the 9 it takes all come out valid. That's good for reliability, because you get an error, not silent bad output. It's bad if your schema is one of the 91. Outlines is the opposite failure: it takes 47 and delivers 3, mostly because generation runs past the 40 s limit. LM-only "accepts" everything (declared 1.00) because it enforces nothing, so its compliance just equals its empirical coverage.

These are averages over thousands of other people's schemas, from late-2024 versions, on a 1B model. None of that is your setup. Test your actual schemas: send each one to the engine, keep any rejection, and validate a few sampled outputs per schema with jsonschema. If a keyword you rely on fails, drop it from the grammar and check it in code afterwards (see repair-loop).
