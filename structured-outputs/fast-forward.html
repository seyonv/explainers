Why constraining can be faster

Also called guidance acceleration (Guidance), fast-forwarding, forced tokens, jump-forward decoding (SGLang), token skipping or, at dottxt, coalescence. It's behind the paper's headline that constrained decoding "can speed up the generation process by 50%" (Table 2, paper, A100, Llama-3.1-8B).

In one breath. Much of a JSON answer isn't a choice at all: once the model has written "sku":"BW-C, the schema allows only ITRUS","qty": next. An engine that notices this can append those forced tokens straight away instead of asking the model for them one at a time, so the model only runs where it actually has to decide something.

Worked example: which parts of our answer are forced?

The correct order, minified, with every character marked. A character is forced if the schema allows exactly one next character at that point, and chosen if there's more than one option.

{"items":[{"sku":"BW-CITRUS","qty":3},{"sku":"CANDLE-LAVENDER","qty":1}],"total_usd":54}

forced by the schemachosen by the model
Computed by a script that tries every printable character at every position against the schema. It assumes keys come in schema order and no whitespace is allowed, as a compact grammar would emit them.

88 characters in the minified answer forced = 74 keys, quotes, colons, commas, the rest of each SKU chosen = 14 5 SKU letters (B W C C L) + 4 digits (3 1 5 4) + 5 structure choices (} , } ] }): end the number? another item? forced fraction = 74 / 88 = 84% of characters the end-of-text after the final } is forced too

The SKU letters show how little the model has to decide. B is one of 6 possible first letters. After BW- there are only 2 options (CITRUS or EUCALYPT), and after C the rest is fixed. In tokens: the answer is 35 Qwen tokens. Counting prefix tokens at each span boundary (prompt_eval_count, measured on M3, qwen3.5:4b) puts about 26 of the 35 inside forced spans and about 9 in chosen ones, so roughly 74%. That token split is approximate, because a token can straddle a boundary (for example -C).

The words, with two examples each

Think of filling in a paper form. The printed labels ("SKU:", "Qty:") are already there, so you only write in the boxes. Fast-forwarding stops the model from rewriting the labels one letter at a time.

The mechanism as a formula

TPOTff≈ tstep× nout−nforcedtokens the model picks + njumpsone pass per forced span nout
t_steptime for one model pass (one ordinary decoding step)15.40 ms LM-only, GlaiveAI n_outtokens in the finished answer35 for our order n_forcedtokens the schema fixes≈ 26 (approx.) n_jumpsforced spans, each read in one batched pass7
TPOT_ff / t_step ≈ (n_out − n_forced + n_jumps) / n_out our order: (35 − 26 + 7) / 35 = 16 / 35 = 0.46 → about 54% less time per token paper: 6.37 / 15.40 = 0.41 (GlaiveAI) 7.44 / 15.83 = 0.47 (GitHub Easy)

The formula is our simplification, not the paper's: it treats a batched pass over a forced span as costing about one normal step, and it ignores mask time. Our 0.46 is a back-of-envelope estimate for our answer, not a measurement. It just happens to land in the same range as the paper's measured ratios.

Your 2× on the M3 is a different effect

In the course's 30-run test, the constrained arm finished in 2.72 s median vs 5.53 s for prompt-only (measured on M3, qwen3.5:4b). That is not fast-forwarding: it wrote about half as many tokens, because the grammar ruled out ```json fences and it chose compact JSON.

end to end: 5.53 / 2.72 = 2.03× faster tokens: 80 / 38 = 2.11× fewer ← most of the gap speed: 17.47 / 14.86 tok/s = 1.18× (67.3 → 57.2 ms per token) writing time: 80 / 14.86 = 5.38 s vs 38 / 17.47 = 2.18 s

There's no sign that Ollama skips forced tokens. A light check (measured on M3, qwen3.5:4b, temperature 0, 3 runs) used a schema whose answer is almost all one forced 66-character key. Ollama reported eval_count = 25 generated tokens every time, for text that tokenizes to 17 on its own. Every forced token still went through the sampler, in more, smaller pieces than normal tokenization would use. That's our reading of the counters; Ollama doesn't document a skip either way. The same run shows Ollama's grammar allows whitespace (it wrote {\n "…": false\n}), so under Ollama even the gaps between tokens are choices. We don't know why per-token speed was 1.18× higher in the constrained arm, and we didn't reuse this run's per-token timing, because other jobs were sharing the machine.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Unconstrained generation

The model writes every brace and key itself, one pass per token, and adds extras: 80 tokens with fences and pretty-printing vs 38 constrained (measured on M3, qwen3.5:4b).

✗ Naive masking

Checks the mask at every step and still samples forced tokens one by one, so it adds overhead without saving any steps: llama.cpp 29.98 ms/token vs LM-only 15.40 ms, 1.95× slower (paper, A100, Llama-3.1-8B).

✗ Speculative decoding

A small draft model guesses several tokens and the big model checks them in one pass. It helps free text too, but it needs a second model, and the guesses can be wrong. The paper lists "constraint-based speculative decoding" as a related optimisation but doesn't measure it.

✓ Fast-forward forced tokens

The grammar already knows the next tokens for sure, so there's nothing to guess and nothing to reject. Guidance cut time per token by 38–60% across the paper's datasets, which is how constraining ends up faster than no constraint.

Will my structured calls get 2× faster?

Only if two things are true: your engine actually fast-forwards, and your output is mostly boilerplate. The paper credits the speedup to Guidance specifically. llama.cpp's grammar and Outlines were about 2× slower per token than no grammar in the same table. Hosted APIs (OpenAI, Gemini, Anthropic) don't say whether they skip forced tokens, and you can't see their steps. Ollama showed no sign of skipping in our check.

The gain also shrinks as the free text grows. Our order is about 84% forced by character, but a schema like {"summary": string} holding 200 words is nearly all chosen text, so there's almost nothing to skip. For those, the levers that matter are shorter output (compact formats) and, for prose, speculative decoding. Also note that TPOT is the wrong yardstick here: it's per output token, so skipping makes it look faster even though the tokens the model chooses cost the same. Compare end-to-end time for your real schema.
