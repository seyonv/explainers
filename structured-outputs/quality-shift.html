Does constraining hurt quality?

Also called distribution shift, the "Let me speak freely" debate, format restriction, or reasoning degradation. The paper's Section 6 re-runs the reasoning tasks from that debate with a {"reasoning", "answer"} schema on four grammar engines (Llama-3.1-8B, Table 8).

In one breath. A grammar only removes tokens that would break the format, so in principle the answer shouldn't get worse. In practice it can, if the removed tokens were how the model wanted to write the answer, or if the format makes it answer before it thinks. With a reasoning field first and a sensible prompt, the paper found every engine matched or beat prompt-only.

The equation shows why the order of allowed tokens never changes. What it can't show is what happens next: the model now continues from text it would not have written, and the paper calls this the "distributional shifts caused by the intervention".

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Free text, then parse

The model reasons freely, but you are back to parsing prose: on M3 only 7/30 raw replies loaded as JSON. In the paper, prompt-only was the lowest or tied lowest on every task.

✗ Answer freely, then constrain (two-step)

Tam et al.'s "NL-to-Format": reason in text, then a second call converts it. Scores stayed close to free text, but it costs a second call and "occasionally introduces generation errors" in the conversion.

✗ No reasoning field

Cheapest (12–14 tokens), and fine when the answer is obvious: 20/20 on the lead reply. On a multi-step question it has no working: 0/10 on Last Letter on M3, the same failure Tam et al. found in answer-first JSON.

✓ Constrain, with an unconstrained reasoning field first

One call, guaranteed JSON, and the model thinks before it commits. The paper's version matched or beat prompt-only in 12 of 12 engine–task pairs. Don't put a template or enum inside reasoning: the cookbook says "the LLM must be free" there.

So it never hurts?

No, it can hurt, and you should test on your own task. The paper's tasks have tiny outputs (one letter, one word, one number) on an 8B model, and it lists "simple output structures" as a limitation. The cases where people report losses: answer-first schemas on multi-step reasoning (Tam et al.'s GPT-3.5 JSON-mode); rigid formats that force whitespace, field order or number style the model wouldn't choose, so it drifts like the 89,000 toy; prompts that change between the two arms, so you are testing the prompt, not the grammar; and small models, which have less room to recover from a forced token (our inference; not measured here).

Also, format helps in some places. Tam et al. themselves found JSON mode raised Gemini-1.5-Flash on the DDXPlus classification task (41.6% → 60.3%), and concluded that strict formats "may hinder reasoning-intensive tasks but enhance accuracy in classification tasks". If your task is extraction or labelling, constraining is usually the safe side; if it's hard multi-step reasoning, put the reasoning first or keep it outside the schema, and run 20–30 samples of each on your own inputs before deciding.
