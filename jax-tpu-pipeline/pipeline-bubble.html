Micro-batches and the pipeline bubble

Also called the pipeline schedule, GPipe schedule, fill and drain, or bubble overhead. It's what Sholto's plan in the post does: run the mini-batches in a loop, shifting the data along after each step, so that n_time_shards micro-batches are being processed at once (paraphrased).

In one breath

When a model's layers are split into stages on different chips, a stage can only work once the stage before it has handed something over. If you send the whole batch through at once, all but one stage sits idle. Cutting the batch into small micro-batches lets every stage work on a different one at the same time. The idle time that's left, while the pipeline fills at the start and empties at the end, is the bubble.

The words, in plain terms

Think of a four-person sandwich line: bread, filling, wrap, bag. With one giant order, three people stand around while one works. With many small orders, everyone is busy once the line fills up. Only the very first and last orders leave people waiting.

Bubble = idle slots ÷ (4 × steps). m = 16 is GPipe's rule of thumb M ≥ 4 × K for p = 4; the paper found the overhead "negligible" from there, partly because recomputation in the backward pass can start early. The backward pass adds its own fill and drain, and the fraction stays the same.

Why the shift is the hard part

Every green arrow in the grid is one stage sending a micro-batch's activations to the next stage. One forward pass makes m × (p − 1) of them: 4 × 3 = 12 sends for m = 4, and 48 for m = 16. The backward pass sends gradients the other way. Inside one host, Sholto expected this to work easily. With one stage per host, every arrow becomes host A → host B, and the post asks how to do that send over ICI instead of copying through the CPU.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ One big batch through the pipeline

m = 1, so only one stage works at a time: 75% of the chips are idle. GPipe: with M = 1 "there is effectively no pipeline parallelism."

✗ Just add more stages

The bubble grows with p. At m = 8, going from 4 to 8 stages raises it from 27.3% to 7/15 = 46.7%. Each stage holds fewer layers, but more of them wait.

✗ Asynchronous pipelines (PipeDream)

No flush, so no bubble, but GPipe says this "suffers from weight staleness": a micro-batch's backward pass can see different weights from its forward pass, so PipeDream keeps several weight copies per chip.

✓ Many micro-batches (GPipe, 1F1B)

Cut the batch into m ≫ p pieces and keep one synchronous update per step. The idle slots stay fixed at p(p − 1) while busy ones grow, so p = 4, m = 16 is 15.8% idle with ordinary training maths.

So why not m = 1,000?

Micro-batches can't shrink forever. m is the batch size divided by the micro-batch size. Smaller slices give each chip less work per step, so it runs less efficiently. Megatron-LM measured up to 1.3× higher per-GPU throughput from larger micro-batches and found the best size depends on the model, p, and the batch size.

Memory grows with m. In the GPipe all-forward, all-backward order, every micro-batch's activations wait in memory until its backward pass. The 1F1B schedule (one forward, one backward, also called PipeDream-Flush) caps the micro-batches "in flight" at the pipeline depth: at most 4 instead of 16 here, with the same bubble. Megatron's interleaved version gives each chip v smaller chunks of layers and cuts the bubble by v, but communication also grows by v.

The bubble is paid every training step, because every step ends with a flush before the weight update. Two conventions get mixed up. Megatron writes the bubble as (p − 1)/m, measured against useful compute time: 3/4 = 75% for m = 4. That's the same 12 idle slots as 42.9% of the total. Pipelining isn't always the right tool: if the model fits without it, data or tensor parallelism has no bubble at all (an inference from the formula, not a measured result).
