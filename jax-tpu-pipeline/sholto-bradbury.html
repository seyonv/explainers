Why a GitHub question became a job at Google

Sholto Douglas, then a McKinsey consultant doing research on nights and weekends, posted "Pipelining, xmap, and the global device array" on the JAX GitHub (discussion #9696, 25 Feb 2022). James Bradbury, a Google engineer on JAX and TPUs, replied the same day. About ten weeks later Sholto announced joining Google's JAX team.

In one breath

The question showed that a complete outsider had independently reached a problem that almost nobody outside Google had: getting many TPU hosts to act as one machine. It also showed they'd already done the work, trying two tools and explaining exactly why each one failed. Bradbury, in Sholto's retelling, reacted with: "I thought I knew all the people in the world who were asking these questions. Who on earth are you?"

The exchange

"…was wondering if there was a jax equivalent that leverages the ICI links that exist between TPU nodes… and allows for send/recv style operations…"

Sholto, the post

"TPUs don't really have a notion of 'node' other than the literal physical host machine; the TPU chips are connected in a homogeneous ICI network that doesn't care about host boundaries."

Bradbury, 4.7 hours later

"Collective-permute is the operation I was looking for to shift the buffer… I'd be able to figure this out if I was able to play around with a pod slice — but I don't currently have access to one."

Sholto, next day

"I was hired, as I understood it later, as an experiment in trying to take someone with extremely high enthusiasm and agency and pairing them with some of the best engineers that he knew."

Sholto, Dwarkesh podcast, 2024

Bradbury's reply settles the whole question: the "between nodes" problem doesn't exist, because ICI ignores host boundaries. That's why Google built the global device array.

How it differs from the usual ways in

✗ Credentials first

Sholto didn't get into the grad programs they wanted. A résumé filter would never have surfaced a McKinsey consultant with an undergrad degree in robotics.

✗ Private tinkering

The same nights-and-weekends work, kept on a laptop, is invisible. Nobody can be impressed by a question they never see.

✗ Generic questions in public

Being visible isn't enough. "How do I start with JAX?" says nothing about how far someone has got.

✓ A frontier question, in public

A specific, well-researched question where the right expert already hangs out shows depth, agency and taste all at once, and it can be found.

Was it just this one post?

No, and the exact link is an inference. In Sholto's telling, Bradbury "saw some of my questions online" and also "the robotic stuff that I'd been putting up on my blog". Sholto doesn't name this thread. It fits closely: right topic, same-day reply from Bradbury, ten weeks before the hire. But it was one visible piece of a longer effort: a TPU Research Cloud grant, 10pm–2am research sessions, and a public blog.

The lesson isn't "post on GitHub and get hired". Most good questions get a good answer and nothing more. It worked because the question was proof of rare, hard-won work, and the person reading it was actively scouting. In 2025, asked whether the "solve an open GitHub issue" route still works, Sholto said: "That's still what we look for in hiring."
