Study path · Day 3 · one page

The math of serving

Day 3 of the inference study plan: the step-time formula, why memory decides the batch, and how one chip becomes a fleet. It covers all of perf-2 and the Scaling Book inference chapter, checks it with Serving station 2, and ends with the Serving guide's capacity-calculator project. Everything is inline: just scroll. Ticks are shared with the checklist version.

What today uses, and why

Day 2 gave you the intuition: decode reads memory, and batching spreads the reads over many users. Day 3 turns that into numbers you can compute: how long one step takes, how many users fit, and how many chips a model needs.

perf-2: the Scaling Book inference courseCourse 2 of the Fast LLM inference series, built on the inference chapter of Google's “How to Scale Your Model”.
The most rigorous serving maths that's still readable. The course reproduces the chapter's numbers one step at a time.
The Scaling Book chapter itselfThe source, in the Go deeper lists.
Open it when a card compresses a derivation too far.
Serving principles 3 and 8, and station 2Field-guide rules and a lesson.
The principles tell you this morning what you're about to prove. Station 2 checks the step-time formula against real measurements (gpt-fast going from 25 to 245 tok/s).
Serving project 1: napkin to meterA project brief from the Serving guide.
You read it at the start and do its paper half at the end. Predicting a real server's capacity before you see its numbers is the proof the day worked.
The chapter's worked problemsOptional practice problems.
Do two from a blank page. Derivations only stick once you've redone them yourself.
By the end of today, you'll be able to
  • Explain why caching K and V is exact, and why a 183× cut in FLOPs becomes only a 3.6× cut in time.
  • Compute any operation's arithmetic intensity, compare it with the chip's ridge point, and derive the critical batch size.
  • Fill in the step-time formula for any model, chip, batch and context, and predict where throughput flattens.
  • Count a model's parameters and KV bytes from its shapes, and work out how many sequences fit on given hardware.
  • Explain why throughput ∝ 1 ÷ KV bytes per token once memory is full, and rank the tricks that shrink the KV cache.
  • Choose a sharding for prefill and for generation, and size a Llama-3-70B deployment end to end.
  • Build a capacity calculator on paper and check it against a real server's numbers.

Phase 1 · What one decode step costs

~2 h · 0 / 8

Six cards that build the step-time formula one term at a time.

Before you read · 5–10 min

1 · Prime. Read what each card below will let you do. Nothing else yet.

  • Sampling and the KV cache. Explain why caching K and V is exact and what it saves, compute its memory cost for a real model, and say why a 183× FLOP cut becomes only a 3.6× time cut.
  • What we optimize. Pick the right objective for a workload, justify it with the reading-speed arithmetic, and say which kind of operation dominates a decode step at short and at long context.
  • The roofline, properly. Compute an operation's intensity, compare it with a chip's ridge, bracket its run time, and explain why a matmul's intensity grows with batch while a dot product's never does.
  • Linear layers and the critical batch size. Derive Bcrit from the matmul bytes, compute it for a chip and a precision, and explain why decode rarely reaches it even though prefill always does.
  • Attention's arithmetic intensity. Derive attention's intensity, explain why B and D cancel, compute it for prefill, decode and GQA, and say which lever speeds up decode attention.
  • The step-time formula. Fill the formula for any model, chip, batch and context; say which term dominates; and predict where throughput flattens and why.

Phase 3 · From one chip to a fleet

~1.5 h · 0 / 7

Sharding, engine design and one full deployment sized end to end.

Before you read · 5–10 min

1 · Prime. Read what each card below will let you do. Nothing else yet.

  • Sharding prefill vs generation. Choose a sharding for prefill and for generation, justify it from the roofline, and compute how far model parallelism can go before communication becomes the bottleneck.
  • Sharding the KV cache. Lay out a KV cache over a chip mesh without copies, count the communication it adds, and say when that communication is bound by latency rather than bandwidth.
  • Designing an inference engine. Pick an engine design for a workload, explain the latency cost of each option from the prefill-vs-step ratio, and size a disaggregated pipeline by matching rates.
  • Serving LLaMA 3-70B end to end. Size a deployment end to end: fit the weights, compute the batch the leftover HBM allows, check the bound and the sharding limit, and read the cost off QPS per chip.
  • Speculative sampling, and is batch > 240 real?. Explain why verifying K tokens costs about one step, compute the expected speedup for given α, K and c, and predict at which batch speculation stops helping.
