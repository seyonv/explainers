Fix 2: tanh units stuck at ±1

Also called: saturated tanh, vanishing gradients through the nonlinearity, dead neurons. Lecture 4 · fixing the saturated tanh · 0:12:59

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: After fix 1 the step-0 loss looks right, yet most hidden units are pinned near ±1. There, tanh passes almost no gradient back, so the weights feeding those units barely learn, and nothing in the loss tells you.

Where it fits: It builds on tanh's backward rule from micrograd ((1 - t**2) * out.grad) and on fix 1, which tamed the output layer. This card does the same for the hidden layer; the next cards explain why ReLU has the same problem and how to pick the scale without guessing.

In one breath: tanh squashes each hidden unit's input into (−1, 1), and on the way back it multiplies the gradient by 1 − t², where t is the unit's output. After fix 1, the MLP's step-0 loss looks healthy, but its hidden preactivations are so large that 61% of the tanh outputs sit beyond ±0.99, where that factor is under 0.02. Shrinking W1 by 0.2 and b1 by 0.01 brings them back into tanh's working range, and the dev loss drops to 2.1027.

Predict first: a tanh unit outputs t = 0.99. If a gradient of 1.0 arrives at its output, how much of it reaches the unit's input hpreact, and so the weights W1 that feed it?

About 2%: 1 − 0.99² = 0.0199. tanh's local slope is 1 − t², so near ±1 it is almost flat and passes almost nothing back. At t = 0 it passes 100%. In the step-0 batch of this card's net, 61.0% of the 6,400 outputs are beyond ±0.99.

On the way back, tanh is a gate

Here hpreact (the preactivation) is what goes into tanh, and h = tanh(hpreact) is what comes out. .grad means "d loss / d this value", as in micrograd. The micrograd tanh backward ((1 - t**2) * out.grad) applies to every one of the 32 × 200 units:

What the net looks like at step 0

Each hpreact entry is a sum of 30 products (embedding entry × weight) plus a bias. With everything drawn from randn (std 1), the sum's spread grows with the count of terms:

std(hpreact) ≈ √(30 · var(emb) · var(W1) + var(b1)) W1 × 1, b1 × 1: √(30 · 0.8347 · 1 + 1) = √26.04 = 5.10 measured 5.18 W1 × 0.2, b1 × 0.01: √(30 · 0.8347 · 0.04 + 0.0001) = √1.002 = 1.00 measured 1.00

var(emb) = 0.9136² = 0.8347 is the measured variance of the step-0 batch's embeddings. A std of 5 puts most preactivations far past ±2.65, where the gate is under 2%.

A dead neuron is a hidden unit saturated on every example: plt.imshow(h.abs() > 0.99) would show it as an all-white column, and its weights would get almost no gradient. None here, so this net was slowed down, not broken. Karpathy calls the preactivations "−5 to 15" and the fixed ones about ±1.5; the 5th–95th percentile of the fixed batch is −1.6 to 1.7. Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Trust the step-0 loss

After fix 1 it reads 3.3221, close to the ideal 3.2958, while 61% of the hidden units are saturated. The loss checks the output layer, not the hidden one.

✗ Train anyway

One hidden layer is forgiving: it still reaches val 2.1311, against 2.1027 with the fix. Karpathy's warning is that in a deep net the shrinking compounds at every layer, and it may not train at all.

✗ Swap tanh for ReLU

ReLU has no flat tail on the right but a flat left half: 50.0% of [−5, 5] has a local gradient under 0.01, against 40.1% for tanh. Its units can die for good (next card).

✓ Scale W1 so hpreact is about unit-sized

W1 × 0.2, b1 × 0.01: hpreact std 1.00, 1.0% saturated, and 3.9× more gradient reaches W1 at step 0. Val 2.1027.

Is any saturation a bug?

No. A unit that is saturated on some examples is fine: it still learns from the others, and after the fix 1.0% saturation is left, which is harmless. The failure is a unit saturated on every example, the all-white column, because then no example ever sends it a useful gradient. Check with h.abs() > 0.99 over a batch, as above.

The 0.2 is a hand-picked number, and picking one per layer does not scale to deep nets. The Kaiming init card derives it instead: (5/3)/√30 = 0.3043. BatchNorm then removes the guessing by standardising hpreact directly.

Understand and remember

  • tanh backward multiplies by 1 − t² → near t = ±1 that factor is almost 0 (0.0199 at 0.99, exactly 0 in float32 past about 9) → almost no gradient reaches hpreact → W1 and b1 for that unit barely move
  • hpreact sums 30 embedding × weight products plus a bias → with std-1 randn its std is about √(30·0.83 + 1) ≈ 5 → most values land past ±2.65 → 61% of units saturated at step 0
  • Saturated on every example → the whole column of h.abs() > 0.99 is white → no example ever sends that unit a useful gradient → a dead neuron that never learns (saturated on only some examples is fine)
  • Multiply W1 by 0.2 and b1 by 0.01 → hpreact std about 1 → 1.0% saturated and 3.9× more gradient passes the gates at step 0 → dev loss 2.1311 → 2.1027
  • detail that matters The loss only sees the output layer → fix 1 already made it 3.32 → saturation in the hidden layer is invisible to the step-0 loss, so you must look at h directly (histogram and imshow)

Reference only

  • plt.hist(h.view(-1).tolist(), 50) and plt.imshow(h.abs() > 0.99, cmap='gray', interpolation='nearest')
  • the 0.99 threshold is a convention, not a law
  • the exact scales 0.2 and 0.01 (hand-picked; the Kaiming card replaces 0.2 with 0.3043)
  • float32 torch.tanh returns exactly 1.0 from about x = 9

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain why a hidden unit whose output is always near +1 contributes almost nothing to learning, even though its output is large.
    Check
    • what matters for learning is the gradient, not the output size
    • tanh's backward multiplies by 1 − t², about 0 near ±1
    • so the unit's incoming weights and bias get almost no update
    • its output is also nearly constant, so it carries little information about the input
  2. mechanism Walk through one backward pass for a single unit with hpreact = 3 and a gradient of 1.0 arriving at h. What value reaches hpreact, and what happens to the update of its W1 column?
    Check
    • t = tanh(3) = 0.995
    • hpreact.grad = (1 − 0.995²) · 1.0 ≈ 0.0099
    • W1's gradient for that column is the input times 0.0099, so about 100× smaller than at hpreact = 0
    • with the same lr the column barely moves
  3. tradeoff You could shrink W1 by 0.05 instead of 0.2: saturation drops to 0.0% and the mean gate rises to 0.944. What do you give up, and why isn't smaller always better?
    Check
    • h's std falls to 0.237, so tanh runs in its nearly linear region
    • the hidden layer then acts almost like a linear map at init and must grow its weights to use the nonlinearity
    • too large saturates, too small linearises: the aim is hpreact of roughly unit size
    • 0.2 is a hand guess; Kaiming init computes the scale
  4. tradeoff A colleague's step-0 loss is 3.31 for a 27-class model, so they say the init is fine. What would you check next, and what would convince you the hidden layer is healthy?
    Check
    • the loss only reflects the output layer
    • histogram h: no big spikes at ±1
    • h.abs() > 0.99 map: few white cells, no all-white columns
    • mean of 1 − h² well above 0.2 (0.605 after the fix vs 0.154 before)

vs Dead neurons across nonlinearities: tanh saturates at both tails; ReLU is flat on its whole negative side. How does each go dead, and which is easier to revive?

Check
  • tanh: large |hpreact| on every example, gate 1 − t² ≈ 0 but not exactly 0 until about |x| = 9
  • ReLU: hpreact negative on every example gives a gradient of exactly 0, so no update can ever bring it back
  • tanh's flat share of [−5, 5] is 40.1%, ReLU's 50.0%
  • Leaky ReLU keeps a small slope everywhere, so it never fully dies

vs A neuron, backpropagated: In micrograd you computed tanh's local derivative for one neuron by hand. What changes when the same rule is applied to 32 × 200 units at once, and why does it suddenly matter?

Check
  • same rule: (1 - t**2) * out.grad
  • now it is an elementwise gate on a whole matrix, so you look at its distribution, not one value
  • at scale, a bad init pushes most entries into the tails at once, and that is what stalls a layer
