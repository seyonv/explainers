BatchNorm: standardise the preactivations

Also called: batch normalization, BN, BatchNorm1d in PyTorch (Ioffe & Szegedy, 2015). Lecture 4 · batch normalization · 0:40:40

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Keeping hidden preactivations roughly unit Gaussian has so far meant picking the right weight scale by hand or by formula. That works for one layer at init, but with many layers the scales interact and nothing holds them in place once training moves the weights.

Where it fits: It comes right after Kaiming init. Instead of choosing weights so that hpreact comes out unit Gaussian, BatchNorm forces it to, on every forward pass, with the mean and standard deviation you met in the toolkit.

  4. What changes about each example's output once its preactivation depends on the other 31 examples in the batch?

In one breath: the last three cards fought to keep the hidden preactivations roughly unit Gaussian by picking the right weight scale. BatchNorm stops negotiating: on every forward pass it takes each hidden unit's 32 values in the minibatch, subtracts their mean and divides by their standard deviation, so every unit starts at mean 0, std 1. Then a learned gain and bias per unit let training move it away from that if it helps. All of this is ordinary differentiable arithmetic, so backprop flows straight through it.

Predict first: you keep the hidden bias b1 and put BatchNorm right after embcat @ W1 + b1. After one loss.backward(), how big is b1.grad?

Zero, up to float rounding: the lab measures a largest entry of 4.4e-10. BN subtracts each column's batch mean, and adding the same b1[j] to all 32 rows of column j raises that mean by exactly b1[j], so the subtraction removes it again. Nothing downstream depends on b1, so its gradient is 0. That's why the BN version deletes b1 and lets bnbias do its job.

Why keep a learned gain and bias?

If BN forced every unit to stay at mean 0, std 1 forever, the net could never make a unit sharper, flatter, or shifted to one side, even when that lowers the loss. bngain and bnbias start at "do nothing" (1 and 0) and let backprop decide. After the full 200,000-step run, the lab reads them back:

learned bngain: mean 1.279, range 0.20 .. 3.27 learned bnbias: range −0.88 .. +1.64 parameters: 11,897 (Kaiming MLP) − 200 (b1) + 400 (bngain, bnbias) = 12,097

Some units ended up 3× wider than unit std, others squeezed to 0.2×. The net used the freedom.

g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(2147483647)
C  = torch.randn((27, 10), generator=g)
W1 = torch.randn((30, 200), generator=g) * (5/3)/(30**0.5)
# no b1: BN would subtract it right back out
W2 = torch.randn((200, 27), generator=g) * 0.01
b2 = torch.randn(27, generator=g) * 0
bngain = torch.ones((1, 200))
bnbias = torch.zeros((1, 200))
parameters = [C, W1, W2, b2, bngain, bnbias]
# in the training loop, between the linear layer and tanh:
hpreact = embcat @ W1
bnmeani = hpreact.mean(0, keepdim=True)
bnstdi  = hpreact.std(0, keepdim=True)
hpreact = bngain * (hpreact - bnmeani) / bnstdi + bnbias
h = torch.tanh(hpreact)

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Hand-tune each weight scale

The W1 *= 0.2 fix worked for one hidden layer. With dozens of layers, every scale interacts with every other, and there is no way to tune them all by hand.

✗ Kaiming init alone

Gain / √fan_in gives a good scale for random weights at step 0, but nothing holds it there once training starts changing the weights.

✗ Standardise, with no gain or bias

Every unit is pinned to mean 0, std 1 for all of training. The net can't learn that a unit should be wider or offset; the learned gains here range from 0.20 to 3.27.

✓ Standardise, then learn γ and β

Unit-Gaussian preactivations on every forward pass, whatever the weights do, plus a per-unit escape hatch. Because it's differentiable, it's just another layer to backprop through.

Is BatchNorm always worth it?

Not here. On this one-hidden-layer MLP it doesn't help: val 2.1057 with BN against 2.1070 with Kaiming init alone (both measured on your M3; the notebook's loss log says 2.1048 for BN, which the lab couldn't reproduce). Its payoff is in deep nets, where no hand-picked scale survives many layers (the activation-gradient-plots card shows how its layers stay evenly scaled).

It also has a real cost: each example's output now depends on the other 31 in its batch. That makes single-example inference impossible without extra work (the next card), and it's a source of subtle bugs. Karpathy himself says he'd avoid it where he can. Transformers use LayerNorm instead, which standardises across the units of one example, not across the batch (z2h-8).

Understand and remember

  • Unit-Gaussian preactivations are what the init fixes were aiming for → mean and std are differentiable → just compute them per column over the batch and standardise → every unit starts centred with std 1 on every forward pass, whatever the weights are
  • Standardising alone pins every unit to mean 0, std 1 forever → the net can't make a unit wider, narrower or offset → a learned per-unit gain γ and bias β (starting at 1 and 0) give that freedom back → trained values here range 0.20..3.27
  • BN subtracts each column's batch mean → any constant added to the column (a bias b1) is subtracted right back out → b1 has no effect and a zero gradient (4.4e-10) → drop it and let β play its role
  • Statistics come from the batch → each example's output depends on its 31 batchmates → this acts as a regulariser, but a single example can't be normalised on its own → inference needs fixed statistics (the next card)
  • detail that matters Standardisation divides out any scale of W1 → multiplying W1 by 10 leaves the step-0 loss at 3.3239 → the forward pass becomes insensitive to the init gain, though the size of each update relative to the weights still changes (the update:data ratio card)

Reference only

  • hpreact.mean(0, keepdim=True) and hpreact.std(0, keepdim=True), shapes 1 × 200
  • torch.std divides by N − 1; the paper and PyTorch's layer also add ε = 1e-5 inside the square root
  • parameter count 12,097 = 11,897 − 200 + 400
  • the paper: Ioffe & Szegedy 2015

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Kaiming init: gain / √fan_in: Kaiming init and BatchNorm both aim at unit-Gaussian preactivations. What is fundamentally different about how each gets there, and how does each one fail?

Check
  • Kaiming chooses the weight scale (gain / √fan_in) once, before training
  • BN measures and corrects the actual preactivations on every forward pass
  • Kaiming fails when training drifts the weights or when many layers compound small errors
  • BN fails with tiny batches or single examples, and in train/eval mix-ups, because its statistics come from the batch

vs LayerNorm, dropout, and scaling up to 1.48: LayerNorm uses the same subtract-mean, divide-by-std, scale-and-shift recipe. Which axis does each one normalise over, and why does that choice make LayerNorm free of BatchNorm's batch coupling?

Check
  • BatchNorm: per unit, over the examples in the batch (dim 0)
  • LayerNorm: per example, over its own units
  • so a LayerNorm output depends only on that one example: no batch coupling and no running statistics
  • that's why transformers use LayerNorm

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

With BN in place, you multiply W1 by 10 at init (a terrible scale without BN). What step-0 loss do you get, compared with 3.3239?

Reveal

Exactly 3.3239 again (computed: the standardised values differ by at most 9.5e-7). Scaling a column by 10 scales its mean and its std by 10, and the division cancels it. So BN makes the forward pass insensitive to the weight scale. It does not make training insensitive: the gradient on W1 shrinks as W1 grows, so the update:data ratio changes and the learning rate may need retuning (the update-to-data-ratio card).
