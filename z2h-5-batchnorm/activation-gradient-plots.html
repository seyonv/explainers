Activation and gradient histograms

Also called: activation statistics, gradient statistics, forward- and backward-pass diagnostics. Lecture 4 · viz #1: forward pass activations statistics · 1:26:51

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: In a deep stack a bad init scale compounds layer by layer: the signal fades to nothing or piles up at ±1, and the early layers get far more or far less gradient than the late ones. The loss is one number and can't tell you which layer is sick.

Where it fits: It uses the Linear/Tanh classes from the pytorchify card and tests the gain from the Kaiming card on a 5-layer tanh stack instead of one layer. The next card adds the last diagnostic, the update:data ratio.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why does gain 0.5 halve both the activations going forward and the gradients going backward, layer after layer?
  2. Gain 3 saturates about 42% of the tanh outputs; why does the gradient still grow toward layer 1 instead of vanishing?
  3. Without the tanh layers, why does gain 5/3 now blow the activations up to std 12.78, while gain 1 holds them at 1?
  4. If six Linear layers collapse into one (30, 27) matrix, what does the tanh actually buy you?
  5. BatchNorm makes the activation histograms identical at gain 1 and 5/3; which statistic still differs, and why does that matter for the learning rate?

In one breath: after one forward and one backward pass, take every tanh layer's outputs and the gradients arriving at them, and look at their spread layer by layer. In a healthy deep net each layer looks roughly the same. If the spread shrinks, grows or piles up at ±1 as you go deeper, the init scale (the gain) is wrong, and you can see it before training for hours.

Predict first: with gain 3, about 42% of the tanh outputs in layers 2–5 sit at ±1, where tanh passes almost no gradient. Is the gradient at layer 1 smaller or larger than at layer 5?

Larger: 1.0e-03 at layer 1 vs 3.1e-04 at layer 5, about 3.2× (measured). Going backward, each layer multiplies the gradient by its weights (made 3× bigger by the gain) and by tanh's gate 1 − t² (which saturation shrinks). At gain 3 the weights win, by about 1.3× per layer. Saturation doesn't make every gradient vanish; it makes the layers lopsided, so the early layers get pushed harder than the late ones.

The fully linear case: remove every tanh

Take the tanh layers out and keep only the six Linears. Now nothing squeezes the signal, so gain 5/3 overshoots: each layer multiplies the spread by about 5/3.

So a linear stack needs gain exactly 1. But then why have layers at all? Matrices multiply into one matrix:

xW1W2⋯W6six Linear layers, no tanh = x(W1W2⋯W6)one (30, 27) matrix
xthe 30 concatenated embedding numbers of one example (emb.view(-1, 30)) Wklayer k's weight matrix: (30, 100), then (100, 100) ×4, then (100, 27)

Checked on your M3: running the six layers vs multiplying x by the one product matrix differs by at most 2.6e-06 (float rounding) on a batch of 32. The stack can only ever compute a linear function; the tanh is what buys expressive power. Karpathy notes the training dynamics of a linear stack still differ from one matrix, even though the function class is the same.

BatchNorm re-standardises every layer's preactivations, so the histograms look the same at either gain (gain 1 reproduces notebook cells 15 and 16 exactly). The gradient scale still moves with the gain (1.3–1.4× bigger at 5/3), which changes how big each update is: that's the next card.

▶ Build itruns on your M3 · about 5 s

When: pause the video at 1:26:51, once your Linear/Tanh classes train. Comment out the BatchNorm layers, stop the loop after the first step, and add the two probes.

for gain in [0.5, 1.0, 5/3, 3.0]:
    # rebuild the no-BN stack, then scale every hidden Linear
    with torch.no_grad():
        for layer in layers[:-1]:
            if isinstance(layer, Linear):
                layer.weight *= gain
    # ... one forward + backward, with layer.out.retain_grad()
    for i, layer in enumerate(layers[:-1]):
        if isinstance(layer, Tanh):
            t, gt = layer.out, layer.out.grad
            sat = (t.abs() > 0.97).float().mean() * 100
            print(i, f'{t.std():.2f} {sat:.2f}% {gt.std():.1e}')
            hy, hx = torch.histogram(t.detach(), density=True)
            plt.plot(hx[:-1], hy)

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Watch only the loss

The loss is one number for the whole net. At gain 0.5 layer 5's activations have std 0.02 and layer 1 gets a 16× smaller gradient, and nothing in the step-0 loss says which layer is sick.

✗ One global gradient norm

Averaging over all layers hides the shape. Gain 3's gradients are 3.2× larger at layer 1 than at layer 5; a single norm would just look "a bit big".

✗ Train and hope

A shallow net forgives a bad gain, but in a deep stack the factor compounds: 0.5 per layer is 0.5⁴ = 1/16 after four layers. You find out hours later, from a loss that never drops.

✓ Per-layer histograms of t and t.grad

One forward and one backward pass, a few seconds. Similar std, few saturated values and similar gradient spread in every layer tells you the scales are right before you spend any compute.

Is gain 5/3 the answer, and do you still need these plots?

These are step-0 snapshots of one batch. They say the init is sane; they don't keep it sane once training moves the weights, which is why the lecture keeps plotting during training (and adds the update:data ratio next). 5/3 is PyTorch's tanh gain; Karpathy says in viz #1 that he isn't sure where it comes from, and these plots are how you check it. A neighbouring gain like 1.5 looks nearly as good (computed: layer-5 std 0.61, 1.94% saturated).

With BatchNorm, LayerNorm or residual connections, the forward histograms become boring by design, and a modern net mostly needs them for debugging. They still pay off when something goes wrong: a layer whose std collapses, or a gradient that is 10× off its neighbours, points straight at the bug.

Understand and remember

  • Each Linear multiplies the spread by about the gain (after 1/√fan_in) → each tanh squeezes it back → gain too small (0.5) and the squeeze wins: std 0.41 → 0.02 by layer 5; gain too big (3) and tanh saturates (about 42%); 5/3 roughly balances the squeeze → std settles near 0.66
  • The backward pass runs the same layers in reverse → each step back multiplies the gradient by the (gained) weights and by the tanh gate 1 − t² → a per-layer factor off 1 compounds → layer-1 vs layer-5 gradient: 0.061 at gain 0.5, 3.2 at gain 3, about 1.35 at 5/3 → layers learn at very different speeds unless the ratio is near 1
  • Remove the tanh → nothing squeezes → the right gain is exactly 1 (5/3 grows std ×5/3 per layer to 12.78) → but a product of matrices is one matrix → the stack can only compute a linear function → the nonlinearity is what makes depth worth having
  • BatchNorm re-standardises every preactivation → activation std (0.63–0.65) and saturation (about 2%) stop depending on the gain → but the gradient scale still moves with it (2.0–2.6e-03 at gain 1 vs 2.6–3.7e-03 at 5/3) → the update size, and so the lr, still needs checking
  • detail that matters layer.out is a non-leaf tensor → PyTorch frees its .grad after backward unless you call retain_grad() → without it the gradient histogram is empty; with it you pay memory, so it's debug-only (the notebook marks it AFTER_DEBUG)

Reference only

  • the 0.97 saturation threshold (a convention; the saturated-tanh card used 0.99)
  • the plotting calls: torch.histogram(t, density=True) and plt.plot(hx[:-1], hy)
  • the tanh layers sit at list positions 1, 3, 5, 7, 9 without BN and 2, 5, 8, 11, 14 with BN
  • torch.nn.init.calculate_gain('tanh') = 5/3
  • the net's shape: n_embd 10, 100 hidden units, 5 hidden layers, output layer shrunk ×0.1

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain why you look at each layer's statistics separately instead of at the loss or one overall gradient norm.
    Check
    • the problem is how scales change from layer to layer, and a single number averages that away
    • the loss can look fine while one layer's activations have collapsed (gain 0.5: std 0.02 at layer 5)
    • a global norm hides lopsidedness (gain 3: layer 1's gradient 3.2× layer 5's)
  2. mechanism Walk the gradient back from tanh layer 5 to tanh layer 1 at gain 0.5. What multiplies it at each step, and what is it at layer 1 if it is 3.1e-04 at layer 5?
    Check
    • each step back multiplies by the next Linear's weights (scaled by 0.5) and by the tanh gate 1 − t², which is close to 1 here because the activations are small
    • so the gradient roughly halves per step
    • four steps: 3.1e-04 · 0.5⁴ ≈ 1.9e-05, which is what the lab measures
  3. tradeoff You have to choose between gain 1.5 and 5/3 for this stack. What does each give you, and which statistic would you use to decide?
    Check
    • 1.5: layer-5 std 0.61, 1.94% saturated, layer-1/layer-5 gradient 1.17
    • 5/3: layer-5 std 0.66, 6.13% saturated, gradient ratio 1.38
    • flatter gradients favour 1.5; stronger, steadier activations favour 5/3
    • both are fine; the plots rule out 0.5 and 3, not the fine tuning
  4. tradeoff Someone removes all the tanh layers to 'make training easier' and keeps gain 5/3. What will the plots show, and what have they given up even if they fix the gain?
    Check
    • activations grow about ×5/3 per layer (to std 12.78) and gradients grow going back (2.6e-03 vs 3.2e-04)
    • fixing it needs gain exactly 1
    • even then six Linears are one (30, 27) matrix, so the model is linear and loses what depth offers

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Kaiming init: gain / √fan_in: The Kaiming card derives a gain on paper; this card measures one in a deep stack. What does each tell you that the other can't, and how does each fail?

Check
  • Kaiming: a rule from the layer's shape and nonlinearity, before any code runs
  • the plots: whether the whole stack is actually healthy, including the compounding across layers
  • Kaiming assumes independent zero-mean inputs, true only at init; the plots show what really happens
  • the plots are one batch at one moment; they can't tell you where training drifts later

vs The update:data ratio (≈ 1e-3): Both are diagnostics for the same net. Which question does each answer, and why can the histograms look perfect while the update ratio says the lr is wrong?

Check
  • histograms: are activations and gradients well scaled through depth?
  • update:data: is each step changing the weights by a sensible fraction (about 1e-3)?
  • with BN the forward histograms are gain-proof, but the gradient scale, and so the update size, still depends on the gain and the lr

vs Fix 2: tanh units stuck at ±1: The saturated-tanh card fixed one hidden layer by staring at a histogram of h. What changes when the same idea is applied to five stacked layers?

Check
  • same signal: a histogram piled at ±1 means a tiny gate 1 − t²
  • with depth, a per-layer factor compounds, so you compare layers, not just look at one
  • you also need the backward view, because gradient scale can drift even when activations look steady
