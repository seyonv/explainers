Kaiming init: gain / √fan_in

Also called: He initialisation, fan_in scaling, variance-preserving init, kaiming_normal_. Lecture 4 · calculating the init scale: "Kaiming init" · 0:27:53

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Fix 2 tamed the saturated tanh layer with W1*0.2, a number found by staring at histograms. With more layers, different widths or a different nonlinearity you would need a new magic number for every layer, and a deep stack whose scales are slightly off either explodes or fades to nothing.

Where it fits: It replaces the hand-tuned fix from the saturated-tanh card with a rule. It leans on the variance idea from the toolkit's mean-and-variance card, and it sets up BatchNorm, the next card, which removes the need to get this rule exactly right.

In one breath: a layer's output is a sum of fan_in products, so if each weight has std 1 the output's spread grows by √fan_in, and a deep stack blows up or dies. Draw the weights with std gain / √fan_in instead, where the gain undoes what the nonlinearity squeezes away; for the MLP's tanh layer that is (5/3)/√30 = 0.3043, a formula in place of the hand-tuned 0.2.

Predict first: x is 1000 rows of 10 unit-Gaussian numbers and w is a 10×200 unit-Gaussian matrix. What is the std of y = x @ w: about 1, about 10, or something else?

About 3.16, which is √10. Each output adds up 10 products, and each product has variance 1, so the variances add to 10 and the std is √10 = 3.162. Your M3 measures 3.161 (Karpathy sees "about 3" at ~0:29:37). Neither the mean nor the size of the numbers moves; only the spread grows.

Why the spread grows by √fan_in

One output of a linear layer is y = x1w1 + … + xnwn. The variance (Var, the average squared distance from the mean; std is its square root) of a sum of independent zero-mean terms is the sum of their variances, and each product xiwi has variance Var(x)·Var(w).

Var(y)=nin·Var(w)·Var(x)the variances of fan_in products add up ⇒ std(w)=gainnincancel fan_in, then undo the nonlinearity
xthe layer's input (the 30 concatenated embedding numbers, embcat, for W1)std ≈ 1 wone column of the weight matrixrandn: std 1 ninfan_in: how many inputs feed each output unit (rows of W)toy 10 · W1 30 gaina per-nonlinearity factor that makes up for what the activation squashes1 · √2 · 5/3

With gain 1, std(w) = 1/√nin gives Var(y) = nin · (1/nin) · 1 = 1: the output keeps the input's spread. This assumes zero-mean, independent inputs and weights, which holds at init, not later in training.

The gain: make up for the nonlinearity

1/√fan_in keeps a linear layer's output at std 1. A nonlinearity after it changes the spread again, so He et al. (2015) multiply by a gain. PyTorch's torch.nn.init.calculate_gain gives these values:

NonlinearityGainWhyChecked on your M3
none (linear)1.0000nothing to undo5 linear layers, gain 1: act std stays 0.97–1.01

tanh1.6667 = 5/3squashes: a std-1 input comes out with std 0.628 (computed)5 tanh layers: act std 0.75 … 0.66 with 5/3, but 0.62 … 0.32 with gain 1

The deep-stack rows are the activation-gradient-plots lab (five Linear → tanh layers of 100 units, at init). 5/3 is PyTorch's choice for tanh; it keeps the stack steady but is a practical constant rather than an exact derivation.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Plain randn (std 1)

Every layer multiplies the spread by √fan_in. For W1 that is √30 = 5.48, so hpreact std is 5.181 and 61.0% of the tanh units start stuck at ±1.

✗ A hand-tuned magic number

W1*0.2 works here (val 2.1027), but it was found by staring at histograms. Change the width, the context length or the depth, and you tune again, layer by layer.

✗ 1/√fan_in with no gain

Right for a linear layer, too small before tanh: each tanh squeezes the spread, so a 5-layer tanh stack at gain 1 fades from std 0.62 to 0.32 by layer 5, and the signal fades with it.

✓ gain / √fan_in

One rule for every layer, read off the layer's shape and its nonlinearity. The 5-layer tanh stack holds std 0.75 → 0.66, and W1 gets 0.3043 without a single experiment.

Does Kaiming init still matter, and is kaiming_normal_ the same thing?

It only sets the statistics at step 0; nothing keeps them there once training moves the weights. Residual connections, normalisation layers (BatchNorm, next card; LayerNorm) and optimisers like Adam made exact init far less critical, and Karpathy's own habit is simply to divide by √fan_in. For a one-hidden-layer MLP it makes no measurable difference (2.1070 vs 2.1027); it earns its keep in deep stacks.

Watch the weight layout if you call PyTorch's helper. nn.Linear stores its weight as (out, in), so kaiming_normal_ reads fan_in from dimension 1. On Karpathy's (30, 200) W1 it would use fan_in 200 and give std 0.1165 instead of 0.3043 (computed). And nn.Linear's own default is not Kaiming-tanh: it draws uniform in ±1/√fan_in, a std of 0.1051 for 30 inputs (measured).

Understand and remember

  • A unit's output is a sum of fan_in products → variances of independent zero-mean terms add → Var(y) = fan_in · Var(w) · Var(x) → with std-1 weights the spread grows √fan_in per layer (3.161 for fan_in 10)
  • Divide the weights by √fan_in → the fan_in cancels → Var(y) = Var(x) → the signal keeps its size layer after layer instead of multiplying by √fan_in each time (3.16⁵ ≈ 316× over five layers)
  • A nonlinearity changes the spread after the linear layer (ReLU halves the mean square, tanh squashes std 1 to 0.628) → multiply by a gain to put it back (√2, 5/3) → a 5-layer tanh stack holds std 0.75 → 0.66 instead of fading 0.62 → 0.32 at gain 1
  • Init scale sets hpreact's spread → too big saturates tanh (W1 × 1: 61.0% at ±1) → gain/√fan_in gives 0.3043 for W1 with no tuning → same result as the hand-tuned 0.2 (val 2.1070 vs 2.1027), but it carries to any layer shape
  • detail that matters The rule only holds at step 0 (independent, zero-mean weights) → training breaks the assumption → deep nets also need normalisation (BatchNorm), residual connections or Adam to keep the statistics healthy
  • detail that matters PyTorch stores nn.Linear weights as (out, in) → kaiming_normal_ reads fan_in from dimension 1 → on a (30, 200) in-by-out matrix it uses fan_in 200 and gives std 0.1165 instead of 0.3043

Reference only

  • torch.nn.init.calculate_gain('tanh') = 5/3, ('relu') = √2, ('linear') = 1
  • kaiming_normal_(w, mode='fan_in', nonlinearity='tanh') and its fan_out mode
  • nn.Linear's default: uniform in ±1/√fan_in (std 0.1051 for 30 inputs)
  • He et al. 2015, arXiv 1502.01852

vs BatchNorm: standardise the preactivations: Kaiming init sets the preactivations' scale once, at step 0; BatchNorm standardises them on every forward pass. Which design choice makes BatchNorm robust to a bad init, and what does it cost you that Kaiming doesn't?

Check
  • BN measures the batch's actual mean and std and divides them out, so any init scale is corrected
  • Kaiming only predicts the scale from shapes and never re-checks
  • BN couples the examples in a batch and needs running statistics at test time
  • Kaiming costs nothing at run time but drifts once the weights train

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

You swap tanh for ReLU in the MLP and keep fan_in 30. Which W1 scale should you use, and if you forgot to change the gain from 5/3, would ReLU units saturate the way tanh did?

Reveal

√2/√30 = 1.4142/5.477 = 0.2582. Leaving 5/3 makes the preactivations about 18% too big (1.667/1.414 = 1.18), but ReLU has no upper flat region, so nothing saturates at the top: outputs just grow, and in a deep stack they compound by about 1.18 per layer. ReLU's risk is the other side: units whose preactivation is negative for every example get zero gradient (the dead-neurons card).
