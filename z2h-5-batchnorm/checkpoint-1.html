Checkpoint 1: from a bad init to a principled scale

Ties together the loss you should see at step 0, fix 1: the humble output layer, fix 2: saturated tanh, dead neurons and Kaiming init, with softmax, tanh's backward rule and variance from earlier courses. Lecture 4, 0:04:19 to 0:40:40.

What this ties together, and why now. The first half of Lecture 4 is one argument told in five cards: the scale of the random numbers you start from decides whether training can start well. Too-big output weights make softmax confidently wrong, and the step-0 loss shows it. Too-big hidden weights push tanh into its flat tails, and only the activations show it. Flat parts of any nonlinearity block the gradient, and ReLU's can block it for good. Kaiming init turns all of that into one rule, gain / √fan_in. BatchNorm comes next and replaces this careful balancing, so check now that you can do the balancing by hand. BatchNorm makes more sense once you know what it replaces.

Comes back from earlier courses: the loss is cross-entropy from z2h-4 and softmax from the toolkit. The gate 1 − t² is the tanh backward rule you wrote in a neuron, backpropagated. The √fan_in growth is "variances add" from the toolkit. The net is the z2h-4 MLP (block 3, embedding 10, hidden 200).

How to use this page. Close the cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before you open its Check. The questions are mixed on purpose and don't say which card they come from, so part of the work is choosing the idea. A wrong or shaky answer points to the exact section to reopen.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. x holds 1000 rows of 10 unit-Gaussian numbers and w is a 10 × 200 unit-Gaussian matrix. Predict the std of y = x @ w. Then give the scale for the MLP's W1 (fan_in 30, tanh after it), and say what the 5/3 is there for.
    Check
    • each output sums 10 products of variance 1, and the variances of independent terms add: Var(y) = 10, std = √10 = 3.162 (measured 3.161)
    • so dividing w by √fan_in keeps std 1: w / sqrt(10) gives 1.000
    • W1: fan_in = 10 · 3 = 30, scale = (5/3)/√30 = 1.667 / 5.477 = 0.3043
    • 5/3 is tanh's gain. tanh squeezes its input (a std-1 input comes out with std 0.628), and the gain pushes back so a deep stack neither fades nor blows up

    If you missed it, reopen Kaiming init, "Why the spread grows by √fan_in" and "The gain: make up for the nonlinearity", and the toolkit's mean, variance and the Gaussian, "Add random numbers, and their variances add".

  2. A tanh unit outputs t = 0.99 and a gradient of 1.0 arrives at its output. How much reaches hpreact? What if the unit were a ReLU with hpreact = −2? And for the micrograd neuron with o = 0.7071, what was the same factor?
    Check
    • tanh backward multiplies by 1 − t²: 1 − 0.99² = 0.0199, so about 2% gets through
    • ReLU: the local gradient is exactly 0 for any negative input, so nothing gets through
    • micrograd: 1 − 0.7071² = 0.5, the same rule on one scalar; here it runs on 32 × 200 units at once, so you look at its distribution
    • W1 and b1 only get gradient through hpreact.grad, so a closed gate stops them learning, however big h.grad is

    If you missed it, reopen fix 2: saturated tanh, "On the way back, tanh is a gate", dead neurons, "What the nonlinearity does to the gradient", and a neuron, backpropagated, "How much tanh lets through".

  3. debugYou rebuild the z2h-4 MLP with every parameter drawn by plain torch.randn, and step 0 prints a loss of 27.8817 on 27 characters. What should it print? What is wrong, which line of the init causes it, and why does zeroing only b2 not fix it?
    Check
    • a model that knows nothing gives every character 1/27: loss −ln(1/27) = ln 27 = 3.2958
    • 27.88 means confidently wrong: the logits have std 12.75, softmax puts 0.894 on its favourite character, and the right one gets a median probability of 6.08e-13
    • the cause is the output layer: each logit sums 200 terms h·W2, so std ≈ √(200 · 0.846 + 1) = √170.2 = 13.0
    • b2 has std 1 next to that 13, so zeroing only b2 still gives 27.9729. The fix is W2 × 0.01 and b2 × 0, which gives 3.3221

    If you missed it, reopen the loss you should see at step 0, "The starter MLP at step 0", and fix 1, "Where the 27 comes from".

  4. Setting W2 to exactly 0 gives the perfect step-0 loss of 3.2958. Why does the lecture use × 0.01 instead? And why is setting every weight and bias to zero a much worse idea than zeroing W2?
    Check
    • the gradient into h is dlogits @ W2ᵀ, so with W2 = 0 no gradient reaches W1 or C on step 0 (the largest W1.grad is 0.0)
    • × 0.01 costs almost nothing in loss (3.3221 vs 3.2958), and gradients flow from the first step
    • in this net zero W2 recovers after one update (after 20,000 steps, val 2.2073 vs 2.2065), so zeroing W2 alone is harmless here; small-but-random is simply the safe habit
    • with all weights equal, every hidden unit computes the same thing and gets the same gradient, so the units never become different. Karpathy leaves this as exercise E01, where the net "trains but only partially"

    If you missed it, reopen fix 1, "Why not exactly zero? And when is this not enough?", and the loss you should see at step 0, the "Set all the weights to zero" cell.

  5. which idea?A colleague's 27-class MLP prints 3.31 at step 0, so they say the init is fine. Training is sluggish, and plt.hist(h.view(-1).tolist(), 50) shows two tall spikes at −1 and +1. Which idea explains how both can be true, and which two numbers would you print to settle it?
    Check
    • saturated tanh: the step-0 loss only reads the output layer. In the lecture's net, fix 1 alone gives 3.3221 while 61.0% of the tanh outputs are beyond ±0.99
    • print the saturated share ((h.abs() > 0.99).float().mean()) and the mean gate ((1 - h**2).mean()): 61.0% and 0.154 before fix 2, 1.0% and 0.605 after
    • check plt.imshow(h.abs() > 0.99) for all-white columns: those units are dead. The lecture's net has 0 of 200, so it was slowed, not broken
    • fix: shrink W1 (× 0.2 by hand, or Kaiming's 0.3043), and b1 × 0.01. Dev loss goes from 2.1311 to 2.1027

    If you missed it, reopen fix 2: saturated tanh, "What the net looks like at step 0" and "Is any saturation a bug?".

  6. Four classes, the correct one is class 0. Compute the loss for logits [0, 0, 0, 0] and for [0, 0, 5, 0]. Then say why random logits with std 10 average a loss of about 10, and with std 100 about 103.
    Check
    • equal logits give the uniform 1/4: loss ln 4 = 1.3863, the know-nothing cost for 4 classes
    • [0, 0, 5, 0]: e5 = 148.41, so p(class 0) = 1/151.41 = 0.0066 and the loss is 5.0200, 3.6× the uniform cost
    • big random logits make softmax confident about a class it has no reason to prefer, and cross-entropy charges roughly the gap between the top logit and the correct one: 10.3656 at ×10, 103.0973 at ×100. The loss has no ceiling
    • only the spread matters: adding the same constant to every logit leaves softmax unchanged, while multiplying the logits sharpens it

    If you missed it, reopen the loss you should see at step 0, "Karpathy's 4-class toy: confidence is what costs", and the toolkit's softmax and cross-entropy, "Scale stretches it, shift does nothing".

  7. which idea?You train a 200-unit ReLU MLP. A typo sets lr to 100 for a single step, and then you go back to lr 0.1. After 1,000 more steps the loss is stuck near 2.85, and 188 of the 200 units are ≤ 0 on every training example. Which idea explains it, why don't those units come back, and which fix from these cards would not have prevented it?
    Check
    • dead neurons: ReLU's local gradient is exactly 0 for x ≤ 0, so a unit that is negative on every example gets exactly 0 gradient on its W1 column
    • the shock step itself killed none (scratch rerun); the huge losses and gradients in the steps after it pushed units below zero (41 dead by the third step, 188 at the end; mean loss 2.8470 vs 2.2806 with no shock)
    • they can come back only if C drifts enough to lift one example above zero, which is rare
    • Kaiming init fixes the init cause, not a too-big step. Leaky ReLU would have kept 1% of the gradient (25 all-negative units after an lr-30 shock, loss 2.4754 vs 2.5262), and a sane lr avoids it

    If you missed it, reopen dead neurons, "From flat to dead" and "So should you always use Leaky ReLU?".

  8. which idea?Your hand-tuned W1 × 0.2 reaches val 2.1027 after 200k steps; Kaiming's 0.3043 reaches 2.1070 and saturates 8.1% of units at init instead of 1.0%. A teammate wants to keep the 0.2. What is the case for Kaiming anyway, and when would it actually matter?
    Check
    • the 0.2 was found by looking at histograms for this one layer. Read as a gain, it is 0.2 · √30 = 1.095, which nobody would have picked from a rule
    • Kaiming reads the scale off the layer's shape and nonlinearity with no experiment: it carries over to a new width, context length or depth, where a magic number has to be re-tuned layer by layer
    • on one hidden layer the difference is noise: 0.004 at 200k steps, and in this checkpoint's build Kaiming is actually ahead at 20k steps (2.1725 vs 2.1793)
    • it matters in deep stacks, where a per-layer error compounds (3.16× per layer is about 316× after five)

    If you missed it, reopen Kaiming init, "Applied to the MLP's W1" and "Why it beats the alternatives".

3 · Build: an init doctor for the one-layer MLP

runs on your M3 · about 10 sWhat it reinforces: the ln V target (step-0 loss), the output scale (fix 1), saturation and the gate (fix 2), dead columns (dead neurons), the √fan_in rule (Kaiming init), and the z2h-4 training loop (lr 0.1, then 0.01). One function reads every symptom from a single batch, before training starts.

In one file, starting from your z2h-4 data code (the random.seed(42) 80/10/10 split):

  1. Four inits. One function init(stage) that reseeds g with 2147483647 and draws C, W1, b1, W2, b2 in that order, with the scales of each stage: original (all × 1), fix-logits (W2 × 0.01, b2 × 0), fix-tanh (plus W1 × 0.2, b1 × 0.01), kaiming (W1 × (5/3)/√30 instead of 0.2).
  2. The doctor. diagnose(P) draws one batch of 32 and, under torch.no_grad(), prints: the loss next to ln 27, the logit std, the hpreact std, the share of |h| > 0.99, the mean of 1 − h², and the number of all-saturated columns.
  3. Predict first. Before running, write down which numbers each stage should move, and which it shouldn't.
  4. Train each for 20,000 steps. Use the z2h-4 loop: batch 32, lr 0.1 for the first 10,000 steps, then 0.01. Print train and val loss.
  5. Explain the table. For each row, name the one number that changed and the card that predicted it.
