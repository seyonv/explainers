The update:data ratio (≈ 1e-3)

Also called: update-to-data ratio, relative update size, ud. Lecture 4 · viz #4: update:data ratio over time · 1:39:55

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The activation and gradient histograms can look healthy while training is still 100× too slow, or while one layer races ahead of the others. Nothing on those plots tells you whether the learning rate fits the weights it is moving.

Where it fits: It is the last of the lecture's diagnostic plots, after the activation and gradient histograms. It reuses the learning rate from the lr-finder card and the 6-layer net from the pytorchify card, and it shows what BatchNorm does not fix.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Why is the grad:data ratio alone not enough to judge a learning rate, and what does multiplying by lr add?
  2. Why does changing lr shift every ud curve by the same amount on a log10 scale?
  3. Why does the last layer's curve start far above the others in the no-BN net, and why does it settle?
  4. If BatchNorm makes the forward pass ignore a weight matrix's scale, why does shrinking that matrix make its updates relatively bigger?
  5. When would you trust the −3 target less: which parameters or optimisers break the assumption behind it?

In one breath: for each weight matrix, divide the size of the step you just took (lr·grad) by the size of the weights themselves, and track the log10 of that every step. If it sits near −3, each step changes the weights by about a thousandth of their size; far below means training crawls, far above means the weights thrash, and one outlying layer means that layer trains at a different speed from the rest.

Predict first: in the BatchNorm net, you multiply every hidden weight matrix by 0.2 at init. BatchNorm standardises what comes out, so the activations don't change at all. Does the update:data ratio go up, go down, or stay put?

It goes up by 25×, from log10 −2.81 to −1.41 for the first layer at step 0 (a shift of +1.40 = log10 25). BatchNorm makes the loss blind to the weights' scale, so the gradient grows 5× when the weights shrink 5×, and the ratio gets both factors. Karpathy only says the ratios "change" (1:46:04); the lab measures how much.

The number you track

After the update p.data += -lr * p.grad, compare two spreads for each parameter tensor p: the standard deviation (std, the typical distance from the mean) of the step, and the std of the weights. Karpathy logs the ratio on a log10 scale, so −3 means 10−3, one thousandth.

first (100,100) W after 1001 steps (BN, gain 1): grad:data = std(grad) / std(data) = 7.13e-3 / 0.1024 = 6.96e-2 ud = log10(lr · grad:data) = log10(0.1 · 6.96e-2) = −1 + log10(6.96e-2) = −1 − 1.157 = −2.157 (tracked: −2.14)

The grad:data ratio is Karpathy's viz #3 (1:36:15): it ignores lr, so it can't tell you whether the step is sensible. Multiplying by lr turns it into the size of the step itself. Since log10(lr) just adds on, lr 0.1 → 0.001 moves every curve down by exactly 2.

Why BatchNorm moves the ratio: the 1/c² rule

With BatchNorm right after a Linear layer, multiplying that layer's weights by any c > 0 changes nothing downstream, because BN divides the scale back out. So the loss is the same, the gradient with respect to the scaled weights shrinks by c, and the weights' std grows by c:

How it differs from related diagnostics

✗ Watching the loss curve

One number for the whole net. At lr 0.001 it just looks slow (3.243 after 1000 steps); it can't say which layer is the problem or by how much to change lr.

✗ Activation histograms

They check the forward pass. With BN and gain 0.2 they look healthy (1.6% saturated), while the updates are 25× too big. The problem is invisible there.

✗ The grad:data ratio

A snapshot without lr: 6.96e-2 for a hidden W means nothing until you know you'll multiply it by 0.1 or by 0.001. It also shows one moment, not the trend.

✓ update:data over time

Measures what training actually does to each matrix, per step, in one unit for every layer. You read off both the lr to try (each 10× moves the curves by 1) and which layer is an outlier.

Is −3 a law?

No. It's Karpathy's rough rule of thumb for plain SGD on nets like this one, and it's meant for spotting settings that are off by 10× or more, not for fine tuning. The video's net sits around −2.5, which he calls "a little on the higher side", and it trains fine. The final quality still has to be judged by the dev loss.

It is less useful in two cases. For 1-D parameters (biases, BN's γ and β) the std of the data can be near zero, so the ratio is noisy; the plot leaves them out. And under Adam (z2h-8), each step is normalised per weight, so the ratio mostly reflects lr divided by the weights' size: in a check on this net, Adam's first step at lr 1e-3 gave exactly −2.00 on every (100,100) weight (computed in scratch, not in the lab). Watch the trend there rather than aiming for −3.

Understand and remember

  • Each step moves p by lr·grad → what matters is that step relative to p's own size → std(lr·grad)/std(data) on log10 → about −3 means each step changes the weights by about 1/1000 (the notebook: −2.1 to −2.3 for hidden weights)
  • log10(lr·x) = log10(lr) + log10(x) → lr ÷ 100 shifts every curve down by exactly 2 (step 0: −2.81 → −4.81) → ud near −4.5 means training crawls (loss 3.243 vs 2.451 after 1000 steps) → raise lr until the curves sit near −3
  • BN after a Linear divides out the weights' scale → loss unchanged when W → c·W → grad shrinks by c while std(W) grows by c → ud moves by −2·log10 c (gain 0.2: +1.40; no fan_in: −1.48 and −2.00) → a new init under BN needs a new lr even though the activations look the same
  • A layer with very small weights (the last layer shrunk ×0.1) has a tiny std(data) → its ratio starts far higher than the rest (−0.63 vs about −3.3 at step 0, no BN) → it trains much faster at first until its weights grow and it settles (−1.49)
  • detail that matters The ratio is computed after p.data += -lr*p.grad under torch.no_grad() → it measures the step actually applied, and tracking it adds no gradient graph → it is cheap enough to log every step

Reference only

  • ud.append([((lr*p.grad).std() / p.data.std()).log10().item() for p in parameters])

  1. concept Explain why the update:data ratio is a better guide to the learning rate than the grad:data ratio Karpathy printed first.
    Check
    • grad:data ignores lr, so the same 6.96e-2 is a fine step at lr 0.1 and a tiny one at lr 0.001
    • multiplying by lr gives the step actually applied, relative to the weights
    • tracking it over time shows the trend, not one snapshot
  2. mechanism Walk through what happens to one (100,100) weight's ud when you rebuild the BN net without the / fan_in**0.5 in Linear, keep lr 0.1, and then raise lr to 1.0.
    Check
    • the weights are 10× bigger (c = √100)
    • BN divides the scale out, so the forward pass and loss are unchanged
    • the gradient is 10× smaller and std(data) 10× bigger, so ud drops by 2 (−2.56 → −4.56 at step 0)
    • lr × 10 adds 1 back (−3.56); after 1000 steps the hidden weights sit at −2.87 to −2.96
  3. tradeoff Your BN net's hidden ud sits at −1.7 after you set gain 0.2, and the activation histograms look perfect (1.6% saturated). Would you lower lr, change the gain back, or leave it? What does each choice cost?
    Check
    • the updates are about 25× larger than with gain 1, so training is on the fast, risky side
    • lowering lr by about 10× brings ud near −2.7 but also slows C, which isn't scaled (it sits at −3.15)
    • restoring gain 1 fixes all hidden layers at once without touching C's step
    • leaving it may still train (loss 2.481 after 1000 steps vs 2.451), but it has less margin
  4. mechanism In the no-BN, gain-5/3 net, the last layer's ud is −0.63 at step 0 while the others are around −3.3. Explain the cause and whether you need to fix it.
    Check
    • the last weight was multiplied by 0.1 to make the output humble, so its std(data) is tiny
    • its gradient isn't tiny, so the ratio is large
    • it trains fast at first, its weights grow, and it settles (−1.49 by step 1000)
    • it's expected from the humble-output init, so watch it rather than fix it

Why this step?

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Activation and gradient histograms: The histograms and the update:data plot both diagnose a deep net. What does each one measure, which design choice makes BN fool one of them, and when would you look at each first?

Check
  • histograms: the forward activations and backward gradients at one moment
  • update:data: the step size relative to each weight, over time
  • BN standardises the activations, so a bad weight scale disappears from the histograms but still shows in ud (gain 0.2: 1.6% saturated, ud −1.7)
  • histograms first after changing the architecture or init; ud first when choosing or changing lr

vs Finding the learning rate, then decaying it: The lr finder sweeps lr and reads the loss; the update:data plot reads per-layer step sizes at one lr. What is fundamentally different, and how does each fail?

Check
  • the finder needs a sweep of short runs and gives one global lr
  • ud needs one run and shows every layer separately, so it catches an outlying layer
  • the finder fails when the loss is too noisy to show a valley
  • ud fails when −3 is the wrong target (Adam, 1-D parameters), and it can't tell you the final dev loss

vs Adam and AdamW, explained: Plain SGD's step is lr·grad; Adam's step is lr times a per-weight normalised gradient. How does that change what the update:data plot tells you, and the 1/c² rule under BN?

Check
  • under Adam the step size is roughly lr per weight, whatever the gradient's scale
  • so ud mostly reflects lr divided by the weights' std
  • BN's 1/c² becomes about 1/c: the gradient's 1/c is normalised away, only the data's c remains
  • the plot still flags layers far from the rest, but −3 is no longer the natural target

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Switch the BN net to torch.optim.Adam with lr 1e-3. On the very first step, what ud do you predict for a (100,100) weight with std 0.1, and does gain 0.2 still raise it by 25×?

Reveal

About −2.00: Adam's first step moves each weight by exactly lr·sign(grad), so std(step) ≈ 1e-3 and log10(1e-3 / 0.1) = −2. With gain 0.2 the weights' std is about 0.02, so ud ≈ log10(1e-3 / 0.02) = −1.30: only 5× higher, not 25×, because Adam cancels the gradient's growth. A check on this net measured −2.00 and −1.30 exactly (run in scratch, not in the lab).
