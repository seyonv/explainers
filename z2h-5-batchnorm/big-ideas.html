Big idea 1: A step-0 loss far above ln 27 means the init is confidently wrong

A net that knows nothing should spread its bets evenly and score −ln(1/27) = 3.2958. The starter MLP scores 27.88, because its random output layer makes huge logits that bet big on the wrong character. Shrink W2, zero b2, and the wasted first steps disappear.

“confidently wrong” · Karpathy, 0:04:19

Why it matters: The same check opens every later training run: GPT-2 should start near ln 50,257 = 10.82 (the 10.82 sanity check). One forward pass catches an over-confident output layer before any compute is spent.

Big idea 2: A tanh stuck at ±1 passes almost no gradient back

On the way back, tanh multiplies the gradient by 1 − t². After fix 1, 61% of the hidden outputs sit beyond ±0.99, where that gate lets through under 2%. The loss can't see this; a histogram of h can.

“permanent brain damage” · Karpathy, 0:12:59

Why it matters: In a deep stack these gates multiply layer after layer, and a ReLU unit pushed below zero on every example gets exactly 0 for good (one lr-100 step killed 188 of 200 in the dead-neurons lab). Every later net guards the scale of its preactivations for this reason.

Big idea 3: Scale weights by gain / √fan_in and the spread survives each layer

A unit sums fan_in products, so std-1 weights grow the spread by √fan_in at every layer. Divide by √fan_in to hold it at 1, and multiply by a gain for what the nonlinearity squeezes: 5/3 for tanh. W1 gets (5/3)/√30 = 0.3043 instead of a hand-tuned 0.2.

“normalize my weights by the square root of the fan_in” · Karpathy, 0:27:53

Why it matters: The same variance argument returns in attention, where scores are divided by √head_size (why divide by √head_size). It only sets step 0, though: nothing holds the scale once training moves the weights. That gap is what BatchNorm fills.

Big idea 4: If you want unit-Gaussian preactivations, just standardise them

BatchNorm takes each hidden unit's 32 values in the batch, subtracts their mean, divides by their std, then lets a learned gain γ and bias β move them. It is plain differentiable arithmetic, so backprop runs straight through it, and the bias before it becomes useless.

if you want unit-Gaussian preactivations, just standardise them: the operation is differentiable · Karpathy, paraphrased, 0:40:40

Why it matters: Karpathy calls it the first layer that made deep nets train reliably, with no per-layer tuning: ResNet repeats weight (bias=False) → BatchNorm → ReLU (the motif). GPT keeps the idea but standardises within each example instead (LayerNorm).

Big idea 5: BatchNorm couples the batch, so test time needs fixed statistics

Batch statistics make one example's output depend on its 31 batchmates, and a batch of one has no std at all. So BatchNorm keeps running averages as buffers, updated without gradients, and switches to them at test time.

“shot myself in the foot with this layer over and over” · Karpathy, 1:14:10

Why it matters: In PyTorch this is model.train() vs model.eval(), and forgetting eval() is a classic bug. The same coupling hides the 3-D BatchNorm bug in the WaveNet lecture, and it is why Transformers use LayerNorm.

Big idea 6: Look inside every layer: activations, gradients, and update:data ≈ 1e-3

Write each layer as a small class that keeps its .out, and one forward and backward pass shows every layer's spread. Healthy looks the same at every depth, and each step lr·grad is about a thousandth of the weights it changes.

“a total balancing act” · Karpathy, 1:32:07

Why it matters: The loss is one number for the whole net; these probes say which layer is sick, and by how much to change the gain or lr, within seconds. WaveNet reuses these layer classes, and the habit of looking inside carries to every bigger model.

Understand and remember

  • The loss is −ln p(correct) → a net that knows nothing should give every character 1/27 → the step-0 target is ln 27 = 3.2958 → std-1 output weights make logits with std 12.75 that bet confidently on wrong characters (27.88) → W2 × 0.01, b2 × 0 gives 3.3221 and removes the hockey stick (val 2.1698 → 2.1311)
  • tanh backward multiplies by 1 − t² → near ±1 almost nothing gets through (0.0099 at t = 0.995) → with std-1 W1, hpreact has std about 5 and 61% of units saturate → shrink W1 so hpreact is about unit-sized → 1% saturated, val 2.1027; a unit saturated on every example is dead
  • A unit sums fan_in products → Var(y) = fan_in · Var(w) · Var(x) → std(w) = 1/√fan_in keeps the spread, and a gain undoes what the nonlinearity squeezes (5/3 for tanh) → W1 = (5/3)/√30 = 0.3043 with nothing tuned (val 2.1070) → but this only fixes step 0
  • Standardise each preactivation column with its batch mean and std, then γ·x̂ + β → unit-scale inputs to every nonlinearity on every step, whatever the weights → but each example now depends on its batchmates → test time needs fixed statistics: running buffers (EMA, momentum 0.001) that match calibration (val 2.1057 vs 2.1056)
  • detail that matters BN divides out a Linear layer's weight scale → loss unchanged when W → c·W, so the gradient shrinks by c while std(W) grows by c → update:data moves by −2·log10 c (gain 0.2: +1.40) → the activation plots look healthy while the step size is 25× off, so the update:data plot is the one that shows it

Reference only

  • the loss log: val 2.1682 → 2.13 → 2.1027 → 2.1070 → 2.1048 (the lab reproduces 2.1057 for BN)
  • torch.nn.init.calculate_gain: linear 1, ReLU √2 = 1.4142, tanh 5/3
  • nn.BatchNorm1d defaults: eps 1e-5, momentum 0.1, affine, track_running_stats
  • the 6-layer stack: 17 layer objects, 47,024 parameters (46,497 without BN)
  • the saturation thresholds: |h| > 0.99 in the imshow check, |t| > 0.97 in the histogram loop

  1. concept Explain why every fix in this lecture is really the same fix, and name the one thing BatchNorm does that the init fixes can't.
    Check
    • each fix keeps a signal at a sane scale: logits near 0, preactivations about unit-sized, weights at gain/√fan_in
    • the init fixes set the scale at step 0 only; training can move the weights away
    • BatchNorm re-standardises on every forward pass, whatever the weights have become
  2. mechanism Walk one minibatch through a BatchNorm layer in training mode, then say what changes in eval mode.
    Check
    • hpreact (32 × 200) = embcat @ W1, no bias
    • per column: subtract the batch mean, divide by the batch std
    • multiply by γ and add β (start at 1 and 0, trained by backprop)
    • under no_grad, nudge the running mean and std 0.1% toward this batch's
    • eval mode: use the running buffers instead of batch stats, so one example gives a fixed answer and no nan
  3. tradeoff Your deep tanh net trains slowly. The step-0 loss is 3.30 and the activation histograms look healthy. Which diagnostic do you check next, what reading would confirm a too-small learning rate, and how would you fix it?
    Check
    • the step-0 loss and histograms only check scale in the forward pass
    • plot log10(std(lr·grad) / std(w)) per weight matrix over time
    • curves well below −3 (e.g. −4.5 to −4.8 at lr 0.001) mean each step barely moves the weights
    • raise lr; each 10× moves every curve up by exactly 1
  4. tradeoff BatchNorm didn't beat Kaiming init on the one-hidden-layer MLP (val 2.1057 vs 2.1070). Give the case for it anyway, and the costs Karpathy warns about.
    Check
    • in deep stacks no hand-set scale survives many layers; BN keeps every layer standardised
    • it makes the forward pass gain-proof (activation std about 0.65 at gain 1 or 5/3)
    • costs: examples in a batch are coupled, a batch of 1 gives nan, train/eval mode and momentum bugs
    • alternatives that avoid the coupling: LayerNorm and GroupNorm

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs Finding the learning rate, then decaying it: The z2h-4 learning-rate finder and this lecture's update:data plot both help pick a learning rate. What does each one actually measure, which design choice makes the update:data plot able to spot a single bad layer, and how does each mislead you?

Check
  • the lr finder sweeps lr over steps and watches the whole-net loss
  • update:data measures each weight matrix's step relative to its own size, per step
  • because it is per tensor, one outlier (like a shrunk last layer) shows up; the loss can't show that
  • the finder is confounded by training progress during the sweep; −3 is a rule of thumb, not a law (Adam changes its meaning)

vs A neuron, backpropagated: micrograd's neuron has a tanh whose backward is (1 - t**2) * out.grad. What did that single line hide that this lecture had to deal with, and why didn't it matter for micrograd's tiny net?

Check
  • the factor 1 − t² can be close to 0, so a saturated unit passes almost no gradient
  • at 200 units with std-1 weights, hpreact has std about 5 and 61% of units saturate
  • micrograd's MLP(3, [4, 4, 1]) had few inputs per unit, so preactivations stayed small
  • at scale you have to control the preactivations' spread: init scaling, then BatchNorm

vs Negative log likelihood: one number for 'how good': The bigram lecture defined the loss as the average negative log likelihood. How does that same definition give you a free sanity check before training, and what does the check fail to catch?

Check
  • with no knowledge the model should give 1/V to every class, so the loss should be ln V (ln 27 = 3.2958)
  • a much larger step-0 loss means over-confident random logits, fixable at init
  • it says nothing about hidden layers: after fix 1 the loss was 3.3221 with 61% of tanh units saturated

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

You stack 50 layers of Linear(100, 100) → tanh with no BatchNorm, weights randn / √100 times a gain. Commit first: at gain 1, is the tanh output std at layer 50 about 0.6, about 0.1, or about 0? And at gain 5/3?

Reveal

At gain 1 it fades: std 0.635 at layer 1, 0.318 at layer 5, 0.153 at layer 20, 0.096 at layer 50, because every tanh squeezes and nothing undoes it. At gain 5/3 it holds: 0.764, then 0.650 from layer 5 on, 0.655 at layer 50. (Computed in scratch, one seeded batch of 32 unit-Gaussian inputs; not a lab number.) This is why the gain matters more the deeper you go, and why hand-balancing it was replaced by BatchNorm.
