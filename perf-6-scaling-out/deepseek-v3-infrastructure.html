DeepSeek-V3 II: infrastructure, FP8 and deployment

Also called DualPipe, cross-node all-to-all, fine-grained FP8 training, and DeepSeek's prefill/decode units. This card walks §3 of the DeepSeek-V3 report (arXiv 2412.19437 v2) section by section: the H800 cluster, the training framework, FP8, the inference deployment and the hardware wish list. It then checks the training bill and the Day 6 production numbers. Card 2 of 2. The architecture (MLA, DeepSeekMoE, node-limited routing, MTP) is card 1. Every number below is for DeepSeek-V3 on H800s, as the paper or post gives it. Numbers marked "ours" are our recomputation or inference.

In one breath

DeepSeek-V3 activates only 37B of its 671B parameters per token, so the math per token is cheap. The expensive part is moving tokens to experts on other nodes over InfiniBand, which is 3.2× slower than NVLink on their H800s. Almost everything in §3 is a way to hide or shrink that traffic. Training pairs a forward chunk with a backward chunk so each one's communication runs under the other's compute (DualPipe), caps each token at 4 nodes, and sends activations in FP8. Serving uses large, separate prefill and decode units so every expert still sees a big enough batch.

§3.1 · The cluster: 2,048 H800s, two very different links

2,048 NVIDIA H800 GPUs, 8 per node (256 nodes, ours). Inside a node, NVLink + NVSwitch. Between nodes, InfiniBand (IB), with all GPUs fully interconnected. The paper states the one ratio that shapes everything else: "NVLink offers a bandwidth of 160 GB/s, roughly 3.2 times that of IB (50 GB/s)." The H800 is the export version of the H100: same compute, cut-down NVLink.

§3.2 · The training framework: PP16 × EP64 × ZeRO-1, no tensor parallelism

DeepSeek trained with their own framework, HAI-LLM, using three kinds of parallelism and deliberately no tensor parallelism ("without using costly Tensor Parallelism"). TP would put two all-reduces per layer on the critical path. The memory tricks in §3.2.3 are what make it possible to skip TP.

§3.2.1 · DualPipe: why it exists

Cross-node expert parallelism gives "an inefficient computation-to-communication ratio of approximately 1:1". Run naively, the GPU computes for half the time and waits on all-to-all for the other half. DualPipe splits each chunk into attention, all-to-all dispatch, MLP, all-to-all combine (plus PP communication), and splits backward into "backward for input" and "backward for weights" (as in ZeroBubble). It then interleaves one forward chunk with one backward chunk. Each chunk's communication runs while the other chunk computes. The share of SMs given to communication is tuned by hand, so "both all-to-all and PP communication can be fully hidden."

Schematic of the idea in Figs 4–5, not to scale. Block sizes are equal to show the paper's ~1:1 ratio. Real backward chunks are longer than forward ones. Fig 4 also splits backward into input and weight parts and adds PP traffic. Fig 5 uses 8 PP ranks and 20 micro-batches in two directions. Idle cells are grey: waiting time.

The bidirectional schedule also shrinks the pipeline bubble. Table 2 of the paper, filled in for their PP = 16 (ours):

MethodBubble (paper, Table 2)Multiplier at PP = 16Params heldActivation memory

The paper says this is cheap because EP is large, so each GPU holds few experts. It needs stages and micro-batches divisible by 2, and "neither the bubbles nor activation memory will increase as the number of micro-batches grows."

§3.2.2 · Cross-node all-to-all: 20 SMs, at most 4 nodes per token

A token crosses IB once per target node, to the GPU with the same in-node index. That GPU forwards it over NVLink to the GPUs that hold its experts (green). Red is the bottleneck link. GPU and expert positions are illustrative.

IB is the bottleneck, so cap it: ≤ 4 target nodes per token (M = 4) NVLink ÷ IB = 160 ÷ 50 = 3.2 while one IB copy arrives, NVLink can forward about 3.2 copies inside the node → 3.2 experts per node "without incurring additional overhead from NVLink" max experts at the same comm cost = 4 nodes × 3.2 = 12.8 → "a maximum of 13" experts actually used = 8 routed # headroom, not a routing parameter SMs spent on communication = 20 of 132 = 15.2% # ours
  • 20 SMs → 10 channels, with warp specialization. For dispatch, separate warps do (1) IB sending, (2) IB-to-NVLink forwarding, (3) NVLink receiving. For combine, they do (1) NVLink sending, (2) NVLink-to-IB forwarding + accumulation, (3) IB receiving + accumulation. Warp counts adjust to the actual load.
  • Tokens never queue behind later arrivals. Forwarding starts on arrival, so the IB and NVLink transfers fully overlap.
  • Custom PTX and an auto-tuned chunk size. These cut L2-cache use and interference with the compute kernels running beside the communication kernels. For DeepSeek's open-source EP kernels (DeepEP), see expert parallelism.

§3.2.3 · Memory savings (why no TP was needed)

  • Recompute all RMSNorm and MLA up-projections in the backward pass instead of storing their outputs: "minor overhead", large activation saving.
  • EMA of the parameters in CPU memory, updated asynchronously after each step. The EMA is a smoothed copy used for early quality checks, and it costs no GPU memory or time.
  • Embedding and output head on the same PP rank. DualPipe places the shallowest and deepest layers together, so the MTP module physically shares the embedding and head (parameters and gradients) with the main model.

§3.3 · FP8 training: small scaling groups and FP32 every 128

The three GEMMs of every Linear layer run in FP8: Fprop (forward), Dgrad (activation gradient) and Wgrad (weight gradient). That "theoretically doubles the computational speed compared with the original BF16 method". FP8 has very little range (E4M3's largest value is 448; see tensor cores and low precision), so one outlier in a tensor-wide scale crushes every other value toward zero. DeepSeek's fix has two parts. The first gives each small group its own scale. The second stops the Tensor Core's short accumulator from losing bits over a long dot product.

Our notation for §3.3.2 and Fig 7. Plain text: each 128-long piece of the dot product is summed on the Tensor Core, copied to FP32 registers, multiplied by its two scales and added to a running FP32 total. Tile, block and promotion counts for K = 7168 (the hidden size) are ours.

  • E4M3 everywhere. Earlier recipes used E4M3 for Fprop and E5M2 (more range, less precision) for gradients. Small scaling groups share the exponent range across 128 values, so E4M3 is enough.
  • Online scaling. The max-abs of each tile or block is computed on the fly, not guessed from past iterations ("delayed" scaling).
  • Validation. At ~V2-Lite and ~V2 scale for about 1T tokens, the loss stays within "0.25%" (relative error) of BF16. App B.2: 128×128 block scaling of activation gradients made a ~16B MoE diverge after ~300B tokens, which is why activations use 1×128 tiles.
  • The same format serves inference: Day 6 runs GEMMs and dispatch in FP8, and core MLA and combine in BF16, "consistent with training".

§3.4 · Inference: separate prefill and decode units

Prefill and decode run on different GPUs (see DistServe I), each with its own layout. The paper's layout and the one in the Day 6 post (Mar 2025) differ, so here are both:

One routed expert = 3 × 7168 × 2048 ≈ 44.0M params (ours, from card 1). "–" = not stated. Routed-expert bytes ignore the shared expert and attention, which every GPU also holds.

  • Prefill: EP32 "ensures that each expert processes a sufficiently large batch size". Dense MLPs in the first layers use TP1 to save communication. Two micro-batches of similar work alternate: one's attention and MoE overlap the other's dispatch and combine.
  • Redundant experts. High-load experts, found from online statistics, are duplicated and refreshed periodically ("e.g., every 10 minutes"). In prefill, experts are rearranged within a node, so cross-node traffic doesn't grow. In decode each GPU has one expert, so there is nothing to rearrange. DeepSeek is also exploring dynamic redundancy: 16 experts per GPU, 9 active, with optimal routing computed each layer. For the algorithm, see EPLB.
  • Decode: the shared expert is treated as a routed one that is always picked, so each token selects 9. Dispatch and combine use point-to-point IB plus IBGDA (InfiniBand GPUDirect Async), which lets the GPU itself post the network operations (our gloss; the paper only cites NVIDIA) for lower latency.
  • Decode is memory-bound per expert. Tokens per expert are "usually within 256" and "the bottleneck is memory access rather than computation". So dispatch + MoE + combine get only a small share of SMs, and attention keeps the rest. Compare the H100's critical batch ≈ 295 (course 2): 256 < 295 agrees (ours, assuming the H800 has the H100's HBM bandwidth).

§3.5 · What they asked hardware vendors for (brief)

  • Take communication off the SMs. 20 of the H800's 132 SMs do forwarding, RDMA-buffer copies, combine reductions and memory layout, while "tensor cores remain entirely under-utilized". They ask for a co-processor (like NVIDIA SHARP) and one interface that unifies IB (scale-out) and NVLink (scale-up), with read, write, multicast and reduce primitives.
  • Fix FP8 math in the Tensor Core. Hopper aligns 32 mantissa products and keeps the top 14 bits, so they want higher-precision accumulation. They also want native tile/block scaling (Tensor Cores that take scale factors), FP8 cast fused with TMA loads (or near-memory casting, "roughly 50%" less off-chip access), and transposed GEMM reads for the 1×128 → 128×1 re-tiling.

Serving V3 and R1 together on H800s, one day: peak 278 nodes, average 226.75 nodes. Nodes are scaled down at night and lent to research and training. Input 608B tokens, of which 342B (56.3%) hit the on-disk KV cache. Output 168B tokens at 20–22 tok/s per user, with an average KV length of 4,989 tokens per output token. The node check matches to within 0.1%, which suggests the per-node figures are the day's token totals divided by the average node count (our inference). They are day-long averages, not peak capacity. At 20–22 tok/s per user, 1.94M output tok/s is about 88k–97k concurrent streams, or ~84–93 per decode GPU (ours).

Caveats the post itself gives about "545%". It's theoretical. Actual revenue is "substantially lower": V3 is priced well below R1, "web and APP access remain free", and night discounts apply. The cost side is only the assumed GPU lease ($2 per H800-hour). Our reading: anything outside that lease, such as staff or R&D, isn't in the $87,072.

The takeaway is only that a 671B MoE, spread over 144 GPUs, delivers per-GPU work of the same order as an ideal single-GPU 8B deployment.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Megatron-style TP across nodes

Two all-reduces per layer over 50 GB/s IB, on the critical path of every step. DeepSeek avoids TP in training entirely and uses TP4 only inside a node for serving attention.

✗ 1F1B pipeline, all-to-all run serially

With a ~1:1 compute:communication ratio, compute sits idle about half the time, and the bubble is (PP−1)(F+B) = 15(F+B) at PP16.

✗ Per-tensor FP8, delayed scaling, 14-bit accumulation

One outlier sets the scale for the whole tensor, gradients need E5M2, and long dot products drift: the paper measured nearly 2% error at K = 4096.

✓ Co-design: routing, kernels, schedule and format

At most 4 nodes per token, 20-SM all-to-all kernels, forward/backward pairing, and 1×128 / 128×128 FP8 with FP32 every 128. The result: 2.788M H800-hours and "no irrecoverable loss spikes".

Should everyone copy this layout?

No. It fits DeepSeek's problem: a huge sparse model, a cluster with a weak NVLink-to-IB ratio (3.2×), and hundreds of H800 nodes of traffic. Cases where it's the wrong choice:

  • Small teams or low traffic. The paper's §6 says it outright: "the recommended deployment unit … is relatively large, which might pose a burden for small-sized teams." A 320-GPU (or 144-GPU) decode unit only pays off if there are enough concurrent users to give every expert a batch. At low volume, the ~671 GB of FP8 weights fit on one 8×H200 node (1,128 GB), which is much simpler (course facts; see sizing a deployment).
  • Dense models. DualPipe's pairing and the 20-SM all-to-all exist because of expert traffic. A dense 70B has no dispatch/combine; TP inside a node is the standard answer (Megatron II).
  • Reading the headline numbers. $5.576M is the final run at an assumed rental price, and 545% is a hypothetical margin. Neither is DeepSeek's total cost or real profit.
