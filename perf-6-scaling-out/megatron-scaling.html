Megatron II: embeddings, the fused loss, and what TP costs

Also called vocab-parallel embedding, parallel cross-entropy, or "the cost of tensor parallelism". Part two of Megatron-LM (Shoeybi et al., 2019, arXiv 1909.08053): the rest of §3, Appendix B's GPU groups, the §5 scaling results on 512 V100s, and then the serving question they set up: what 2, 4 or 8-way TP does to one Llama-3.1-70B decode step. The paper says "intra-layer model parallel"; "tensor parallelism" (TP) is the later name.

In one breath

Megatron splits the model's two ends the way it splits the layers: each GPU keeps a slice of the vocabulary, only small tensors (b × s × H activations or b × s losses) ever cross GPUs, and cheap work like layernorm and dropout is simply repeated on every GPU. That kept an 8-way split at 77% efficiency inside one server. For serving, the same all-reduces are a fixed cost per layer that doesn't shrink as you add GPUs, so TP stops paying once the weight read gets small or the all-reduce has to leave the NVLink node.

The first Megatron card covers §2.3 and how the MLP and attention are split (two all-reduces per layer in the forward pass). This card picks up where §3 turns to the embeddings.

Worked example 1: the two ends of the model §3, the embedding paragraphs

GPT-2's vocabulary is 50,257 tokens and its output layer reuses the input embedding matrix (weights "tied"). The table E is H × v, so it's too big to copy onto every GPU. Megatron splits it along the vocabulary: GPU r holds the rows for its range of token ids.

Split 8 ways, as in the paper's 8.3B run. The padded vocabulary (51,200) is split into 8 slices of 6,400 ids.

Megatron's MP runs: b = 8, s = 1,024, v = 51,200 (padded) all-gather logits b × s × v = 8 × 1,024 × 51,200 = 419,430,400 values ≈ 839 MB in fp16 fused loss b × s = 8 × 1,024 = 8,192 values → v = 51,200× less traffic Vocab padding (§5.1): each GPU's slice a multiple of 128, up to 8-way 50,257 → next multiple of 128 × 8 = 1,024 → 51,200 = 50 × 1,024 +943 rows, 1.9% per GPU at 8-way: 51,200 / 8 = 6,400 = 50 × 128 Llama-3.1 128,256: / 8 = 16,032 = 125.25 × 128 → would pad to 129,024 (our recomputation)

The paper: "Communicating scalar losses instead of logits is a huge reduction in communication". It says only that the fusion "reduces the dimension to b × s". To keep the softmax exact, each GPU needs the per-token max and sum of exponentials over all slices, plus the target token's logit from whichever GPU holds it. That's three all-reduces of b × s values, still tiny (our inference; the lab checks it matches the unsplit loss).

Worked example 2: how the 512 GPUs are grouped Appendix B.1

8-way model parallel × 64-way data parallel = 512 GPUs. A DGX-2H holds 16 GPUs, so each server hosts two complete model-parallel groups and every model-parallel all-reduce stays on NVSwitch. Only the data-parallel gradient all-reduces cross InfiniBand. "All communication is implemented in PyTorch by Python calls to NCCL."

Worked example 3: what TP costs a 70B decode step our model, H100

Llama-3.1-70B in BF16 is 141.1 GB, too big for one 80 GB H100. Splitting it n ways divides the weight read by n. But every layer still does 2 all-reduces of the B × 8,192 activation (160 per step, our inference from Megatron's forward pass), and the message size doesn't depend on n. So the communication cost doesn't shrink (its latency part even grows with n) while the thing it's added to gets smaller.

With the shared illustrative values, TP16 over two nodes is about 5% faster at B = 1 (7.13 vs 7.52 ms), ties at B = 64 (8.18 vs 8.16) and loses at B = 256 (11.40 vs 10.11). The break-even cross-node latency sits right around the assumed 28 µs, so the latency verdict hinges on a number nobody publishes; measure it before trusting either answer. What doesn't hinge on it: at best, twice the GPUs buy 5%. Throughput per GPU falls the whole way: 5,596 tokens/s per GPU at TP2, 3,164 at TP8 and 1,404 at TP16 (B = 256). That's the serving version of Table 8's 82% → 37%. More TP buys latency for one user, paid for in GPUs.

How far TP can go, in the sources' own words

  • Scaling Book, GPU chapter (training, compute-bound). With Tmath = 4BDF/(Y·C) and Tcomms = 4BD/W, TP stays compute-bound while Y < F·W/C. "Within a node, this gives us about F / 2200 or F / 2475 beyond a node. For F=28000 like LLaMA-3, this is about 11-way TP (or rounding down, about 8-way, which is how large a node is)." Its TLDR: "Tensor parallelism is compute-bound up to about 8-ways but we lack the bandwidth to scale much beyond this before becoming comms-bound. This mostly limits us to a single NVLink domain". It also says 16-way across exactly 2 nodes is possible.
  • Scaling Book, Part 7 (generation). When decode is bound by the weight read, "we can increase model sharding beyond this point and improve latency at a minimal throughput cost", until the link time exceeds the weight read: Y < F / (B·β), β = WHBM / Wlink.
  • Kiely, Inference Engineering §5.4 (pp.142–147). "Tensor Parallelism should be your default strategy for multi-GPU model inference." TP "Requires synchronization across GPUs, not suitable for multi-node", and it "generally requires too much communication across GPUs to be a good fit for multi-node inference". For dense models: TP inside the node, PP across nodes ("TP8PP2").

The book's formula counts bandwidth only. At B = 1 it allows hundreds of ways, and it's the per-all-reduce latency above (and Llama's 8 KV heads) that stops you well before that. Over IB at B = 256, even 2-way TP would be link-bound.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Gather the logits, then take the loss

The simple output layer all-gathers b × s × v values: 419M per step in Megatron's runs, v = 51,200 times more than the fused loss needs.

✗ Compute layernorm and dropout once, broadcast

It saves a little arithmetic but adds a broadcast per op, and the paper's goal is "reducing communication and keeping the GPUs compute bound". Repeating cheap work is cheaper than sending it.

✗ Stretch TP across nodes

Only 2.63 ms of weight read is left to save (TP8 to TP16 for 70B), and all 160 all-reduces now pay cross-node latency and 9× less bandwidth. Best case in our model: 5% faster at B = 1 for twice the GPUs. Kiely: "not suitable for multi-node".

✓ Megatron's recipe: small messages, TP inside the node

Only activations and scalars cross GPUs. The TP group sits on NVSwitch and anything bigger (DP then, PP or replicas now) goes across nodes. That's how 8.3B hit 77% on 8 GPUs, and why Kiely makes TP inside one node the default for serving.

Is more TP always faster for serving?

For one user's latency, usually yes up to 8 inside one node. In our model TP8 is 2.8× faster than TP2 at B = 1. But it isn't free, and it isn't always the right choice:

  • Throughput per GPU drops. In our model it falls from 5,596 to 3,164 tokens/s per GPU going from TP2 to TP8 at B = 256, if the KV cache fits. In BF16 it barely does at TP2: 18.8 GB free ≈ 57k tokens, about 224 per sequence. That is one reason to look at FP8, which halves the weights and frees room on fewer GPUs. Course 2's serving-llama-70b card has the full numbers, and pipeline and data parallelism compares the layouts.
  • Latency, not bandwidth, is the small-batch cost. 16 kB messages don't care about 450 vs 370 GB/s. The per-all-reduce fixed cost does matter, and it's the one number here that is illustrative. Measure it with nccl-tests on the node you rent (the sizing card's [H] script does this).
  • Past 8 ways for Llama-3.1-70B, the 8 KV heads have to be copied onto several GPUs, so KV memory per GPU stops shrinking (our inference). Engines also overlap all-reduces with compute, which this model ignores.
  • Megatron is a training paper. Its f operator (the backward all-reduce) doesn't exist in inference, and its efficiency numbers are for V100 training throughput, not decode latency. Use it for the mechanism, not the percentages.
