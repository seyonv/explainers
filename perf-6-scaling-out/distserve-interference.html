DistServe I: why prefill and decode want different GPUs

Also called prefill–decode (P/D) disaggregation, disaggregated serving, or goodput-optimized serving. Card 1 of 2 on DistServe (Zhong et al., OSDI '24, arXiv 2401.09670 v3). It walks §1–3 in order: goodput, the Fig 1 arithmetic, interference and coupling, and the queueing analysis of each phase. Card 2 covers §4–7: the placement algorithms, KV transfer and the results.

In one breath

Prefill is one long, compute-heavy burst per request. Decode is many short, memory-bound steps. If one GPU does both, each new prompt freezes every stream in progress, and one set of GPUs has to be sized for whichever latency target is harder. DistServe puts the two phases on separate GPUs (instances), sizes and parallelizes each one for its own target (TTFT for prefill, TPOT for decode), and measures success as goodput: requests per second per GPU that still meet the targets.

§1 The metric: goodput under two SLOs

DistServe judges a serving system by cost per query under latency targets, not by raw throughput:

"maximize per-GPU goodput, defined as the maximum request rate that can be served adhering to the SLO attainment goal (say, 90%) for each GPU provisioned – higher per-GPU goodput directly translates into lower cost per query."DistServe §1. SLO attainment is "the proportion of requests that meet the SLOs", and a request counts only if it meets both TTFT and TPOT.

The paper names two latencies. TTFT is "the duration of the prefill phase" (plus any queueing). TPOT is "the average time taken to generate a token for each request (except for the first token)". Its footnote 1: request latency = TTFT + TPOT × tokens generated. Applications weight them differently. A chatbot wants low TTFT, and TPOT only has to beat reading speed ("250 words/min"). Summarization wants low TPOT. Different papers use different names for the per-token metric, so check the definition when you compare numbers:

§1 Fig 1: the 2.1× in four numbers

Fig 1 serves a 13B model on one A100-80GB with a synthetic workload (input 512, output 64) and SLOs meant to mimic a short summary. It plots P90 TTFT and P90 TPOT against request rate for three setups. The baseline is vLLM (the paper's ref [32]; the text just says "existing systems"). Read off the plot, the goodputs are:

Colocated (vLLM), 1 GPU1.6 rps Prefill-only GPU5.6 rps Decode-only GPU10 rps 2 prefill + 1 decode, per GPU3.3 rps
2 prefill GPUs 2 × 5.6 = 11.2 rps ≥ 10 # prefill keeps up 1 decode GPU 10 rps # the tighter stage system min(11.2, 10) = 10 rps on 3 GPUs per GPU 10 ÷ 3 = 3.33 rps gain 3.33 ÷ 1.6 = 2.08× # the paper rounds to 2.1×

Colocation's 1.6 rps is capped by whichever SLO breaks first. Split up, each phase runs until its own SLO breaks, and you buy each kind of GPU in the ratio the workload needs. The paper calls this ideal ("Ideally, by allocating 2 GPUs for prefill and 1 GPU for decoding"). It leaves out KV transfer, which §3.3 and card 2 cover.

§2.1 Two phases, two bottlenecks

Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel, so it is compute-bound once the prompt isn't short. The paper: "for a 13B LLM, computing the prefill of a 512-token sequence makes an A100 near compute-bound". Decode makes one token per step but reads "a similar level of I/O" (all the weights), so it is bound by memory bandwidth. The KV cache that prefill writes is needed by every later decode step, and that is why engines kept both phases on the same GPUs. In the running example:

prefill, 1,000 tokens 2 × 8.03e9 × 1,000 ÷ 989e12 = 16.24 ms # compute floor decode step, batch 1 16.06 GB ÷ 3.35 TB/s = 4.79 ms # bandwidth floor one prefill 16.24 ÷ 4.79 = 3.4 decode steps

Both values are floors from course 1 (100% of peak). Real kernels are slower, but the ratio makes the point: one 1,000-token prompt costs as much GPU time as 3.4 decode steps for everyone in the batch.

§2.2 The tools every engine already had

Continuous batching (Orca, vLLM, Sarathi) puts new requests' prefills in the same batches as running requests' decodes. That maximizes throughput, "tokens generated per second across all users", but DistServe argues that it trades TTFT against TPOT. Chunked prefill "trades TTFT for TPOT and cannot eliminate the interference" (more below). For model parallelism the paper uses two terms (citing AlpaServe, Megatron-LM and Alpa). Intra-operator parallelism splits each matmul across GPUs. That is tensor parallelism (Megatron I): it cuts execution time but needs NVLink-class bandwidth. Inter-operator parallelism splits the layers into pipeline stages (pipeline and data parallelism). It "moderately increases execution time" but "linearly scales the system's rate capacity". DistServe adds that both kinds also cut queueing delay, because shorter execution empties the queue faster. Footnote 3: latency = execution time + queueing delay. §3.1 turns this into equations.

§2.3 Three problems with colocation

1. Prefill–decode interference Fig 2

Fig 2 times one batch of a 13B model against batch size. It compares a decode-only batch with the same batch plus one prefill, at input lengths 128 (a) and 1,024 (b). Adding one prefill slows the whole batch ("decoding slowdown"), much more at 1,024 tokens. The decodes also slow the prefill once the GPU is saturated ("prefill slowdown"). The paper gives these results only as curves, with no table or quoted values, so we don't quote numbers from Fig 2. (The y-axes run to about 25 ms in (a) and about 125 ms in (b).) Here is the same effect with the running example's floors:

Our floors, not a measurement. On GPU B, TPOT is just the decode step. The new request's KV has to travel from A to B before it joins B's batch (card 2).

2. Ineffective scheduling

Running prefills and decodes in separate batches, one after another, doesn't fix this either. Decodes queue behind running prefills, and decode-only batches leave the GPU underused. "Prioritizing tasks in either phase adversely affects the latency of the other." Prefill-first protects TTFT and hurts TPOT. Decode-first does the opposite.

3. Resource and parallelism coupling

Colocated phases have to share one GPU count and one parallelism plan. Prefill is compute-bound and "benefits from more intra-op parallelism" to meet a tight TTFT. Decode's best plan "depends on the running batch size". So existing systems size everything for the harder of the two SLOs, which over-provisions for the other.

The chunked-prefill debate: both sides

The best-known colocated fix is chunked prefill with piggybacking (Sarathi-Serve II): cut each prompt into chunks and run each chunk in the same batch as the running decodes. DistServe §2.3 argues it "alleviates" the decode slowdown "but it does not eliminate it", and names three costs:

DistServe's case against

(1) Chunks well below the size that saturates the GPU make the prefill take longer, since it shares the GPU with decodes. (2) Chunks near that size leave little room to piggyback decodes. (3) Every chunk reloads the KV of all earlier chunks: "N + (N − 1) + ... + 1 = O(N²) chunks of KV Cache in total, compared to O(N)", which grows with context length.

Sarathi-Serve's case for

Chunking overhead is at most about 25% at chunk 512 and "almost negligible" at 2,048 (Yi-34B, Fig 14), and attention prefill stays compute-bound even at small chunks. Stall-free batching gets 2.6× vLLM's capacity (Mistral-7B, one A100) with no KV to move. Sarathi's §6 says disaggregation's KV migration is "challenging in the absence of high-bandwidth interconnects" and leaves prefill GPUs' memory underused.

# O(N²) re-reads for Llama-3.1-8B, 128 KiB/token (ours) 1,000 tokens, 4 chunks of 250 1+2+3+4 = 10 chunk loads vs 4 0.33 GB = 0.10 ms of HBM vs 16 ms of math (0.6%) 32,768 tokens, 64 chunks of 512 2,080 chunk loads vs 64 139.59 GB = 41.67 ms vs 532 ms of math (7.8%)

Our reading: with GQA's small KV, the re-read cost is tiny for chat-length prompts and becomes real only for long contexts. That matches both papers: Sarathi measured short-to-medium prompts, and DistServe's §7 itself says chunked prefill "may be preferred" for throughput-oriented jobs, while long context favours disaggregation. The rest of the argument is about the SLOs: chunking evens out TBT, but a prompt still shares every batch with decodes.

The opportunity

Split each request into a prefill instance, which computes the prompt and first token and then sends the KV cache on, and a decoding instance, which generates the rest. "Because decoding computation often has low GPU utilization, we may allocate multiple prefill instances per decoding instance", which lets the decode batch grow. Each instance type gets its own GPU count and its own parallelism plan.

§3.1 The prefill instance: a queue you can solve on paper

Batching. Fig 3(a): for 13B, "processing a single sequence of 512 tokens can fully engage an A100". After that, batching only makes the batch longer and delays everyone in it. So DistServe profiles a threshold Lm, the input length where prefill turns compute-bound, batches only requests shorter than it, and keeps prefill batches small.

Parallelism. The experiment: a 66B model on two A100s, uniform 512-token prompts, Poisson arrivals. Fig 4(a) measured: intra-op wins at low rates, inter-op at high rates. To explain why, the paper models a prefill-only instance as an M/D/1 queue (Poisson arrivals, deterministic service time, one server, FCFS, no batching since one request already saturates the GPU):

Eq 1 · one GPU
Avg_TTFT= Dexecution + RD22(1−RD)queueing

Avg_TTFTinter= Ds+ RDm22(1−RDm) = Dexecution, unchanged + RD24(2−RD)queueing, much smaller
Eq 3 · 2-way intra-op with speedup K
Avg_TTFTintra= DKexecution, faster + RD22K(K−RD)queueing

Eq 2 in words: the pipeline doesn't make one prefill faster, but it accepts a new request every D/2, so it behaves like one server that is twice as fast for queueing. Eq 3: tensor parallelism makes every prefill K times faster but can accept only K times the load.

Worked example with D = 1 (times in units of one single-GPU prefill) and K = 1.5, from the lab:

RD = 0.5 inter 1 + 0.5 ÷ (4 × 1.5) = 1.083 intra 1/1.5 + 0.5 ÷ (2×1.5×1.0) = 0.833 RD = 0.9 inter 1 + 0.9 ÷ (4 × 1.1) = 1.205 intra 1/1.5 + 0.9 ÷ (2×1.5×0.6) = 1.167 RD = 1.2 inter 1 + 1.2 ÷ (4 × 0.8) = 1.375 intra 1/1.5 + 1.2 ÷ (2×1.5×0.3) = 2.000 crossover Eq 2 = Eq 3 at RD ≈ 0.936, both ≈ 1.22 D

From the formulas, D = 1, K = 1.5. Left of the dashed line, the execution term dominates and intra-op's D/K wins. To the right, queueing dominates, and intra-op's capacity of K = 1.5 runs out long before inter-op's 2. A Monte Carlo run of 200,000 requests in the lab matches all three formulas (at RD = 0.5: 1.508 / 1.084 / 0.835 simulated vs 1.500 / 1.083 / 0.833).

The crossover moves with K: RD ≈ 0.94 at K = 1.5, 1.10 at 1.6, 1.26 at 1.7, 1.43 at 1.8 and 1.63 at 1.9 (lab). This is the paper's Fig 4(b) point: "a decrease in K notably reduces the efficacy of intra-op parallelism". The paper also says a stricter TTFT SLO favours intra-op, because only intra-op shortens execution. K itself depends on input length, model architecture, bandwidth and placement. For the running example, D = 16.24 ms gives one GPU a capacity of 61.6 req/s and puts the K = 1.5 crossover at 57.6 req/s (our arithmetic; K = 1.5 is illustrative, not measured for Llama-3.1-8B on H100s).

§3.2 The decoding instance: batch it, then parallelize for the SLO

Batching. One decode job is "heavily bandwidth-bound" (Fig 3(b): decode throughput keeps rising with batch size), so a big batch is the whole game. Colocated systems can't grow it without hurting latency. With disaggregation, several prefill instances feed one decoding instance, so it can build "a larger batch size on dedicated GPUs for the decoding phase without sacrificing TPOT". In our sim, a decode-only H100 runs at 120.5 req/s with an average batch of 110 (Little's law), still under 10 ms P90 TPOT.

Parallelism. Memory then caps the batch, since every active request's KV has to fit. Model parallelism, PagedAttention and GQA push the batch toward the compute-bound point, where decode "begins to resemble the prefill phase". Fig 5 (13B, batch 128, input 256, 1–8 GPUs): intra-op "reduces latency with diminishing returns", and inter-op "can almost linearly scale the throughput". The rule:

stringent TPOT SLO → intra-op (tensor) parallelism first, to cut the step time then, for throughput → inter-op (pipeline) parallelism, which scales ~linearly model fits one GPU → replication is also an option: N copies turn R into R/N in Eq 1, at the cost of N copies of the weights

Megatron II puts numbers on what tensor parallelism costs per decode step, and pipeline and data parallelism applies this rule to Llama-3.1-70B.

§3.3, in brief. Two practical wrinkles. Real prompts vary in length, which leaves pipeline bubbles in inter-op prefill and bends the M/D/1 predictions. And the KV cache has to move from prefill to decode. The paper's example is OPT-66B: about 1.13 GB (really GiB) per 512-token request, so 90 Gbps at 10 rps. Card 2 does that arithmetic and the placements that keep transfers on NVLink.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Colocated, prefill-first

TTFT is protected, but every prompt freezes all running streams. Fig 1: 1.6 rps per A100. Our sim: 20.5 req/s per H100, capped by TPOT while TTFT has room to spare.

✗ Priority scheduling either way

Separate prefill and decode batches, prioritizing one. "Prioritizing tasks in either phase adversely affects the latency of the other", and decode-only batches underuse the GPU.

✗ Chunked prefill with piggybacking

Smooths TBT, but in DistServe's words it "trades TTFT for TPOT and cannot eliminate the interference". It also re-reads O(N²) KV, and both phases still share one GPU count and one parallelism plan.

✓ Disaggregated prefill and decode

No interference, and each phase gets its own GPU count and parallelism, tuned to its own SLO: intra-op prefill at low load, inter-op at high load, a big decode batch. Fig 1: 3.3 rps per GPU, 2.1×.

Is disaggregation always better?

No. The 2.1× is an "ideal" from Fig 1 that leaves out KV transfer, and the gain depends on how tight the SLOs are: in our sim it drops from 1.68× to 1.11× when the TPOT SLO goes from 10 to 20 ms. DistServe's own §7 names when not to use it. For throughput-oriented or offline jobs, chunked prefill with piggybacking "may be preferred since it can fill each batch to the compute-bound threshold". With few GPUs, or one, there is no room to split the phases. Moving KV needs NVLink or fast InfiniBand, or placement that keeps transfers inside a node (card 2). Sarathi-Serve adds that prefill GPUs' memory sits mostly idle.

Two cautions about §3.1. Eq 1–3 assume uniform prompts, FCFS and no batching, and real length variation causes pipeline bubbles that the formulas don't see (§3.3). And the crossover RD ≈ 0.94 is for K = 1.5. With a good NVLink split (K near 1.9), intra-op wins almost everywhere.
