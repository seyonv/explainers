DeepSeek-V3 I: an architecture built for serving

Also called DeepSeekMoE, fine-grained and shared experts, sigmoid gating, node-limited routing, auxiliary-loss-free load balancing, and multi-token prediction (MTP). This card walks the DeepSeek-V3 report (arXiv 2412.19437) §2.1, §2.2, the §4.2 hyperparameters and the §4.5 ablations, in order. Its §3 (cluster, DualPipe, FP8, deployment) is on DeepSeek-V3 II. Anything labelled "our recomputation" or "our reading" is not in the report.

In one breath

DeepSeek-V3 is a 671B-parameter transformer where each token uses only 37B. In 58 of its 61 layers the feed-forward block is split into 256 small routed experts plus 1 shared expert, and a sigmoid router picks 8 of the 256, drawn from at most 4 nodes. Load is kept even by nudging a per-expert bias instead of adding a loss, so no token is ever dropped. Attention (MLA) caches only 576 numbers per token per layer, and one extra MTP block, trained to predict the token after next, can be reused as a speculative draft.

§2.1.1 · MLA, in three lines

MLA compresses each token's keys and values into one latent vector, cKV ∈ ℝ512, and caches it next to a 64-dim key kR that carries RoPE and is shared by all heads. At inference, the up-projections are absorbed into the query and output matrices, so per-head K and V are never built. For V3 that is (512 + 64) · 61 = 35,136 cached values per token, compared with 1,998,848 for MHA with the same heads. The full walk-through, including absorption and decoupled RoPE, is on MLA in course 4, Attention and the KV cache.

§2.1.2 · DeepSeekMoE: many small experts plus one that always runs

Node-limited routing: at most M = 4 nodes per token

In training, each layer's 256 routed experts are spread over 64 GPUs on 8 nodes, so 32 experts per node. Before picking its top 8, a token ranks the nodes by "the sum of the highest Kr/M affinity scores of the experts distributed on each node". With Kr = 8 and M = 4, that is each node's top 2 scores added together. It keeps the best 4 nodes and takes its top 8 from their experts only.

cross-node copies of one token's hidden state, 7,168 B each in FP8 (our arithmetic): no limit, uniform: 8 · (1 − C(224,8)/C(256,8)) = 5.29 nodes avg → 37.1 KiB M = 4: at most 4 nodes → ≤ 28.0 KiB

The paper's claim is that under this limit "our MoE training framework can nearly achieve full computation-communication overlap". The part where a token crosses InfiniBand once per node and is then forwarded over NVLink is §3.2.2, on DeepSeek-V3 II. Our reading: the released config.json writes the same rule as n_group: 8, topk_group: 4, so the limit applies at inference too. Uniform routing is an illustrative assumption; real routers are skewed.

Auxiliary-loss-free load balancing: steer with a bias, not a loss

If a few experts get most of the tokens, routing collapses and the GPUs holding those experts become the bottleneck. The usual fix, an auxiliary loss, "will impair the model performance" if it is too large. V3 instead gives each expert a bias bi that affects only which experts are picked:

No token dropping. "DeepSeek-V3 does not drop any tokens during training", and with "specific deployment strategies to ensure inference load balance" (the redundant experts of §3.4) it "also does not drop tokens during inference". Switch-style MoE sets a capacity factor and drops whatever overflows it (see MoE basics). A dropped token skips its expert, and whether that happens depends on which other requests share the batch. Our reading: without dropping, one request's output doesn't depend on its batch neighbours through capacity.

Why it helps: balance per batch, not per sequence (§4.5.3)

The real difference is scope. A sequence-wise loss forces every sequence to use every expert evenly. Batch-wise balance only asks the whole batch to, which "allows experts to better specialize in different domains". In Fig. 9 (16B models, Pile domains Wikipedia, Github and DM Mathematics, layers 9 and 18) the aux-free model's relative expert loads are visibly more domain-specific. To isolate scope from method, they also trained with a batch-wise auxiliary loss:

The report names two costs of batch-wise balance: imbalance "within certain sequences or small batches" (handled in training by large EP/DP micro-batches) and "domain-shift-induced load imbalance during inference" (handled by redundant experts, §3.4). Both matter when you serve it.

§2.2 · Multi-token prediction: a training signal that doubles as a draft

With D = 1, one extra module predicts the token after next. It is a single transformer block plus a d × 2d projection M1 that combines the main model's final hidden state for token i with the embedding of token i+1. Unlike Gloeckle et al.'s parallel heads, DeepSeek predicts the extra tokens sequentially to "keep the complete causal chain". The embedding and output head are shared with the main model. The loss weight λ is 0.3 for the first 10T tokens, then 0.1 for the last 4.8T. The main purpose is better training. In Table 4, adding MTP took large-model HumanEval from 44.5 to 53.7 and GSM8K from 72.3 to 74.0, "exactly the same" inference cost once the module is dropped. It can also be kept as a draft for speculative decoding (§5.4.3):

second-token acceptance (report): 85% … 90% across generation topics tokens per decode step (≤ 2): 1 + 0.85 … 1 + 0.90 = 1.85 … 1.90 reported speedup: 1.8× TPS implied step cost with the draft: 1.85/1.8 … 1.90/1.8 = 1.03 … 1.06× (ours)

The report doesn't give the batch size or hardware behind the 1.8×. The full comparison with EAGLE, and why verifying extra tokens on an MoE can touch extra experts, is on EAGLE, MTP and n-gram drafts in course 5, Inside an inference engine.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ A dense 671B model

Every token reads all 671 GB at FP8, 17.9× V3's 37.6 GB, and costs about 17.9× the matmul FLOPs. At small batch, every decode step pays that whole read.

✗ Coarse MoE, Mixtral-style

8 big experts, top-2, no shared expert. With only 8 experts per layer, expert parallelism can't spread wider than 8 GPUs (our reading). The DeepSeekMoE line argues finer experts specialize better (the V3 report cites it rather than re-testing it).

✗ Balance with an auxiliary loss

Works, but trades quality for balance. In Table 5 the large model loses 6.1 points of HumanEval (40.2 vs 46.3) and 3.8 of GSM8K relative to the bias method.

✓ V3's combination

MLA for a 68.6 KiB/token cache, 256 small experts with a 4-node cap on traffic, bias-based balancing without dropping tokens, and an MTP module that later serves as a draft for 1.8× TPS.

Is this design always the right one?

No. These choices pay off at DeepSeek's scale and with its hardware. They cost you elsewhere:

  • Small deployments. The report itself says "the recommended deployment unit for DeepSeek-V3 is relatively large, which might pose a burden for small-sized teams". At batch 1 you still have to hold 671 GB somewhere. A dense 70B on one 8-GPU node is simpler to run.
  • Batch-wise balance moves the problem to inference. Training balances the whole batch, but a serving batch drawn from one domain (all code, say) can overload a few experts. DeepSeek copes with redundant experts rebuilt from live load statistics (§3.4, EPLB). Without that machinery, you'd see stragglers.
  • Aux-free is not strictly better. MMLU dropped from 68.3 to 67.2 on the large ablation. And §4.5.3 shows that a batch-wise aux loss performs just as well, so the win comes from the looser balancing scope, not specifically from the bias.
  • MTP drafting on an MoE. Verifying 2 tokens can route them to different experts, reading more weights per step. The 1.8× is DeepSeek's own number on an unstated setup, and your engine may see less.
