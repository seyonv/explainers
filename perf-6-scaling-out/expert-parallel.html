Expert parallelism: dispatch, combine and EPLB

Also called EP, "MoE all-to-all", or dispatch/combine; the load balancer is EPLB (expert-parallel load balancer). DeepSeek-V3 serves its 256 routed experts per layer this way: prefill on 32 GPUs (EP32) and decode on 320 GPUs in the paper (EP320) or 144 in production (EP144), using its open-source DeepEP kernels and EPLB placement.

In one breath

Put whole experts on different GPUs. At every MoE layer, each token is sent to the GPUs that hold its chosen experts (dispatch), those experts run, and the results are sent back and summed (combine). Two all-to-all exchanges per layer replace the all-reduce of tensor parallelism. The layer can only finish when the busiest GPU finishes, so hot experts get extra copies (redundant experts), and EPLB decides where every copy lives.

The layout: attention stays local, the experts are spread out

Each GPU holds a few complete experts, not a slice of every expert. Attention runs data-parallel or with a small TP group: each GPU (or TP4 group) keeps its own requests and their KV cache. DeepSeek's prefill unit is 4 nodes / 32 GPUs (attention TP4 + SP with DP8; MoE EP32, 9 experts per GPU = 8 + 1 redundant). Its decode unit is 40 nodes / 320 GPUs (attention TP4 + SP with DP80; MoE EP320, one expert per GPU, with 64 GPUs holding the redundant and shared experts). Kiely's example: 128 experts in EP8 means each GPU hosts 16 full experts.

One MoE layer on four GPUs. The router is small and is copied onto every GPU, so each GPU picks its own tokens' top-8 experts and knows where to send them.

Why all-to-all, not all-reduce

Tensor parallelism splits every expert across GPUs. Each GPU computes a partial sum for every token, so everyone needs the total: that's an all-reduce. Under EP, a token's hidden vector has to go to specific GPUs, and each token picks different ones. That's a personalised scatter where each (sender, receiver) pair exchanges its own data, which is what AllToAll means. The combine is the same pattern in reverse. Only the token's home GPU needs the result, so nobody pays to broadcast a sum to everyone.

bytes per token per MoE layer (DeepSeek-V3: hidden d = 7,168, top-k = 8) dispatch 8 × 7,168 × 1 B (FP8) = 57,344 B = 56 KiB (+ 56 FP32 scales per copy → 59,136 B) combine 8 × 7,168 × 2 B (BF16) = 114,688 B = 112 KiB round trip = 172,032 B = 168 KiB per token per layer (× 58 MoE layers) over IB, node-limited to ≤ 4 nodes: dispatch ≤ 4 × 7,168 = 28,672 B · combine ≤ 4 × 14,336 = 57,344 B

Our recomputation. FP8 dispatch and BF16 combine are the paper's (§3.3.3) and DeepEP's settings. The scale format (one FP32 per 1×128 tile) is our assumption, but it reproduces DeepEP's bandwidth column almost exactly (table below).

A cross-node copy is 3.2× slower than an in-node one on H800. So V3 sends a token over IB once per target node, to the GPU with the same in-node index, which then forwards it over NVLink to the experts it needs there. IB and NVLink transfers overlap. The paper says this lets a token pick "an average of 3.2 experts per node" at no extra NVLink cost. That 3.2 is the bandwidth ratio 160/50, not a routing setting: the router itself scores each node by its top 8/4 = 2 experts.

Overlap without spending SMs

Normal kernels move data with SMs, like V3 training's 20 SMs. The low-latency kernels add a receive hook: low_latency_dispatch(..., return_recv_hook=True) starts the RDMA transfer and returns at once. The data arrives "in the background, without costing any GPU SMs", and only calling hook() waits for it. With two micro-batches, one computes attention or experts while the other's dispatch or combine is in flight. The four parts "may not have the exact same execution time", so the stages are tuned per workload. DeepSeek's production decode goes further, with a 5-stage pipeline that splits attention in two.

Stragglers, redundant experts and EPLB

Routing is uneven: some experts are hot for a given workload. A GPU that holds a hot expert gets more tokens, and every other GPU waits for it at the combine. The fix is to duplicate the hot experts and pack the copies so every GPU carries about the same load. EPLB is DeepSeek's open implementation, and it runs on predicted loads (for example a moving average). It greedily gives each extra slot to the expert with the highest load per copy, then sorts the copies heaviest first and puts each one on the lightest GPU that still has a free slot.

  • Hierarchical (used when the number of nodes divides the number of expert groups; prefill, smaller EP): first pack whole expert groups onto nodes evenly, then replicate inside each node, then pack onto GPUs. Tokens routed to one group stay on one node, which saves IB traffic.
  • Global (otherwise; decode, larger EP): replicate and pack across all GPUs, ignoring groups. In the code, it's just the hierarchical policy with 1 group and 1 node.

EP vs TP for a MoE model

Kiely (Inference Engineering §5.4, pp. 143–147): "Tensor Parallelism is generally best for low-latency model inference within a single node, while Expert Parallelism improves throughput for MoE LLMs." EP "requires less inter-GPU communication": unlike TP, "it is not required to collect the results of each layer", so it "scales well to multi-node deployments". Over InfiniBand he suggests TP8PP2 for dense models and trying EP16 for MoE. For MoE, "TP8PP2 will generally offer lower latency per user and EP16 will yield higher overall system throughput."

Our recomputation, total bytes on the wire per token per MoE layer. TP = n × 2(n−1)/n × 14,336 B. EP = 8 × (n−1)/n × (7,168 + 14,336) B, because a copy is free when the expert sits on the token's own GPU. TP's all-reduce grows with n. EP's traffic is capped by top-k, which is why it scales across nodes. At n = 8 the gap comes entirely from the FP8 dispatch: with BF16 in both directions the two would tie at 200.7 kB.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ TP across the experts

Slices every expert over every GPU. Each GPU's matmuls get tiny, and the all-reduce grows with the GPU count (430 kB vs 161 kB per token at 16 GPUs), which is too much for InfiniBand.

✗ Plain EP, fixed placement

The step waits for the busiest GPU. In the README example the hot expert alone is 1.42× the ideal load. In the lab's skewed 256-expert case, EP32 without copies runs at 1.80× the mean.

✗ Drop the overflow tokens

Switch-style capacity limits skip tokens that overflow an expert, which costs quality. DeepSeek-V3 drops no tokens in training or inference and balances by bias and placement instead.

✓ EP + DeepEP + EPLB

Whole experts per GPU give big per-expert batches. Two all-to-alls, capped at 168 KiB per token, overlap with a second micro-batch. Redundant copies bring the busiest GPU to within about 1.2× of ideal, or better.

Is EP always the right way to serve a MoE?

No. EP pays off when the batch is large enough that every expert gets real work, and when the model is too big for one node. It isn't the right choice when:

  • The model fits in one node and you care about per-user latency. Kiely: TP8PP2 gives lower latency per user and EP16 more total throughput. Small MoEs like Mixtral are commonly served with TP (general practice, not from these sources).
  • You can't fill it. DeepSeek calls its own deployment unit (320 GPUs for decode) "a burden for small-sized teams". At small batch, each expert gets a handful of tokens and the all-to-all latency (77–360 µs per call) dominates.
  • You assume "less communication" means cheap. In training, DeepSeek measured a computation-to-communication ratio of "approximately 1:1" for cross-node EP. It needed custom kernels, 20 SMs and DualPipe to hide it. EP avoids the per-layer all-reduce, but at scale its all-to-all is still the bottleneck.

The load balancer also only predicts. EPLB balances the estimated loads, so a sudden shift in traffic unbalances things until the next rebalance (DeepSeek adjusts about every 10 minutes).
