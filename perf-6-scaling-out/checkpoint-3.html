Checkpoint 3: feeding the experts

Ties together mixture of experts, expert parallelism, DeepSeek-V3 I, DeepSeek-V3 II, MegaScale-Infer I and MegaScale-Infer II: why an MoE's experts starve in decode, and the three ways to feed them (pool with EP, balance with copies, or split attention from the experts).

How to use this page. Close the six cards. Answer each question in writing, in full sentences, before opening its Check. A wrong or shaky answer isn't a failure: it tells you exactly which section to reopen. The questions are mixed on purpose, so you have to work out which idea applies before you can answer.

1 · Mixed retrieval

  1. which concept?A single DeepSeek-V3 replica decodes at batch 128. Profiling shows the step reads nearly all 671 GB of weights, yet every expert GEMM is memory-bound. Which idea explains both facts, and what is the fix?
    Check
    • the shrinking batch: each expert sees about B·k/E = 128 × 8/256 = 4 tokens
    • but a token misses an expert with probability 1 − 8/256, so 98.3% of experts are touched at B = 128 (uniform routing)
    • each expert needs ≈ 9,440 tokens per step (295 × 32 on an H100) to be compute-bound: pool tokens from many replicas with expert parallelism

    Missed it? Reopen mixture of experts, "The serving catch" and "And almost every expert gets read anyway".

  2. Per token per MoE layer at 16 GPUs, how many bytes does tensor parallelism put on the wire, and how many does expert parallelism (top-8, FP8 dispatch, BF16 combine)? Which grows with the GPU count?
    Check
    • TP: 16 × 2(15/16) × 14,336 B ≈ 430 kB, and it grows with n
    • EP: 8 × (15/16) × (7,168 + 14,336) B ≈ 161 kB, capped by top-k
    • so EP scales across nodes where TP can't

    Missed it? Reopen expert parallelism, "EP vs TP for a MoE model".

  3. which concept?In an EP32 deployment the same GPU finishes every MoE layer last, and all others wait at the combine. Which concept names this, and what fixes it without dropping tokens?
    Check
    • the layer waits for the busiest GPU: a hot expert makes its GPU a straggler (the lab's skewed load: 1.80× the mean)
    • add redundant copies of hot experts and pack them so every GPU's load is near the mean (EPLB): 1.016× hierarchical, 1.005× global with 32 copies
    • loads are predicted, so a traffic shift unbalances things until the next rebalance

    Missed it? Reopen expert parallelism, "Stragglers, redundant experts and EPLB".

  4. DeepSeek-V3 keeps two experts with sigmoid affinities 0.881 and 0.832 (top-2 shown for simplicity). What are the gates, and why doesn't V3's balancing bias change them?
    Check
    • normalise over the kept experts: 0.881 / 1.713 = 0.514 and 0.832 / 1.713 = 0.486
    • the bias b_i is added only when selecting the top experts
    • the gate uses the raw affinity, and b isn't in the loss, so balancing doesn't pull on the outputs

    Missed it? Reopen DeepSeek-V3 I, "Auxiliary-loss-free load balancing", and mixture of experts, "The same logits, three gating orders".

  5. which concept?V3 trained with near-perfect expert balance, but a serving batch made entirely of code requests overloads a handful of experts. Which property of its balancing explains it, and how does DeepSeek cope?
    Check
    • balance is enforced over the whole training batch, not per sequence, so experts specialise by domain
    • a single-domain serving batch concentrates on those experts ("domain-shift-induced load imbalance")
    • redundant copies of high-load experts, refreshed from live statistics about every 10 minutes

    Missed it? Reopen DeepSeek-V3 I, "Why it helps", and DeepSeek-V3 II, §3.4.

  6. An attention–expert disaggregated deployment has balanced compute and a network time of 0.6 × the per-micro-batch compute. How many micro-batches does it need, and what happens with one fewer?
    Check
    • m ≥ 2(1 + 0.6) = 3.2, so m = 4
    • at m = 3 the round trip isn't covered: the card's simulator gives 92.7% busy at T_c/T_f = 0.6 (steady-state bound 3/3.2 ≈ 94%)
    • more micro-batches raise per-token latency: each layer waits up to m·T_f

    Missed it? Reopen MegaScale-Infer II, "The three conditions" and the busy-share table.

  7. Why does DeepSeek's decode unit give the MoE part only a small share of SMs, when prefill gets a large one?
    Check
    • in decode, tokens per expert are "usually within 256", below the H100-class critical batch of ≈ 295
    • so the expert GEMMs are bound by memory access, not compute
    • extra SMs wouldn't speed them up; attention keeps the rest

    Missed it? Reopen DeepSeek-V3 II, §3.4 "Decode is memory-bound per expert".

  8. With Mixtral 8×22B on the A100 roofline (F/B = 156) and 128 tokens per attention micro-batch, how many attention nodes make each expert compute-bound, and what is one node's utilization alone at m = 3?
    Check
    • n_a = ⌈156 × 8 / (2 × 128)⌉ = ⌈4.88⌉ = 5, giving b_e = 160 tokens per expert
    • alone, a node's 384 tokens give 384 × 2/8 = 96 per expert: 61.5%
    • only the ratio E/K and the GPU's F/B matter for this sizing

    Missed it? Reopen MegaScale-Infer I, "§4.2 Sizing the split".

3 · Project: a toy MoE decode fleet, from router to ping-pong

What it reinforces: routing and the shrinking batch (mixture of experts), dispatch bytes and redundant experts (expert parallelism), V3's shapes (DeepSeek-V3 I), per-expert batches in decode (DeepSeek-V3 II), attention-node sizing (MegaScale-Infer I) and micro-batch count (MegaScale-Infer II).

In numpy, in about 80 lines:

  1. Route. Send B tokens top-8 of 256 experts with uniform scores. For B = 16, 64 and 128, count experts touched and the mean tokens per touched expert. Check against 256 · (1 − (1 − 8/256)^B).
  2. Pool. DeepSeek's paper decode unit has 80 attention replicas feeding EP320. If each replica contributes b tokens per step, how many tokens does each routed expert see on average for b = 32, 64 and 128? Which b first clears the H100 critical batch of 295?
  3. Balance. Give 8 experts the loads [120, 60, 50, 40, 30, 30, 20, 10] and place them on 4 GPUs with 3 slots each. Implement EPLB's greedy replicate-then-pack and report max/mean, with and without copies.
  4. Traffic. Compute dispatch + combine bytes per token per step for V3 (d = 7,168, top-8, FP8 out, BF16 back, 58 MoE layers).
  5. Pipeline. Simulate m micro-batches through attention → link → experts → link for L = 56 layers with T_a = T_e = 1 and T_c = 0.4. Report busy share for m = 2, 3, 4.
Done when: your touched counts match the formula within a few percent, you have the three pooled per-expert batches, a packing with its max/mean, the per-token bytes, and three busy shares that match the card's table. Then compare with the check.
Check: the numbers (the cards' tables and our arithmetic)
  • Touched (formula): 39.8% at B = 16, 86.9% at 64, 98.3% at 128 of each layer's 256 experts; mean tokens per touched expert stays small (e.g. 128 × 8 / 251.6 ≈ 4.1 at B = 128).
  • Pooled: b × 80 × 8 / 256 = 80, 160 and 320 tokens per expert for b = 32, 64, 128. Only b = 128 clears 295; the card's DeepSeek note ("usually within 256") says production decode sits below it.
  • Balance: copies E0 × 3 (40 each), E1 × 2 (30), E2 × 2 (25); GPU loads 95, 95, 90, 80 → 95 / 90 = 1.06×. Without copies E0's GPU carries at least 120 = 1.33×.
  • Traffic: 57,344 + 114,688 = 172,032 B per token per layer; × 58 ≈ 9.98 MB per token per step.
  • Busy share (the card's simulator): 71.0% at m = 2, 98.9% at m = 3, 99.2% at m = 4, since 2(1 + 0.4) = 2.8.
