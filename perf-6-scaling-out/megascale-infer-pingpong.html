Before this: MegaScale-Infer I: splitting attention from the experts

MegaScale-Infer II: the ping-pong pipeline, M2N and results

Also called ping-pong pipeline parallelism, micro-batch ping-pong, or attention–FFN disaggregation (AFD) with micro-batching; M2N/N2M is the many-attention-GPUs to many-expert-GPUs token exchange. This card walks MegaScale-Infer (ByteDance Seed, arXiv 2504.02263 v4) §4.1–4.2, §5 and §7: m = 3 or 4 micro-batches keep attention and expert GPUs busy, a custom RDMA library replaces NCCL, and decode throughput per GPU rises up to 1.90× over TensorRT-LLM. Part I covers why attention and experts are split at all.

In one breath

Once attention runs on one set of GPUs and the experts on another, each GPU would sit idle while the other side works and while tokens cross the network. So the batch is cut into m micro-batches that bounce back and forth ("ping-pong"): while the experts work on micro-batch 1, attention works on micro-batch 2. With compute balanced on both sides and m ≥ 3 (or 4 on a slower network), every GPU stays busy. The network hop itself has to be fast, so MegaScale-Infer drops NCCL for its own CPU-driven RDMA path.

Why a pipeline at all

Each token has to finish attention before its experts can run, and the experts before the next layer's attention. With one batch, the paper says, this "would result in idle time for both the attention nodes and the expert nodes when the other module is busy. GPUs also remain idle during the inter-node communication." The fix is the same trick as pipeline parallelism (see pipeline and data parallelism), but with only two stages that alternate for every one of the L layers. The nodes "exchange intermediate results twice in each MoE layer": attention → experts (dispatch) and experts → attention (combine).

Simulated by labs/pingpong.py (every resource first come, first served; the two link directions are separate). With m = 3 the attention node runs back to back from 0 to 6, because micro-batch 1 comes back at 2.8, just before the node frees up at 3.0. (1) removes idle time caused by one side waiting on the other. (2) and (3) hide the network: one micro-batch's transfer must be shorter than its compute, and "the forward time of one MoE layer for the global batch on each node must be sufficient to cover the time required for a single micro-batch to pass through the layer". The upper bound m·Tf·L is the key trade-off: one micro-batch's token waits for m slots of work at every layer. The paper says so directly: ping-pong "does not reduce the per-token latency for an individual micro-batch … this approach may even incur additional latency, as indicated by constraint 3". Filled in with the paper's 150 ms time-between-tokens (TBT) SLO and Mixtral's 56 layers (our recomputation): Tf ≤ 150 ms ÷ (3 × 56) = 893 µs per layer at m = 3, or 670 µs at m = 4.

M2N: why NCCL falls short here (§5)

Token dispatch is point-to-point with destinations that change every step, so NCCL's send/recv is the natural baseline. The paper compared it against perftest, a plain RDMA benchmark used as a lower bound: one sender, 128 KB to each of 8, 16 or 32 receivers. NCCL's median latency "significantly exceeds" perftest, and its P99 shows "a significant surge, particularly when scaling to 32 or more receivers" (Fig 5; the values are only in the figure). The reasons it gives:

  • Extra copies: NCCL moves data from GPU memory to a CPU proxy that does the networking. User buffer registration reduces these copies but doesn't remove them.
  • Batching: peer-to-peer group operations are processed "in batches of at most 8 operations", which hurts as receivers scale.
  • Generic setup: preparing and launching a group of N sends, plus internal handling and verification.
  • P99 instability: GPU synchronization and device-memory accesses, present in NCCL and absent in perftest.

The replacement: a CPU-driven RDMA path, six steps per side

StepSender (Fig 6)Receiver (Fig 7)
1Wait on a CUDA event for the kernels that produce the dataWait on a CUDA event until the buffer is no longer in use
2…so the pre-registered tensor is fully written…so the pre-registered tensor can be overwritten
3Block the stream with a CUDA driver operation so the next kernel waitsBlock the stream
4CPU "Core Sender" does an RDMA write with immediatePoll the completion queue to confirm the data arrived
5Poll the completion queue: data is in the remote buffersCPU "Core Receiver" uses GDRCopy and a flush for GPU-level consistency
6Unblock the stream by flipping a shared-memory flagUnblock the stream via the flag

No GPU-to-CPU copies, no group setup, no GPU synchronization, and no SMs used for communication. Two traffic fixes came from scale testing. High-priority ACKs: in two-way ping-pong traffic, ACK packets queued behind data, which stalled senders, so ACKs go in their own high-priority queues with tuned weights. Congestion-control tuning: latency degraded when each receiver got a different amount of data, so they tuned congestion control to reduce rate-limiting and converge faster. Against DeepEP (see expert parallelism), the paper argues that a single CPU thread saturates the NIC at "several hundred kilobytes" per pair, but GPU-driven sends could win if messages get smaller.

Quirks to keep straight

  • 1.90× is vs TensorRT-LLM, on Scaled-MoE, decode only. Against vLLM the same setup is 7.11×.
  • Heterogeneous gain: 1.7× or 1.86×? The intro says "1.7× higher throughput per unit cost". The contributions list and §7.2 say 1.86× (vs TensorRT-LLM on H20). The paper doesn't reconcile them.
  • "Per cost" is normalized purchase price, not a dollar figure; the paper notes rental prices could be swapped in.
  • m = 2 already helps (1.9× over m = 1) even though the formula demands m ≥ 3. There's no conflict: m = 2 hides the other side's compute, and the third micro-batch hides the network. With Tc → 0 the bound tends to 2.
  • "minimize" in §4.2 should be "maximize" (see Algorithm 1).
  • Figure values aren't in the text. Everything here is a number stated in the prose.

Contrast: Step-3's AFD (StepFun, arXiv 2507.19427)

Step-3 (321B total, 38B active) also splits attention from the FFN, but designs the model for it and aims at latency. It reports up to 4,039 tokens/GPU/s under a 50 ms TPOT SLA (4K context, FP8, no MTP, Hopper), against 2,324 for DeepSeek-V3 in its comparison (a DeepSeek profiling number on H800, not re-run by Step-3). Its pipeline has 3 stages (attention / communication / FFN) at 16.6 ms per stage, which over 61 layers leaves 272 µs per layer per stage. Compare MegaScale's 893 µs per layer at m = 3 under 150 ms (our recomputation). Step-3 criticizes exactly that: "the reported latency per token of Megascale-Infer is 150ms … not applicable for real-time applications like chatbots." One mismatch in Step-3 itself: its text extrapolates 8K context to ≈ 2,693 tokens/GPU/s, while Table 8 lists 2,643.

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Colocated serving (vLLM, TRT-LLM)

Attention and experts share the GPUs, so each expert sees only its own small slice of the batch and stays memory-bound. Scaled-MoE: 7.11× (vLLM) and 1.90× (TRT-LLM) behind MegaScale-Infer on decode throughput per GPU.

✗ Disaggregate, one batch (m = 1)

Each side waits for the other plus two network hops: at best Tf / 2(Tf + Tc), under 50% busy. The paper measured m = 2 at 1.9× over m = 1.

✗ Ping-pong over NCCL

Copies through a CPU proxy, batches of ≤ 8 P2P ops and spiky P99 push Tc toward Tf, so the network stops hiding. Its own M2N added up to 1.53× on top of the NCCL version.

✓ Balanced ping-pong + M2N

na chosen so Ta ≈ Te, m = 3–4, and a CPU-driven RDMA path at 4.2× NCCL's throughput for 256 KB messages. Both sides stay about 99% busy in the model, at a TBT comparable to the baselines.

Is the ping-pong pipeline always worth it?

No. It trades per-token latency for throughput per GPU, and it pays off only when the experts would otherwise be starved.

  • Tight latency targets. Each token waits for m·Tf per layer (Eq 4). MegaScale-Infer was evaluated at 150 ms TBT; Step-3 had to design its model and pipeline around 50 ms TPOT to use the same idea for chat.
  • Small deployments or dense models. With too few attention replicas the experts idle anyway (DBRX at DP 1–4). A dense model's FFN already sees the whole batch, so there's little to gain (see Part I).
  • Prefill. It's already compute-bound, so end-to-end gains shrink to 1.18× on homogeneous Ampere.
  • Imbalanced or shifting load. Uneven sequence lengths across attention nodes and hot experts create bubbles. In production (§8) they had to build each attention node's batch toward a target time and rebalance experts.
  • Slow or tiny messages. If Tc ≥ Tf, no m helps (76.5% busy even at m = 8 in the lab). With more experts and smaller per-pair messages, GPU-driven all-to-all like DeepEP may beat a CPU path.
