The toolkit in one training step

Also called: the math and code toolkit, the prerequisites. The overview of course 1 of Zero to hero: neural networks, built. It has no lecture of its own: it teaches what the lectures assume, and fits all twelve cards onto one step of training, from a batch of inputs to a loss and its gradient. Read it first as the map, or last as the recap.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: Zero to Hero never stops to teach its tools. Lecture 1 assumes Python classes and calculus, and from Lecture 2 on a single line such as loss = F.cross_entropy(h @ W2 + b2, Y) uses a matmul, broadcasting, softmax, a log and the chain rule at once. If one of them is shaky, you can't tell a bug in your code from a gap in the math.

Where it fits: This is the front page of course 1. It comes after the start-here map (z2h-0) and before micrograd. It fits the twelve toolkit cards onto one training step: a layer, a loss and its gradient. Every later course repeats that step, so their cards point back to its parts here.

Builds on: The whole stack in one sketch

You'll meet: the loss formula: softmax of XW + b, then the average −log p shapes first: matmul, dims, views and broadcasting why weights start at randn / √fan_in gradients: local slopes multiplied along a chain and added across paths sampling from p with a seeded generator

By the end: Point at any symbol in the loss formula and name the card behind it, predict what each lever in the table does to a number, and check off the six finish conditions before you start micrograd.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. Which symbols in the three-line formula come from which card group, and which two cards don't appear in the formula at all?
  2. In the levers table, which mistakes raise an error and which ones just change a number? Why is the second kind more dangerous?
  3. In the sketch, which three operations move numbers without doing arithmetic, and which of them costs memory?
  4. Why does the backward arrow run the whole length of the step, when each card only ever worked out one local slope?
  5. Before any training, which two numbers tell you a fresh model is set up correctly, and which cards predict them?

In one breath: every model in the series ends the same way. A layer turns a batch into scores with one matrix multiply, softmax turns the scores into probabilities, the loss is the average −log of the probability given to the right answer, and the chain rule sends that loss back to every weight. The twelve cards are the pieces of that step. Python and your M3 are what it runs on.

The whole course on one step

Read it as one step of the MLP from Lecture 3 (sizes from the tensors and matmul cards). Three ops move numbers around without arithmetic: indexing copies, .view relabels, and broadcasting stretches without copying. Two ops do the arithmetic: the matmuls and softmax. The loss comes back through all of them.

Glossary

Every term this course introduces, linked to the card that introduces it.

Your tools

dunder method: a method named __like_this__ that Python calls for you when you use an operator

operator overloading: defining __add__, __mul__ so that a + b runs your code

reflected method: __rmul__, __radd__: tried on the right operand when the left gives up; they make 2 * a and sum() work

closure: a function plus the outer variables it captured; how each _backward remembers its inputs

topological order: every node after all of its inputs; reversed, it is the backprop order

venv: a folder holding its own Python and packages

device (cpu, mps, cuda): where a tensor lives and computes; MPS is PyTorch's Apple GPU backend

seed, generator: a random-number source started from a fixed number, so the draws repeat; each device has its own stream

Calculus

derivative: how much the output moves per tiny nudge of the input, f′(x)

nudge ratio: (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h: a numeric estimate of the slope (a finite difference)

centred difference: (f(x+h) − f(x−h)) / 2h: cancels the leftover 3h error

partial derivative (∂): the slope with respect to one input while the others stay fixed

tanh: squashes any number into −1…1; its slope is 1 − tanh²

local derivative: one operation's slope with respect to its own input, ignoring the rest

chain rule: dz/dx = dz/dy · dy/dx: rates multiply

multivariable chain rule: a value that reaches the output by several paths gets the sum of their products

Numbers in bulk

exp, log: ex and its inverse, the natural log (base e)

log likelihood: the sum of the log-probabilities of the data

underflow, overflow: a float32 result too small to store (rounded to 0) or too big (inf)

vector: an ordered list of numbers

dot product: multiply position by position, then add: x·w

bias: one extra number added after the dot product

cosine similarity: the dot product with both lengths divided out: direction only

matrix multiply (@): every row of A dotted with every column of B

fan_in, fan_out: inputs feeding one neuron; number of neurons in the layer

batched matmul: extra leading dims come along: (4, 5, 80) @ (80, 200) → (4, 5, 200)

tensor: a block of numbers plus a shape

dim (axis), ndim: a position in the shape, counted from 0; ndim is how many there are

keepdim: keep a summed-away dim as size 1, so the result still lines up

view, storage, stride: a new shape written over the same memory; fails on a non-contiguous tensor, where .reshape copies

indexing with a tensor: C[X] looks up every index at once; shape X.shape + C.shape[1:], and a copy

dtype: the number type: torch.tensor infers int64, torch.Tensor is always float32

broadcasting: line shapes up on the right; stretch size-1 and missing dims to match

Probability

discrete distribution: non-negative numbers, one per outcome, that sum to 1

normalise: divide by the total so the numbers sum to 1

cumulative-sum sampling: pick the first outcome whose running total is bigger than a uniform u

torch.multinomial, replacement: draws indices in proportion to weights; replacement=True lets an outcome repeat

law of large numbers: frequencies approach p as draws grow; the noise shrinks like 1/√n

mean, variance, std: where numbers sit; their average squared distance from it; its square root

Bessel's correction: dividing by n − 1 instead of n; torch's default

Gaussian N(0, 1): the bell curve torch.randn draws from: 68%, 95%, 99.7% within 1, 2, 3 std

independent: knowing one tells you nothing about the other; then variances add

standardising: (x − mean) / std: mean 0, std 1; BatchNorm's core step

logits: the raw scores a network outputs, one per class, any sign

softmax: exp each logit, then divide by the total

temperature: dividing the logits by T before softmax: flatter or peakier

likelihood: the product of the probabilities given to the correct answers

cross-entropy, NLL: −log p(correct), averaged over the examples: the loss

Open each step in order · why this works
1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • A neuron is a dot product → put examples in the rows of X and neurons in the columns of W → one matmul computes a whole layer, z = XW + b, with b broadcast down the rows → the shapes decide what gets computed, so a shape that fits the wrong way (a missing keepdim) gives wrong numbers and no error
  • Each output adds up fan_in products → independent variances add, so the std grows like √fan_in (3.16 at fan_in 10) → dividing W by √fan_in keeps the logits near std 1 → softmax stays soft and the loss at step 0 starts near log 27 = 3.2958
  • Logits can have any sign → softmax exponentiates and normalises, and only the gaps between logits matter → the loss is −log p(correct), averaged over the batch → working in logs avoids float32 underflow (a product of 0.0 vs a log-sum of −3295.84) and ranks models the same way
  • Each operation knows its own local slope → the chain rule multiplies slopes along a path and adds across paths → one backward sweep gives ∂L/∂W for every weight → check it with a nudge at a moderate h (about 10⁻⁵), and use += so a value used twice keeps both paths
  • detail that matters A seed fixes one generator's stream, not every device's → the CPU generator replays the notebook's [0.7081, 0.3542, 0.1054] while mps gives [0.9843, …] → the makemore labs stay on cpu so that every "you should see" matches

Reference only

  • the exact dunder names (__rmul__, __radd__) and the build_topo code
  • the seven derivative rules as a table
  • torch.tensor (int64) vs torch.Tensor (float32)
  • .view vs .reshape, and strides
  • Bessel's n − 1 default in torch.var
  • the venv install commands and package versions

Why this step?

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain where the 3.2958 in the formula's key comes from, using only the softmax and log cards, and why it is the loss you expect from a well-initialised 27-class model before training.
    Check
    • equal (for example all-zero) logits make softmax uniform: p = 1/27 for every class
    • the loss is −log(1/27) = log 27 = 3.2958, whatever the target
    • a good init keeps the logits small, so the step-0 loss lands near it
    • a step-0 loss far above it means the logits are too large: confidently wrong (28.29 with an unscaled 200-input output layer)
  2. mechanism Walk one batch of 32 three-character contexts through the formula to the loss and back. At each step, name the card and say what happens to the shape.
    Check
    • X (32, 3) integers → C[X] (32, 3, 2), a lookup that copies
    • .view(-1, 6) → (32, 6), relabelled without copying
    • @ W1 (6, 100) + b1 (100,) → (32, 100): a matmul, with b broadcast down the rows
    • then logits (32, 27) → softmax along each row → pick p[n, yn] → −log, mean → one number
    • backward: local slopes multiplied along the chain; b's gradient is summed over the 32 rows it was stretched across
  3. tradeoff You suspect a bug in a hand-written gradient. Compare checking it with a nudge and checking it against torch autograd: what does each cost, and when does each mislead you?
    Check
    • a nudge needs one extra forward pass per input and is only approximate
    • a big h leaves the curvature term (14.3 at h = 0.1), and a tiny h is swamped by round-off (14.0012 at 10⁻¹²); use about 10⁻⁵, or the centred difference
    • autograd is exact and does every input in one backward pass, but it only helps if you compare against it (0.141302 vs the nudge's 0.141301)
    • neither catches a mistake in the forward formula itself
  4. tradeoff A teammate wants to move the makemore labs to device='mps' because a matmul was 2.5× faster there. What do you gain, what do you lose, and what do you recommend?
    Check
    • gain: 27.4 ms → 10.9 ms in one run, but only 14.4 → 8.5 ms (1.7×) in a quieter one
    • lose: the mps generator runs a different stream, so seeded draws, losses and samples stop matching the notebooks
    • the makemore nets are small: the longest makemore run is 301 s on the cpu
    • keep cpu for makemore, and use mps for the GPT lectures

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

The toolkit gave you seven local rules and the chain rule, but you applied them by hand. The small network at the end of Lecture 1 has 41 parameters. Commit before revealing: what must each number remember so that a program can find all 41 gradients for you, and how many passes does that take compared with nudging?

Reveal

Its parents, the operation that made it, and a closure holding its local slope. One forward pass then records the graph, and one backward sweep in reversed topological order gives every gradient exactly. Nudging would need 41 extra forward passes, each only approximate. That's micrograd's Value, and its whole engine is 94 lines. Start with the Value object.
