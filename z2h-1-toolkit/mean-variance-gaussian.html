Mean, variance and the Gaussian

Also called: the average (μ), the spread (σ², σ), the standard deviation (std); the normal distribution, N(0, 1), torch.randn. Lecture 4 · calculating the init scale: "Kaiming init" · 0:27:53

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A layer adds up many products of inputs and weights. With careless weights the numbers grow (or shrink) at every layer, so a deep network's activations blow up or fade out before training even starts, and nothing tells you why unless you can measure spread.

Where it fits: It builds on the dot product (one layer output is a sum of fan_in products) and on probability distributions. It is the arithmetic behind L4's Kaiming init and BatchNorm, and later behind attention's divide-by-√head_size.

In one breath: the mean says where a pile of numbers sits, and the variance (or its square root, the standard deviation) says how widely it spreads. torch.randn draws from the Gaussian bell curve with mean 0 and std 1. Add up n independent random numbers and their variances add, so the std grows like √n. That one fact tells you why a layer's weights get divided by √fan_in.

Predict first: x has shape (1000, 10) and w has shape (10, 200), both drawn with torch.randn, so both have std 1. What is the std of y = x @ w: about 1, about 10, or something else?

About 3.16, which is √10 (measured: 3.1613). Each output adds up 10 products, and each product has variance 1. Variances add, so the sum has variance 10 and std √10. Std 1 would need the weights divided by √10, and 10 would be the variance, not the std.

The Gaussian: what torch.randn draws from

A Gaussian (normal distribution) is the bell curve: values near the mean are common, and each extra std away gets rapidly rarer. N(0, 1) means mean 0, std 1, and that's what torch.randn samples. The lab draws 100,000 values with seed 2147483647 and gets mean −0.0037 and std 1.0014: close to 0 and 1, never exactly.

Shaded bands: within 1, 2 and 3 std of the mean. Exact areas computed from the Gaussian formula (68.27%, 95.45%, 99.73%); measured = the fraction of the lab's 100,000 randn draws inside each band.

Add random numbers, and their variances add

If two numbers are independent (knowing one tells you nothing about the other), the variance of their sum is the sum of their variances. So n unit-variance numbers add up to variance n, and std √n: the spread grows, but only like a square root, because positive and negative draws partly cancel.

So a layer's output grows by √fan_in

One output of y = x @ w is a dot product: 10 inputs times 10 weights, added up. The number of inputs feeding one output is called fan_in (10 here). Each product x·w of two independent unit-variance numbers has variance 1, so the output is exactly the sum from the last section.

Standardising: forcing mean 0, std 1

You can also skip the careful init and fix the numbers after the fact: subtract the mean and divide by the std. For v that gives (v − 5)/2 = [−1.5, −0.5, −0.5, −0.5, 0, 0, 1, 2], which has mean 0 and std 1 (/n) (computed). Done per column on the lab's y, it gives mean 0.0000 and std 1.0000. BatchNorm does exactly this to a layer's outputs over each batch, then lets the network learn its own scale and shift.

h = (y - y.mean(0, keepdim=True)) / y.std(0, keepdim=True) dim 0 = down the 1000 rows, so each of the 200 columns gets its own μ and σ

import torch
v = torch.tensor([2., 4., 4., 4., 5., 5., 7., 9.])
print(v.mean(), v.var(unbiased=False), v.var())   # /n and /(n-1)
g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(2147483647)
z = torch.randn(100_000, generator=g)
print(z.mean(), z.std(), (z.abs() < 1).float().mean())
for n in [1, 4, 10, 100]:
    print(n, torch.randn(100_000, n, generator=g).sum(1).std())
x = torch.randn(1000, 10, generator=g)
w = torch.randn(10, 200, generator=g)
y = x @ w
print(x.std(), y.std(), (x @ (w / 10**0.5)).std())
h = (y - y.mean(0, keepdim=True)) / y.std(0, keepdim=True)
print(h.mean(), h.std(0).mean())

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Leave weights as plain randn

Each layer multiplies the std by about √fan_in. Five fan_in-10 layers in a row take std 1 to about 295 (computed, seeded), and tanh units stuck at ±1 stop learning.

✗ Make every weight tiny

w * 0.01 with fan_in 10 multiplies the std by 0.0316 per layer, so five layers shrink it to about 3e-8 (computed): the signal fades to nothing.

✗ Hand-tune a constant

Karpathy's 0.2 worked for one layer, but the right number depends on fan_in, so it has to be re-found for every layer size. Nobody can do that for 50 layers.

✓ Divide by √fan_in

Var(w) = 1/fan_in cancels the fan_in in Var(y), so every layer passes on std about 1 whatever its size: 0.9997 here, and the same five-layer stack stays between 0.89 and 1.06 (computed).

Is it always exactly √fan_in?

No. The rule assumes the inputs and weights are independent with mean 0, and that nothing squashes the numbers between layers. tanh squeezes its outputs toward 0, so Kaiming init multiplies by a gain of 5/3 for tanh (√2 for ReLU): W1 gets (5/3)/√30 = 0.3043 (computed), next to the hand-tuned 0.2. That belongs to the Kaiming init card. And mean and std don't describe every pile of numbers: two very different histograms can share both, so Karpathy plots histograms, not just the std.

Modern networks lean less on perfect init. Normalisation layers (BatchNorm, LayerNorm) standardise as they go, and better optimisers like Adam are more forgiving. Standardising isn't free either: BatchNorm makes each example's output depend on the rest of its batch, and a batch of one has no variance at all (torch.tensor([3.]).std() is nan, computed).

Understand and remember

  • Independent random numbers → their variances add → a sum of n unit-variance numbers has variance n → its std is √n, not n, because positive and negative draws partly cancel
  • One output of x @ w sums fan_in products x·w → each has variance Var(x)·Var(w) → Var(y) = fan_in · Var(w) · Var(x) → with randn weights and fan_in 10 the std becomes √10 ≈ 3.16 (measured 3.1613)
  • Choose Var(w) = 1/fan_in (divide w by √fan_in) → the fan_in cancels → every layer passes on std ≈ 1 whatever its width → a deep stack neither explodes (≈295 after five raw layers) nor fades (w·0.01)
  • Subtract the mean and divide by the std → any column becomes mean 0, std 1 → BatchNorm does this per batch at every layer, so it fixes the scale even when the init is wrong
  • detail that matters torch.var/torch.std divide by n − 1 by default (Bessel's correction) → on 8 numbers that's 4.5714 instead of 4.0 → small batches give noticeably different statistics, and a batch of one gives nan

Reference only

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

The √n rule needs the numbers to be independent. Suppose instead you add the same randn number to itself 10 times (10·z). Commit: is the std of the result about 3.16, about 10, or about 1?

Reveal

About 10 (computed with seed 2147483647: 10.0136). The copies never cancel each other, so the std scales by n, not √n; Var(10·z) = 100·Var(z). Independence is what gives √n. That's why the init rule weakens when inputs are strongly correlated.
