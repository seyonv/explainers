Softmax and cross-entropy, first look

Also called: softmax, the normalised exponential; cross-entropy, negative log likelihood (NLL), log loss. Lecture 2 · transforming neural net outputs into probabilities: the softmax · 1:18:46

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A network's last layer outputs raw scores, logits, that can be negative and don't sum to anything in particular. You can't sample from them, and you can't say how wrong they were. Without a way to turn scores into probabilities and grade them with one number, there's nothing for gradient descent to minimise.

Where it fits: It's the last card of the toolkit and joins two earlier ones: exp and log from the exp-and-log card, and probability distributions from the sampling card. Every model in the course, from the L2 bigram to GPT-2, ends in exactly this softmax and this loss.

  2. Why does adding 100 to every logit leave the probabilities unchanged, while multiplying by 8 pushes the top class to 0.9997?
  3. Why does the cross-entropy of the three possible targets differ by exactly the gaps between their logits?
  4. What sets the two ends of the loss's range, 0 and 3.2958, and what happens as the correct class's probability goes to 0?
  5. Which of likelihood, log likelihood and average NLL do you maximise and which do you minimise, and why do they all pick the same model?

In one breath: a network's last layer outputs one raw score per possible answer, called a logit, which can be any real number. Softmax turns those scores into probabilities: exp makes each one positive, then dividing by the total makes them sum to 1. Cross-entropy grades that prediction with a single number, −log of the probability given to the correct answer: 0 when the model was certain and right, and bigger the more surprised it was.

Predict first: logits [2.0, 1.0, 0.1] give class 0 a probability of 0.659. What does class 0 get if you (a) multiply every logit by 8, or (b) add 100 to every logit?

(a) 0.9997. (b) Still 0.659. Multiplying stretches the gaps between logits (1.0 becomes 8.0), and exp turns a gap of 8 into a ratio of e8 ≈ 2981, so one class takes almost everything. Adding a constant multiplies every exp by the same e100, which cancels when you divide by the total. Softmax only cares about the gaps between logits (all numbers from the lab).

Scale stretches it, shift does nothing

The ratio of two probabilities is e raised to the gap between their logits: p0 / p1 = 0.659 / 0.2424 ≈ e1 = 2.718 (computed). So multiplying every logit stretches every gap and makes the distribution peakier, while adding a constant leaves the gaps, and the answer, unchanged.

From the lab. Dividing logits by a number T before softmax is called temperature: T = 2 is the ×0.5 row (flatter), T = 1/8 the ×8 row (nearly one-hot). You'll meet the ×8 effect again in self-attention, which divides its scores by √head_size so softmax doesn't turn peaky: Karpathy's example peak of 0.2872 becomes 0.8000 when the same scores are ×8 (recomputed).

Because only gaps matter, you can subtract the largest logit first. That keeps every exponent ≤ 0, so exp can't overflow (the exp and log card has the details). F.softmax and F.cross_entropy do this for you.

From the lab; log 11.2125 = 2.4170 computed. The three losses differ by exactly the logit gaps (1.0 and 0.9), so the loss only needs the correct logit and the log of the total. That is why F.cross_entropy takes the raw logits, not probabilities. The loss runs from 0 upward with no ceiling: as py → 0, −log py → ∞.

import math, torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
logits = torch.tensor([2.0, 1.0, 0.1])
counts = logits.exp()                  # log-counts -> counts
probs = counts / counts.sum()          # softmax
print(counts.tolist(), probs.tolist())
print(F.softmax(logits * 8.0, 0).tolist())
print(F.softmax(logits + 100, 0).tolist())
print(-probs[0].log().item())          # cross-entropy, target 0
y = torch.tensor([0])
print(F.cross_entropy(logits[None], y).item())   # raw logits in
print(-math.log(1/27))
ps = torch.tensor([0.4, 0.1, 0.25])
print(ps.prod().item(), ps.log().sum().item(), -ps.log().mean().item())

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Divide by the sum

Logits [2, 1, −1] become [1.0, 0.5, −0.5] (computed): a negative "probability". Logits that sum to 0 divide by zero.

✗ Square, then divide

Always positive, but [2, 1, −2] gives [0.444, 0.111, 0.444] (computed): a score of −2 looks as likely as +2, so the network can't vote against a class.

✗ Just take the biggest (argmax)

[1, 0, 0] says which, not how sure. Nudging a logit changes nothing until the order flips, so there's no gradient, and a wrong pick scores −log 0 = ∞.

✓ Softmax + cross-entropy

Positive, sums to 1, keeps order and sign ([2, 1, −2] → [0.7214, 0.2654, 0.0132], computed), smooth everywhere, and the loss log∑ez − zy is always finite.

Is softmax's output the model's confidence?

Only loosely. The same ranking gave 0.659 or 0.9997 depending on the scale of the logits, and training sets that scale, so a probability near 1 means "the logits are far apart", not "this is surely right". And don't softmax before F.cross_entropy: it applies softmax itself, so with our logits and target 0 it silently returns 0.8021 instead of 0.4170 (computed) and trains on the wrong number.

Softmax + cross-entropy is for picking exactly one answer out of N, like the next character. It's the wrong tool when the output is a number (regression uses squared error; Karpathy notes at 1:35:49 that classification uses NLL instead), or when several labels can be true at once. This card is a first look: z2h-3 builds it for 27 characters and z2h-4 switches to F.cross_entropy.

Understand and remember

  • exp makes every logit positive and keeps their order → dividing by the total makes them sum to 1 → any real-valued scores become a valid probability distribution you can sample from and differentiate
  • pi / pj = ezi − zj → only the gaps between logits matter → adding a constant changes nothing (so subtracting the max is free and stops overflow) → multiplying stretches the gaps and sharpens toward one-hot (×8 takes 0.659 to 0.9997)
  • loss = −log py → 0 when the model is certain and right, 3.2958 when it's uniform over 27, unbounded as py → 0 → rewrites as log∑ez − zy → the loss needs only the correct logit and the log of the total, which is why F.cross_entropy takes raw logits
  • log only goes up when its input goes up → maximising the likelihood, maximising the log likelihood and minimising the average NLL pick the same model → the average NLL is the loss the course minimises (1.5351 for p = 0.4, 0.1, 0.25)
  • detail that matters F.cross_entropy applies softmax itself → feeding it probabilities softmaxes twice → the loss for target 0 silently becomes 0.8021 instead of 0.4170 and training follows the wrong number

Reference only

  • logits [2.0, 1.0, 0.1] → exp [7.3891, 2.7183, 1.1052], total 11.2125 → softmax [0.659, 0.2424, 0.0986]
  • cross-entropy for targets 0, 1, 2: 0.4170, 1.4170, 2.3170; log 11.2125 = 2.4170
  • F.softmax(x, dim), F.cross_entropy(logits, targets) with logits shaped (N, C) and integer targets shaped (N,)

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain why the loss is built from −log p of the correct class rather than from 1 − p or from p itself.
    Check
    • over a dataset the probabilities multiply; logs turn that product into a sum that doesn't underflow
    • −log p is 0 for a certain, correct answer and grows without limit as p → 0, so confident mistakes cost a lot
    • the average of −log p is the average NLL, which ranks models like the likelihood does
    • with softmax it simplifies to log∑ez − zy, which is finite and has a gradient on every logit
  2. mechanism Walk through softmax and cross-entropy for logits [2.0, 1.0, 0.1] with target 1, giving the intermediate numbers.
    Check
    • exp gives [7.3891, 2.7183, 1.1052], total 11.2125
    • divide: [0.659, 0.2424, 0.0986], which sums to 1
    • pick p1 = 0.2424; −log 0.2424 = 1.4170
    • check: log 11.2125 − z1 = 2.4170 − 1.0 = 1.4170
  3. mechanism Walk through what softmax does to logits [1000, 999, 998], first computed naively in float32, then with the max subtracted.
    Check
    • exp(1000) overflows to inf, so inf / inf = nan for every class
    • subtract 1000: [0, −1, −2], every exponent ≤ 0
    • exp and divide: [0.6652, 0.2447, 0.09]
    • unchanged answer, because only gaps matter
  4. tradeoff You can make a trained model's top probability bigger just by multiplying its logits by a constant. What does that buy you, and what does it cost?
    Check
    • same ranking and same argmax, so accuracy doesn't change
    • the distribution gets peakier (×8: 0.659 → 0.9997), so the numbers look more confident without being more right
    • when the top class is wrong the loss explodes: target 2 at ×8 costs 15.2003 vs 2.3170
    • for sampling it lowers diversity (temperature below 1); for attention at init it's why scores are divided by √head_size

vs exp and log: turning products into sums: That card treats exp and log as separate tools; this card chains them. Where does each one appear in softmax + cross-entropy, and which numeric failure does each bring with it?

Check
  • exp appears in softmax, turning logits into positive counts
  • log appears in the loss, as −log py
  • exp can overflow (exp(89) is inf in float32) → subtract the max
  • log can hit log 0 = −inf → compute the loss from logits as log∑ez − zy, as F.cross_entropy does

vs Probability distributions and sampling: The sampling card starts from counts and normalises them; this card starts from logits. What is fundamentally different about where the numbers come from, and why does the course switch from one to the other?

Check
  • counts are already ≥ 0, so normalising is just dividing; logits can be any sign, so they need exp first
  • Karpathy reads logits as log-counts: exp(logits) plays the role of the count table N
  • counts come from a table that explodes with more context; logits come from a network that scales
  • both end in the same kind of distribution you sample with torch.multinomial

vs Why divide by √head_size: Attention runs softmax over scores between tokens. Using this card's ×8 row, what goes wrong if those scores grow with head_size, and which design choice fixes it?

Check
  • large-magnitude scores make softmax peaky, close to one-hot (×8 here: 0.9997)
  • each token would then take information from a single other token at initialisation
  • dividing the scores by √head_size keeps their variance near 1, so softmax stays diffuse
  • same mechanism as temperature: only the gaps, scaled, decide the peakiness

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

At ×8 the logits [16, 8, 0.8] give class 0 a probability of 0.9997. Now suppose the correct answer was actually class 2. Commit to a rough cross-entropy for that example, compared with 2.3170 at the original scale.

Reveal

About 15.2003 (computed). The loss is log∑ez − zy; at ×8 the log of the total is almost exactly the largest logit, 16.0003, and z2 = 0.8, so the loss is about the gap 16 − 0.8 = 15.2. Scaling the logits multiplies the cost of a confident mistake by roughly the same factor: sharper predictions pay far more when they're wrong.
