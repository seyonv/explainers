Vectors and the dot product

Also called: inner product, scalar product; in a neuron, "the weighted sum". Lecture 1 · manual backpropagation example #2: a neuron · 0:52:52

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A neuron has to turn many inputs into one number it can squash and pass on, and it needs a knob per input to decide how much each one counts. Without a rule for combining a list of inputs with a list of weights, there is no neuron, no layer and no attention.

Where it fits: The calculus cards gave you slopes of single numbers. This card moves to lists of numbers: the dot product is the first operation in every neuron of lecture 1, and the next card stacks many of them into a matrix multiply.

Builds on: The chain rule · The Python you need for micrograd

You'll meet: a vector as an ordered list the dot product as a weighted sum length and the angle between vectors cosine similarity

By the end: Compute the lecture 1 neuron's x·w + b by hand and in torch, say what the sign and size of a dot product tell you, and explain why a weight whose input is 0 gets no gradient.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  1. In the neuron x = [2, 0], w = [−3, 1], which weight can you change freely without moving the output, and why?
  2. Why does a neuron sum the pairwise products instead of keeping the list [−6, 0]?
  3. What does a negative dot product say about the two arrows, and how does x·w = −6 fit that picture?
  4. u = [1, 0] scores 2.00 against [2, 0] but 1.00 against itself. What does that reveal about what the dot product measures?
  5. If both vectors had 64 entries of random noise instead of 2, what would happen to the typical size of their dot product?

In one breath: a vector is an ordered list of numbers, like x = [2, 0]. The dot product of two vectors of the same length multiplies them position by position and adds the results, turning two lists into one number. Every neuron in this course starts with one: its inputs dotted with its weights.

Predict first: the lecture's neuron has inputs x = [2, 0] and weights w = [−3, 1]. If you change the second weight w2 from 1 to 100, what happens to x·w?

Nothing: it stays −6. x·w = 2·(−3) + 0·w2, and 0 times anything is 0. A weight only matters in proportion to the input it multiplies. That's why the lecture later finds w2.grad = 0: wiggling w2 can't move the output while x2 = 0.

0.7071 is 1/√2. The bias b is one extra number added after the dot product; it shifts how easily the neuron fires (Karpathy's "trigger happiness").

What the number means: alignment

Picture each vector as an arrow from the origin. Its length is |x| = √(x1² + x2²). The dot product also equals |x| · |w| · cos θ, where θ (theta) is the angle between the arrows. So it's big and positive when they point the same way, zero when they're at right angles, and negative when they point apart. Cosine similarity, cos θ = x·w / (|x| |w|), is the dot product with both lengths divided out.

the neuron's pair: |x| = √(2² + 0²) = 2.0000 |w| = √((−3)² + 1²) = √10 = 3.1623 cos θ = −6 / (2.0000 × 3.1623) = −0.9487 → θ ≈ 161.6° (computed: acos)

Measured by the lab. x and w point almost opposite ways, so x·w is strongly negative. The first row shows that the dot product grows with length (2.00) while cosine doesn't (1.00).

▶ Build itruns on your M3 · about 1 s

When: pause the video at 0:52:52, when Karpathy draws the neuron, and type this before he builds it out of Values.

import math, torch
x = [2.0, 0.0]                  # inputs x1, x2
w = [-3.0, 1.0]                 # weights w1, w2
b = 6.8813735870195432          # bias, chosen so o = 1/sqrt(2)
dot = sum(xi*wi for xi, wi in zip(x, w))   # multiply pairs, add
print('x.w = 2*(-3) + 0*1 =', dot)
n = dot + b
print(f'n = x.w + b = {n:.4f}  (full: {n!r})')
print(f'o = tanh(n) = {math.tanh(n):.4f}')
xt, wt = torch.tensor(x), torch.tensor(w)
d = torch.dot(xt, wt).item()
print('torch.dot(x, w) =', d, ' x @ w =', (xt @ wt).item())
cos = (xt @ wt / (xt.norm() * wt.norm())).item()
print(f'x vs w: |x|={xt.norm():.4f} |w|={wt.norm():.4f} cos={cos:.4f}')

zip(x, w) walks the two lists in step, which is the "position by position" part. In torch, @ on two 1-d tensors is the same dot product; on 2-d tensors it becomes a matrix multiply, which is many dot products at once.

How it differs from related ideas

✗ Elementwise product

x * w in torch gives [−6, 0]: still a list. A neuron needs one score to push through tanh, so the dot product also sums.

✗ Plain sum of inputs

2 + 0 = 2, whatever the weights are. With nothing to tune, there's nothing for training to learn.

✗ Cosine similarity

−0.9487 here: direction only, lengths divided out. Good for comparing two embeddings, but a neuron could never respond more strongly by growing a weight.

✓ Dot product

One number that mixes direction and size, and each weight's effect is exactly its input: d(x·w)/dw1 = x1 = 2, so w1.grad is 2 × the upstream gradient 0.5 = 1.0 in the lecture.

Is a bigger dot product always "more similar"?

No. The dot product grows with length as well as alignment: [2, 0] scores 2.00 against [1, 0] while an identical copy [1, 0] scores only 1.00 (u·u = 1). When only direction should count, divide out the lengths and use cosine similarity instead.

Length also grows with the number of entries. The sum of n random unit-variance numbers has a spread (standard deviation) of about √n (the mean-variance card measures 9.960 for n = 100), so dot products of long random vectors get large. That's why attention divides its dot products by √head_size (z2h-8). And one dot product per output is slow to write out by hand; stacking many into a matrix multiply is the next card.

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • Each input is multiplied by its own weight → the products are added into one number → a single score the neuron can shift with b and squash with tanh (−6.0 + 6.8814 = 0.8814 → 0.7071)
  • x·w = x1·w1 + x2·w2 → the slope of x·w with respect to w_i is just x_i → an input of 0 makes its weight irrelevant (x2 = 0 → w2.grad = 0) and a large input makes its weight matter a lot
  • x·w = |x| |w| cos θ → the sign says whether the arrows point together or apart and the size also grows with their lengths → dot product ≠ pure similarity; divide out the lengths (cosine) when only direction should count
  • Longer vectors add more product terms → for random unit-variance entries the sum's spread grows like √k → big dot products that saturate softmax or tanh → attention divides by √head_size
  • detail that matters A matrix multiply is a grid of dot products (row of one · column of the other) → the (n,k)@(k,m) shape rule is just "the two vectors in each dot product must have the same length k"

Reference only

  • torch.dot(a, b) for 1-d tensors; a @ b gives the same number for 1-d tensors
  • length |x| = √(Σ x_i²), torch x.norm()
  • the lab's numbers: |x| = 2.0000, |w| = 3.1623, cos θ = −0.9487 (θ ≈ 161.6°)
  • b = 6.8813735870195432 was chosen so that o = 1/√2 = 0.7071

vs Matrix multiply by shapes: One dot product vs a matrix multiply: what is the same, what does the matmul add, and what error do you get when the shapes are wrong in each?

Check
  • a matmul is many dot products: every row of the left with every column of the right
  • the inner lengths must match in both cases (k in (n,k)@(k,m))
  • the matmul computes a whole layer, or a whole batch, in one call
  • mismatched lengths: zip silently stops at the shorter list, while torch raises a shape error

vs Self-attention: one head (Q, K, V): A neuron dots its input with fixed learned weights; attention dots a query with the keys of other tokens. What is fundamentally different about the second vector, and why does attention need to scale the result?

Check
  • neuron: w is a parameter, the same for every input
  • attention: both vectors are computed from the data, so the scores change per token pair
  • the scores feed softmax, and with head_size entries their spread grows like √head_size
  • without dividing by √head_size, softmax saturates towards one token

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Double every weight in the lecture neuron: w = [−6, 2], with x and b unchanged. Before computing: what happens to x·w, to cos θ between x and w, and to the output o?

Reveal

x·w doubles to −12, but cos θ stays −0.9487, because scaling a vector changes its length, not its direction. n = −12 + 6.8814 = −5.1186 and o = tanh(n) = −0.99993 (computed): the neuron flips from 0.7071 to almost fully "off", and it's saturated. The dot product carries size, so weight scale alone can push a neuron into tanh's flat region.
