The six big ideas of the toolkit

The one-page version of the math and code toolkit: six claims, six sketches to redraw. Supplemented prerequisites, no lecture of its own; each quote links to the lecture where Karpathy leans on the idea.

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: The toolkit is twelve separate cards: derivatives, logs, shapes, broadcasting, variance, softmax. Studied one at a time, they stay a pile of facts, and the lectures then use three of them in two lines of code (logits = h @ W2 + b2, loss = F.cross_entropy(logits, Y)) without stopping to say which is which.

Where it fits: This card spans the whole toolkit, the course before micrograd. It compresses the math and tensor cards into six claims, each tied to the lecture moment where Karpathy leans on it, so every later course can point back here.

Big idea 1: Slopes multiply along a chain and add across paths

Each step only needs its own small slope. Walk from the output back to the input and multiply them; where a value reaches the output by two routes, add the two products.

“just a recursive application of chain rule backwards through the computation graph” · Karpathy, L1 0:32:10

Why it matters: That sentence is all of backprop. micrograd writes each op's slope beside its forward code and accumulates with += so the two paths add, which is the += bug fixed.

Big idea 2: Logs turn a vanishing product into an ordinary sum

The probability of a word is a product of small numbers, and a computer rounds long products to 0. Take logs and the product becomes a sum of modest negative numbers, and the best model stays the best.

“equivalent to maximizing the log likelihood (because log is monotonic)” · Karpathy, L2 0:50:14

Why it matters: Every loss in the series is an average of −log p, and 3.2958 is the number to expect from a model that knows nothing about 27 characters, which is how you check the loss at step 0.

Big idea 3: Read the shapes: a whole batch through a layer is one matmul

A neuron is a dot product. Put the examples in the rows of X and the neurons in the columns of W, and one matmul computes every neuron on every example. The shapes tell you what came out before the numbers do.

“the dumbest, smallest, simplest neural net” · Karpathy, L2 1:13:53

Why it matters: That net is one xenc @ W. The MLP, WaveNet and GPT are the same move with more dims: (4, 5, 80) @ (80, 200) is still one call, and a shape error means two inner sizes that don't match.

Big idea 4: Broadcasting stretches from the right, and a shape that fits can still be wrong

PyTorch lines two shapes up on the right and stretches any size-1 or missing dim. An impossible pair errors, but a pair that fits the wrong way runs silently and gives wrong numbers.

“Treat this with respect.” · Karpathy, L2 0:36:17

Why it matters: Keep keepdim=True on any sum you divide by, and print .shape before you trust a result. WaveNet's BatchNorm bug is this same silent fit, and backprop sums a stretched value's gradient back (sum–broadcast duality).

Big idea 5: Random sums grow like √n, so weights are divided by √fan_in

Add n independent unit-variance numbers and their variances add, so the std grows like √n. A layer output sums fan_in products, so plain randn weights inflate the signal by √fan_in at every layer.

“normalize my weights by the square root of the fan_in” · Karpathy, L4 0:27:53

Why it matters: This is Kaiming init (with a gain for tanh), the reason BatchNorm standardises to mean 0, std 1, and why attention divides its scores by √head_size.

Big idea 6: Softmax turns any scores into a distribution, and the loss is −log of the right answer

exp makes every logit positive, dividing by the total makes them sum to 1, and cross-entropy scores the result as −log of the probability given to the correct class. Only the gaps between logits matter.

“the last 2 lines here are together called a 'softmax'” · Karpathy, L2 1:18:46

Why it matters: The bigram net, the MLP, WaveNet and GPT all end in these two lines plus this loss. To generate, you sample from p with torch.multinomial; to train, you push −log p[y] down.

1 · Core model what to internalise, what to just look up

Keep it if it changes what you can explain, predict, compare, choose, build or debug. Otherwise it's reference.

Understand and remember

  • Each op's slope is known locally → along a chain the slopes multiply (0.0707 × 2 = 0.1413) → where a value reaches the output by several paths the products add → one backward walk with += gives every gradient (backprop)
  • Probabilities ≤ 1 multiply toward 0 → float32 rounds a long product to exactly 0.0 → log turns the product into a sum and keeps the ranking → every loss in the series is an average −log p, 3.2958 for a uniform guess over 27
  • A layer is many dot products sharing an input → rows of X are examples, columns of W are neurons → (n, k) @ (k, m) → (n, m) does them all in one call → read shapes first; broadcasting then stretches size-1 dims from the right, so a shape that fits the wrong way (a (3,) of row sums) runs silently
  • Variances of independent numbers add → a sum of fan_in products has std √fan_in (3.1613 at fan_in 10) → stacked layers explode or fade → divide w by √fan_in so each layer passes on std ≈ 1 → logits stay small, softmax stays soft and the first loss is near 3.2958
  • detail that matters Softmax depends only on logit gaps → subtracting the max changes nothing but keeps every exponent ≤ 0 → no overflow; and the loss rewrites as log∑ez − zy, which is why F.cross_entropy takes raw logits rather than probabilities

Why this step?

3 · Retrieve from memory, card closed
  1. concept Explain, without code, why a neural net's loss is built from log-probabilities rather than from the probability of the whole dataset.
    Check
    • the probability of the data is a product of many numbers ≤ 1, which underflows to 0.0 (1000 × 1/27 in float32)
    • log turns it into a sum of modest numbers (−3295.84)
    • log only goes up when its input does, so the best model is the same
    • negate and average to get a loss that is ≥ 0 and smaller-is-better
  2. mechanism Walk the shapes of C[X].view(-1, 6) @ W1 + b1 for X of shape (32, 3), C (27, 2), W1 (6, 100) and b1 (100,), and say what each step does to memory.
    Check
    • C[X]: one row of C per index → (32, 3) + (2,) = (32, 3, 2), a new copy
    • .view(-1, 6): new shape over the same storage → (32, 6), nothing copied
    • @ W1: (32, 6) @ (6, 100) → (32, 100), the 6s summed away
    • + b1: (100,) padded to (1, 100) and stretched down the 32 rows with stride 0
  3. tradeoff Your 27-way classifier's first-layer weights are plain randn with fan_in 10. Give two ways to keep the signal at std ≈ 1, and what each one costs.
    Check
    • divide w by √fan_in (Kaiming-style): free at run time, but assumes independent mean-0 inputs and needs a gain for tanh
    • standardise the outputs (x − mean)/std, as BatchNorm does: works even when the init is wrong
    • but it couples each example to its batch, and a batch of one has no variance (std is nan)
  4. tradeoff A teammate writes P = N / N.sum(1) on the 27×27 bigram counts, sees no error, and moves on. What went wrong, how would you catch it in one line, and why didn't PyTorch complain?
    Check
    • N.sum(1) has shape (27,), which broadcasting pads on the left to (1, 27), a row
    • so column j is divided by row j's sum; row 0 sums to 7.02, not 1
    • a square table fits both readings, so no error is possible
    • catch it with P[0].sum() or by printing the shape; fix with keepdim=True

4 · Compare against what you already know

vs The six big ideas of micrograd: micrograd's big ideas build backprop on scalars; the toolkit's add tensors, logs and softmax. Which toolkit idea is what lets micrograd's engine train a real language model, and where would micrograd itself fail first?

Check
  • the chain rule is the same in both: local slope × upstream gradient, added across paths
  • one matmul per layer replaces a Python object per number, so the same math runs on 11,897 parameters and beyond
  • micrograd has no softmax or log-loss yet: it trains with squared error on 4 examples
  • it fails first on speed and memory, not on math

vs The chain rule: The chain-rule card works on one scalar x. What changes when x is a whole tensor that was broadcast or matrix-multiplied, and which toolkit idea tells you how its gradient must be combined?

Check
  • a broadcast value is used many times, one per stretched copy
  • so, as with any value used twice, its gradient contributions add: summed back along the stretched dim
  • the matmul's slope is itself a matrix, so the product of slopes becomes a matmul (z2h-6)

vs Softmax and cross-entropy, first look: Sampling from p and computing −log p[y] both start from the same softmax. What does each one need from the distribution, and how does each fail when the logits are scaled up by 8?

Check
  • sampling needs the whole p and draws in proportion to it
  • the loss needs only p[y] (equivalently the correct logit and log∑ez)
  • ×8 makes p nearly one-hot: sampling loses variety, always picking the top class
  • and the loss becomes huge whenever the top class is wrong: confidently wrong

Why this step?

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

An untrained 27-way classifier's logits come straight out of an unscaled fan_in-10 layer, so they have std ≈ 3.16 instead of 0. Commit first: is its average loss at step 0 near 3.2958, a bit above it, or about twice as big?

Reveal

About twice as big: 6.96. Over 100,000 random rows of 27 logits with random targets (seed 2147483647), std 0 gives 3.2958, std 1 gives 3.76, std 3.16 gives 6.96 and std 10 gives 20.18 (computed). Large random logits make softmax confidently pick a random class, so −log p[y] is large when that class is wrong, which is most of the time. That is the problem the BatchNorm lecture fixes first.

6 · Apply use the model, don't restate it

Use five of the six ideas on one example. Logits [2.0, 1.0, 0.1], correct class y = 0. By hand: softmax, then the loss −log p[0]. Then predict each logit's gradient by the chain rule (∂loss/∂zi = pi − 1 for the correct class, pi otherwise). Check the gradients two ways: nudge each logit by h = 0.001 in float64 and recompute F.cross_entropy, then call loss.backward() in torch.

Done when: p = [0.659, 0.2424, 0.0986] and loss 0.4170; the chain-rule gradients are [−0.3410, 0.2424, 0.0986]; the forward nudge gives about [−0.34089, 0.24252, 0.09861]; and torch's z.grad prints [−0.3410, 0.2424, 0.0986]. The three gradients sum to 0, because shifting every logit by the same amount changes nothing.

Hint

Write the loss as log∑ez − z0. The derivative of log∑ez with respect to zi is ezi/∑ez = pi; the −z0 adds −1 to the correct class only.
