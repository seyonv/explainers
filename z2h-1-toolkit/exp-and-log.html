exp and log: turning products into sums

Also called: the exponential ex and the natural logarithm ln x; in code exp / log; summing log-probabilities, "working in log space". Lecture 2 · loss function (the negative log likelihood of the data under our model) · 0:50:14

Prime · 2 minutes before you read

The problem: A language model scores a word by multiplying many probabilities, each below 1. The true product shrinks so fast that float32 rounds it to exactly 0, and then two models can no longer be compared, let alone trained.

Where it fits: It comes after the calculus cards and before probability and softmax. The loss of every model in the course is a sum of log probabilities, and every softmax runs exp on the logits, so this card is the arithmetic under both.

Used in the field guides: Project 2: A character-level language model: noise at step 0, plausible names at the end (Models Field Guide) · Principle 2: The loss at step 0 should be ln(V). (Models Field Guide)

You'll meet: exp and log as inverses log(a·b) = log a + log b −log(1/27) = 3.2958 float32 underflow and overflow subtract the max before exp

By the end: Turn a product of probabilities into a sum of logs and back, explain why the loss is written as a negative log-sum, and spot when exp or a product will silently break in float32.

Read with these questions (answer them in your head as you go; don't take notes line by line):

  2. Why is the log of every probability zero or negative, and what does the size of −log p tell you about the model?
  3. At which factor does a float32 product of 1/27s become exactly 0, and why does the log-sum never have that problem?
  4. Why does exp fail on logits like 100–102, and why does subtracting the largest logit leave the softmax answer unchanged?
  5. Which failure does working in log space not fix, and what does the course do about it instead?

In one breath: exp(x) is e ≈ 2.71828 raised to the power x, and log undoes it: log(exp(x)) = x. The log turns multiplying into adding, so the probability of a whole word, a long product of small numbers that a computer rounds to 0, becomes a sum of ordinary negative numbers that it handles easily.

Predict first: each factor is 1/27 (a uniform guess over 27 characters). Multiply 1000 of them in float32, PyTorch's default number type. What does the computer print? And what is the sum of their logs?

The product prints exactly 0.0; the sum of logs is −3295.84. The true product is about 10−1431, far below the smallest number float32 can hold, so it rounds to zero (at the 32nd factor already, computed). Each log is just −3.2958, and 1000 of them add to a perfectly ordinary number.

Probabilities live in (0, 1], and the log of anything in that range is ≤ 0: log 1 = 0 (certain), and the smaller p is, the more negative its log. So the loss uses −log p, which is 0 for a sure thing and grows as the model gets more surprised.

float32's smallest normal number is 1.175e-38; below it the value keeps fewer and fewer digits, and past about 1.4e-45 it is 0 (computed with cumprod; the 1000 row is the lab). Once the product is 0 you can't compare models, and its log is −inf.

exp breaks the other way: overflow

float32's largest number is about 3.4e38, and log of that is 88.72 (computed), so exp(88) = 1.6516e+38 still fits but exp(89) is inf. Softmax (turning raw scores called logits into probabilities by exp-then-normalise) hits this with large logits. The fix: subtract the largest logit first. That multiplies top and bottom by the same exp(−m), so the answer is unchanged.

logits z [100, 101, 102] m = max = 102 naive exp(z) [inf, inf, inf] → inf/inf = [nan, nan, nan] z − m [ -2, -1, 0] exp(z − m) [0.1353, 0.3679, 1.0] sum 1.5032 ÷ sum [0.09, 0.2447, 0.6652] (correct)

Why it beats the alternatives

✗ Multiply the probabilities

Exact on paper, but in float32 a run of 1/27 factors hits 0.0 at the 32nd one (computed). The L2 training set has 228,146 bigrams.

✗ Switch to float64

Moves the cliff, doesn't remove it: the float64 product of 1/27 factors is 0.0 by factor 227 (computed), and every number costs twice the memory.

✗ Rescale as you go

Multiply by a big constant whenever the product gets small and count how often you did. It works, but that count is just the exponent: you've rebuilt the log by hand.

✓ Add log-probabilities

Each term is a modest number (−0.94 to −3.68 for "emma"), 1000 terms sum to −3295.84 without trouble, and the best model by product is still the best by sum.

Does log fix everything?

No. log 0 is −inf (torch.tensor(0.).log() gives -inf), so one pair the model thinks is impossible makes the whole sum −inf and the loss infinite. Karpathy hits this with "andrejq"; the cure is smoothing the counts, not a better log. And the log only splits products: the log of a sum, like softmax's denominator, has no such rule, which is why softmax needs the subtract-the-max trick instead.

Stay in log space for accumulating and comparing; come back with exp when you need real probabilities, for example to sample the next character. The base doesn't matter for choosing a model: log10(1/27) = −1.4314 and log2(1/27) = −4.7549 (computed) are the same ranking on a different scale, but PyTorch losses use base e, so 3.2958 is the number to remember.

Understand and remember

  • log(a·b) = log a + log b → the log of a word's probability is the sum of its per-pair log-probs → each term stays a modest number (−0.94 to −3.68 for “emma”) → a million-term likelihood becomes an ordinary sum the computer can hold
  • Probabilities are ≤ 1 → their product shrinks geometrically (1/27 per factor) → float32 hits exactly 0.0 at the 32nd factor → products can't compare or train models, so the loss is built from the log-sum instead
  • log only goes up when its input goes up → the model with the biggest product also has the biggest log-sum → maximising likelihood = maximising log-likelihood = minimising the average negative log-likelihood (2.5138 for “emma”, 3.2958 for a uniform guess)
  • exp grows so fast that exp(89) is inf in float32 → a softmax over large logits gives inf/inf = nan → subtracting the max multiplies top and bottom by the same exp(−m) → same probabilities with every exponent ≤ 0
  • detail that matters log 0 = −inf → a single pair with zero count makes the whole log-likelihood −inf and the loss infinite (“andrejq”) → the cure is smoothing the counts, not a different log

Reference only

  • e ≈ 2.71828; math.log and torch.log are the natural log (base e)
  • log(1/27) = −3.2958; log 0.5 = −0.6931; log 0.1 = −2.3026
  • float32 limits: largest ≈ 3.4e38 (log = 88.72), smallest normal 1.175e-38
  • “emma” under the L2 bigram model: product 3.4782e-06, log-sum −12.5690
  • torch.full((1000,), 1/27), .prod(), .log().sum(), .exp()

5 · Predict commit to an answer before revealing

Softmax's denominator is a sum of exps, and log only splits products. For logits [100, 101, 102], what is log(exp(100) + exp(101) + exp(102))? Commit to a rough number and to whether float32 can compute it directly.

Reveal

About 102.4076 (computed). Computed directly, the exps overflow and the result is inf. Instead, pull the max out: log Σ exp(z) = m + log Σ exp(z − m) = 102 + log(1.5032) = 102.4076, which is what torch.logsumexp returns. It's always a bit more than the largest logit, because the largest term dominates the sum.
